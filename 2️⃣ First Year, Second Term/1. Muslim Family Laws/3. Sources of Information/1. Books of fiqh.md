### 3.1 Books of Fiqh ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This topic is foundational. To research or apply Muslim law, one must understand the vessels in which the law is contained. Distinguishing between primary texts (_Matn_), commentaries (_Sharh_), and digests is essential for legal practice.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** In the context of Bangladeshi legal education, questions often arise regarding authoritative texts (like the _Hedaya_ or _Fatawa-e-Alamgiri_) and their role during the British colonial period. It is a frequent topic in short notes or part of broader essay questions on sources.
    

---

#### Introduction: The Repository of Legal Knowledge

While the Quran and Sunnah are the _divine sources_ of law, **Books of Fiqh** are the _human repositories_ where legal rulings have been derived, debated, systematized, and recorded over centuries. In the context of "Sources of Information," we are not looking at _where_ the law comes from (God/Prophet), but _where_ a lawyer or judge looks to find that law today.

**Fiqh** (Jurisprudence) refers to the human understanding of the Sharia. The books of Fiqh represent the massive corpus of legal literature produced by jurists (_Faqihs_) covering rituals, family law, contracts, and criminal justice.

#### Classification of Fiqh Literature

Academic study categorizes Fiqh literature based on function and structure. A student of Muslim law must distinguish between these types to determine the weight of authority a book holds.

**1. Usul al-Fiqh (Principles of Jurisprudence)** These books do not list laws (like "divorce is valid if..."); rather, they explain the _methodology_ of deriving laws. They discuss how to interpret the Quran, the rules of analogy (_Qiyas_), and the consensus (_Ijma_).

- _Example:_ _Usul al-Shashi_, _Al-Mustasfa_ by Al-Ghazali.
    

**2. Furu al-Fiqh (Substantive Law)** These are the actual codebooks containing rules and regulations governing daily life (marriage, inheritance, property). When a lawyer needs to know the specific ruling on a divorce case, they consult _Furu_ works.

- _Structure:_ Usually organized by "Books" (_Kitab_), e.g., _Kitab al-Nikah_ (Book of Marriage).
    

**3. Fatawa Collections (Verdicts)** These are compilations of legal opinions issued by muftis in response to specific questions posed by the public or the courts. They represent the practical application of law (`Case Law` equivalent in Islamic tradition).

- _Example:_ _Fatawa-e-Alamgiri_ (The most authoritative in South Asia).
    

#### The Hierarchy of Texts: Matn, Sharh, and Hashiyah

To understand Muslim law books, one must understand the unique literary layering used in Islamic scholarship.

- **Matn (The Text):** The core text, usually very concise and condensed, written to be memorized. It contains the absolute ruling without much argument. _Matn_ texts generally hold the highest authority for determining the "accepted view" (_Zahir al-Riwaya_) of a school.
    
- **Sharh (The Commentary):** A detailed explanation of the _Matn_. The commentator unpacks the concise sentences, provides evidence from the Quran/Sunnah, and discusses differing opinions.
    
- **Hashiyah (The Gloss/Margin Notes):** Notes written on the margins of the _Sharh_ or _Matn_ to clarify difficult words or specific points.
    

#### Key Books of Fiqh (Hanafi School)

Since Bangladesh predominantly follows the **Hanafi School** of Sunni law, the courts and syllabus focus heavily on Hanafi texts.

**1. Al-Hidayah (The Guide)**

- **Author:** Burhan al-Din al-Marghinani (d. 1197 AD).
    
- **Significance:** It is considered the most celebrated textbook of the Hanafi school. It combines both _Matn_ and commentary. It was the standard text used by British judges in India to understand Muslim law.
    
- **Translation:** Translated into English by Charles Hamilton (commissioned by Warren Hastings) in the late 18th century. This translation became the basis for Muslim law in the British Indian courts.
    

**2. Fatawa-e-Alamgiri (also known as Fatawa-e-Hindiyya)**

- **Origin:** Compiled under the patronage of the Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb (Alamgir) by a panel of 500 jurists.
    
- **Significance:** It is a massive digest of legal verdicts. It is the absolute authority on Hanafi law in the Indian Subcontinent (and Bangladesh). If there is a conflict between texts in South Asia, _Alamgiri_ usually prevails.
    
- **Translation:** Partially translated by Neil B.E. Baillie as _A Digest of Moohummudan Law_.
    

**3. Radd al-Muhtar (The Response to the Perplexed)**

- **Author:** Ibn Abidin (d. 1836 AD).
    
- **Significance:** Often referred to simply as _The Hashiyah of Ibn Abidin_. It is the final authoritative word in the Hanafi school for the late Ottoman period and modern times.
    

#### The "Anglo-Mohammedan" Textbooks

In the context of the syllabus (Khulna University/Bangladesh), "Books of Fiqh" also encompasses the textbooks written by modern scholars that synthesize these classical works for legal practitioners.

- **D.F. Mulla:** _Principles of Mahomedan Law_ (The standard practitioner's guide in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh).
    
- **Syed Ameer Ali:** _Mahommedan Law_.
    
- **Faiz Badruddin Tyabji:** _Muslim Law_.

### Important Notes & Tips

> - **Tip for Exams:** Always mention **Charles Hamilton's translation of the Hedaya** and **Neil B.E. Baillie's translation of the Fatawa-e-Alamgiri**. These are the two bridges through which classical Fiqh entered the modern legal system of Bangladesh.
>     
> - **Fact:** The _Hedaya_ was originally translated from Arabic to Persian, and then from Persian to English by Hamilton. Consequently, some nuances were lost, but it remains a primary source for courts.
>     
> - **Clarification:** Do not confuse "Sources of Law" (Quran, Sunnah) with "Books of Fiqh." The books are the _evidence_ of the law, not the lawgiver itself.
