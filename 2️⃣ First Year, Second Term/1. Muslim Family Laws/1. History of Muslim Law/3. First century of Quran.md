## 1.3 First Century of Quran (The Rashidun Era) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4.5/5):** This period explains how Muslim Law survived the death of its founder. It bridges the gap between divine revelation and human reasoning.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3.5/5):** Exam questions often focus on "The Collection of the Quran," "The Administration of Justice under the Orthodox Caliphs," or "The origins of Ijma and Qiyas."
    

---

**The Era of the Companions (632–661 AD)**

This period is technically known as the era of the **Rashidun Caliphs** (The Rightly Guided Caliphs: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali). It marks the "First Century" of the Quran's existence as a compiled text and a source of law.

The central crisis of this era was the **Cessation of Revelation**. With the death of Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) in 632 AD, the direct communication from God stopped. However, the Muslim empire rapidly expanded into Syria, Persia, and Egypt, encountering new cultures and complex legal issues (like land tax and inter-faith marriage) that were not explicitly mentioned in the Quran.

---

The legal history of this period focuses on two massive achievements: **Preservation** and **Interpretation**.

### 1. Preservation: The Collection of the Quran

Legally, the Quran is the "Ground Norm" (Supreme Law). If the text had been lost or altered, the entire legal system would have collapsed.

- **The Crisis (Battle of Yamama):** During the reign of the first Caliph, Abu Bakr, many _Huffaz_ (memorizers of the Quran) were killed in battle. There was a genuine fear that parts of the Quran might be lost.
    
- **The Compilation (Abu Bakr's Era):** At the urging of Umar, Abu Bakr ordered Zaid ibn Thabit (a chief scribe) to collect all written fragments (on parchment, bone, leaves) and verify them against the memorizers. This produced the first complete written copy (_Mushaf_).
    
- **The Standardization (Uthman's Era):** By the time of the third Caliph, Uthman, disputes arose regarding the _pronunciation_ (dialects) of the Quran. To prevent legal and theological anarchy, Uthman standardized the text into the **Qurayshi dialect**, made official copies, sent them to major provinces, and ordered all other variant manuscripts to be burned.
    
    - _Legal Significance:_ This ensured that the primary source of law remained **uniform** and **unalterable** for all time.
        

### 2. Interpretation: The Birth of Juristic Reasoning

Since there were no new verses to solve new problems, the Companions (_Sahaba_) had to develop a method to find the law. This established the prototype for later jurisprudence (_Usul al-Fiqh_).

**The Hierarchy of Sources:** The Caliphs (especially Umar) followed a strict method when deciding cases:

1. **Look in the Quran:** If the answer is there, follow it.
    
2. **Look in the Sunnah:** If not in the Quran, ask: "Did the Prophet judge on this?"
    
3. **Ijma (Consensus):** If the Sunnah was silent, the Caliph would assemble the leading Companions (Shura) to discuss. If they agreed on a solution, it became **Ijma-as-Sahaba** (Consensus of the Companions).
    
    - _Note:_ This form of Ijma is considered binding and infallible by most Sunni schools.
        
4. **Ijtihad/Qiyas (Individual Reasoning):** If there was no consensus, the jurist-caliph would use his own judgment to draw an analogy (_Qiyas_) between the new case and an old Quranic principle.
    

### 3. Administration of Justice

The legal system was simple and informal but effective.

- **No Separation of Powers:** Initially, the Caliph was the Head of State, Commander-in-Chief, and Chief Justice.
    
- **Appointment of Qadis:** As the empire grew, the Caliphs could not hear every case.
    
    - _Umar ibn al-Khattab_ is credited with structuring the judiciary. He appointed separate judges (_Qadis_) to provinces (e.g., Abu Musa al-Ashari in Basra).
        
    - **The Epistle of Umar:** A famous letter sent by Umar to Abu Musa serves as the earliest "Judicial Code of Conduct." It instructed the judge to:
        
        - Treat all parties equally.
            
        - Ask for proof from the plaintiff and oath from the defendant.
            
        - Review his own judgment if he realized he was wrong (Judicial Review).
            

### 4. Emergence of Divergent Opinions

During this century, the "seeds" of the future schools of law were sown. Companions settled in different cities, taking their specific knowledge of Hadith and their personal methods of reasoning with them.

- **Medina:** Remained the center of Tradition (Hadith).
    
- **Kufa (Iraq):** Became the center of Reason (_Ra'y_) because it was a cosmopolitan city with few Hadith scholars but many new social problems. This divergence eventually led to the Maliki (Medina) vs. Hanafi (Iraq) split.

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Key Exam Question:** "Discuss the legal reforms of Caliph Umar." Focus on his formalization of the judiciary and his use of _Ijtihad_ (e.g., he suspended the punishment for theft during a famine, arguing that the _intent_ of the law was justice, not cruelty).
    
- **Bangladeshi Context:**
    
    - The **"Triple Talaq"** (Three divorces in one sitting) controversy in Bangladesh traces back to this era. While the Prophet treated three pronouncements as one, Caliph Umar enforced it as _three separate/final divorces_ to deter men from abusing the words. Bangladeshi law (MFLO 1961) effectively reverted to the pre-Umar position to protect women, showing how modern law navigates early history.
        
- **Term to Memorize:** **"Ijma-as-Sahaba"**. In any legal argument, if you can quote that the "Companions agreed on this," it overrides almost any later scholarly opinion.
