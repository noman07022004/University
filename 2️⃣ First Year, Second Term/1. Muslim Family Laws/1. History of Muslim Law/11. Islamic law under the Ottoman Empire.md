## 1.11 Islamic Law under the Ottoman Empire ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4.5/5):** This era marks the most significant structural change in Islamic legal history: the shift from **Uncodified Jurist Law** to **Codified State Law**. It sets the precedent for all modern legislation, including the laws currently applied in Bangladesh.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3.5/5):** Questions often focus on "The Majallah," "The Ottoman Law of Family Rights 1917," or "The concept of Takhayyur (Eclecticism)."

---

***The Era of Codification***

For over a thousand years, Islamic Law existed as a vast collection of scholarly opinions found in Fiqh manuals (like the _Hedaya_ or _Fatawa Alamgiri_). There was no single "Law Book" with numbered sections. A judge (_Qadi_) had to search through these voluminous texts to find a ruling.

By the 19th century, the Ottoman Empire (which included most of the Arab world and Turkey) faced pressure to modernize its legal system to cope with European trade and administrative efficiency. This led to the **Tanzimat Reforms** and the process of **Codification**.

---

### 1. The Majallah (The Mejelle) – 1876

This is the most famous legal document of the Ottoman era and a landmark in Islamic legal history.

- **Definition:** The _Majallah-el-Ahkam-i-Adliya_ was the first **Civil Code** of the Ottoman Empire.
    
- **Nature:** It was derived entirely from **Hanafi Jurisprudence**, but it was arranged in the form of a modern European code (with numbered articles).
    
- **Scope:** It covered civil laws like **Contracts, Sale, Hire, Guarantee, and Torts**.
    
- **Exclusion:** Crucially, it **excluded Family Law** (Marriage, Divorce, Inheritance). The Ottomans felt that Family Law was too sacred and personal to be subjected to secular-style codification at that stage.
    
- **Significance:** It proved that Sharia could be codified into a modern statute without losing its religious character. It remained the civil law of Israel, Jordan, and Kuwait well into the 20th century.
    

### 2. The Ottoman Law of Family Rights (1917)

While the _Majallah_ ignored family law, social pressures eventually forced the Ottomans to act. Women in the empire suffered because the dominant **Hanafi School** was very strict regarding divorce: a woman could _not_ divorce her husband for neglect, desertion, or cruelty (unlike the Maliki school).

In 1917, the Empire passed the **Law of Family Rights**. This was the first true codification of Muslim Family Law in history.

- **Methodology – Takhayyur (Eclecticism):** This is the most critical legal innovation of the era.
    
    - The drafters did not stick strictly to the dominant Hanafi view.
        
    - Instead, they **picked and chose (_Takhayyur_)** the most suitable opinions from other Sunni schools (mainly Maliki) to solve modern problems.
        
    - _Example:_ They adopted the **Maliki** view that allows a wife to seek judicial divorce if her husband deserts her or mistreats her—something the Hanafi school did not allow.
        

### 3. Administrative Reforms

The Ottomans also reformed the **Judiciary**:

- **Secular Courts (Nizamiyya):** They created separate courts for criminal and commercial law that applied French-inspired secular laws.
    
- **Sharia Courts:** The jurisdiction of Sharia courts was restricted primarily to **Personal Status** (Family Law and Waqf). This "splitting" of the legal system is a model followed by many Muslim-majority countries today (though Bangladesh has a unified court system that applies different laws).
    

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Bangladeshi Context (The Link to 1939 Act):**
    
    - The Ottoman experiment with _Takhayyur_ (using Maliki rules for Hanafi women) directly inspired legislation in British India.
        
    - The **Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939**, which is currently valid in Bangladesh, uses the exact same logic: it allows a Bangladeshi Hanafi woman to divorce on grounds (like failure to maintain for 2 years) that are borrowed from the Maliki school.
        
- **Exam Definition:** If asked about **"The Majallah,"** define it as: "The first codified Civil Code of the Ottoman Empire (1876), based on Hanafi law, covering contracts and torts but excluding family law."
    
- **Key Concept:** **Codification**. Be able to argue the pros and cons.
    
    - _Pro:_ Certainty, uniformity, easier for judges.
        
    - _Con:_ Rigid, loses the flexibility of the Qadi to tailor justice to the individual case.
