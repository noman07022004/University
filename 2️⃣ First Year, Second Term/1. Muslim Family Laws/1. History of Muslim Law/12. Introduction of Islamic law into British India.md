## 1.12 Introduction of Islamic law into British India ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the direct origin of the legal system currently operating in Bangladesh. To understand why we cite "Privy Council" decisions or why the "Shariat Application Act 1937" exists, you must grasp this historical transition.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4.5/5):** Extremely frequent. Questions often ask to "Define Anglo-Muhammadan Law," "Discuss the provisions of the Warren Hastings Plan 1772," or "Explain the legislative interventions of the British period."

---

***The Birth of Anglo-Muhammadan Law***

When the British East India Company took over the administration of justice in Bengal (Diwani rights), they faced a dilemma: should they impose English law or retain the existing Mughal system? The path they chose created a unique hybrid legal system known as **Anglo-Muhammadan Law**.

---
This history is generally divided into three phases:

### 1. Phase I: Retention and The Warren Hastings Plan (1772)

In 1772, **Warren Hastings** (Governor-General) formulated a judicial plan that became the charter for Muslim Law in the subcontinent.

- **Section 27 of the Regulation of 1772:** This was the foundational provision. It stated that in all suits regarding **inheritance, marriage, caste, and other religious usages or institutions**:
    
    - The laws of the **Koran** shall be valid for **Mahomedans**.
        
    - The laws of the **Shaster** shall be valid for **Gentoos (Hindus)**.
        
- **Native Law Officers:** The British judges did not know Arabic or Persian. To solve this, they attached **Maulvis** (Muslim scholars) and **Pandits** (Hindu scholars) to the courts. The British judge would find the facts, ask the Maulvi for the applicable law, and then deliver the judgment.
    

### 2. Phase II: The Era of Precedent and Misinterpretation (1790s–1864)

Over time, the British began to reshape the law.

- **Criminal Law:** Initially, the British applied Muslim Criminal Law (which included punishments like amputation). However, they found it "barbaric" and gradually replaced it, culminating in the **Indian Penal Code (1860)**, which completely abolished Islamic criminal law.
    
- **Evidence:** The Islamic Law of Evidence (which required specific numbers of male witnesses) was replaced by the **Indian Evidence Act (1872)**.
    
- **Personal Law:** Only personal family law remained "Islamic."
    
- **Abolition of Law Officers (1864):** The British eventually felt confident enough to dispense with the Maulvis. They began relying on **English Translations** of Islamic texts (like _Hamilton’s Hedaya_ and _Baillie’s Digest_).
    
    - _The Problem:_ These translations were often inaccurate or translated from Persian summaries rather than original Arabic texts. British judges applied "English rules of equity" where the text was unclear, leading to rulings that sometimes contradicted pure Sharia.
        

### 3. Phase III: "Anglo-Muhammadan Law" and Legislative Correction

By the late 19th century, a new body of law had emerged. **Anglo-Muhammadan Law** is defined as: _The rules of Islamic law as interpreted, modified, and applied by the British Courts in India._

This system relied heavily on **Stare Decisis** (Binding Precedent). A decision by the **Privy Council** (the highest court of appeal in London) became the law of the land, even if it contradicted the Quran.

Key Conflict & Legislation:

Sometimes, the Privy Council made grave errors due to their lack of understanding of the spirit of Islam.

- **The Waqf Controversy (Abul Fata Case 1894):** The Privy Council ruled that "Family Waqf" (Waqf-al-Aulad) was invalid because it was just a way to keep property in the family forever, which violated the English rule against "Perpetuities."
    
    - _The Reaction:_ The Muslims of India were outraged because family waqf is a valid religious concept.
        
    - _The Remedy:_ The legislature passed the **Mussalman Wakf Validating Act 1913** to specifically _overturn_ the Privy Council’s decision and restore the Islamic position.
        

### 4. The Shariat Application Act 1937

Before 1937, many Muslim communities (like the Khojas and Memons) followed **Hindu Customary Law** in inheritance (giving women nothing).

- The **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937** was passed to unify the community.
    
- **Section 2:** It mandated that in matters of marriage, divorce, inheritance, waqf, etc., the "rule of decision" shall be the **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat)**, overriding any custom or usage to the contrary.
    
- _Significance:_ This Act is still the primary authority that forces Bangladeshi courts to apply Muslim law today.
    

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- Definition for Exams: If asked to define Anglo-Muhammadan Law, use this formula:
    
    $$Islamic \ Principles + English \ Legal \ Procedure + Judicial \ Precedents = Anglo-Muhammadan \ Law$$
    
- **Bangladeshi Context:** The legal system in Bangladesh _is_ effectively the Anglo-Muhammadan system.
    
    - We use the **English adversarial procedure** (Judge is a referee, not an investigator).
        
    - We rely on **Precedents** (DLR/BLD cases).
        
    - We rely on the **1937 Act**.
        
- **Key Criticism:** British judges often treated the _Hedaya_ (a text from the 12th century) as if it were a modern statute, ignoring the centuries of legal development that happened in between. This fossilized the law.
