## History of Muslim Law ⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This topic provides the foundational context for the entire course. Understanding the evolution from divine revelation to codified law is essential to grasping why current laws exist and how they are interpreted.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While fundamental, exam questions often prioritize practical problem-solving (inheritance/divorce) over pure history. However, broad questions on the "Evolution of Islamic Law" or specific notes on "Sources" or "Anglo-Muslim Law" are common.

---

Muslim Law, or Islamic Law (_Sharia_), is not merely a system of commands and prohibitions but a comprehensive code of life derived from divine revelation. Unlike Western legal systems, which are secular and fluid, traditional Islamic law is theoretically grounded in immutable divine texts. However, its history reveals a dynamic process of human interpretation (_Fiqh_) where jurists adapted divine principles to changing social realities. The history of Muslim law is the story of the tension and synthesis between the divine text (_Nass_) and human reasoning (_Ra'y_), evolving from the tribal customs of Arabia to the codified statutes of modern states like Bangladesh.

---

### Phase I: The Foundation and The Prophetic Era

**Subtopics Covered:** _Pre-Islamic background, Muhammad and the Quran, Nature of Islamic Law._

To understand the nature of Islamic law, one must look at what preceded it. The **Pre-Islamic background** (often termed _Jahiliyya_ or Age of Ignorance) in Arabia was characterized by a tribal society governed not by a central state, but by customary law (_Sunnah_ of the ancestors). There were no written codes; disputes were settled by arbitrators (_Hakam_) based on tribal honor and retaliation (_Qisas_). Women had little to no legal status, and practices like unlimited polygamy and female infanticide were prevalent.

The advent of **Muhammad (PBUH) and the Quran** (610–632 AD) marked a radical shift. The Quran introduced legal reforms that modified, rather than entirely abrogated, existing Arab customs.

- **Nature of Quranic Legislation:** The Quran is not a law book in the modern sense; only about 80–500 verses (depending on interpretation) deal strictly with legal matters (_Ayat al-Ahkam_). These primarily focused on family law (marriage, divorce, inheritance) to improve the status of the weak (women, orphans).
    
- **The Prophet’s Role:** Where the Quran was silent, the Prophet’s practice and sayings (_Sunnah_) served as the living interpretation of the law. This period established the **Nature of Islamic Law** as divine in origin—sovereignty belongs to Allah alone, not to any earthly king or parliament.
    

### Phase II: The Formative Period (The First Century & Umayyads)

**Subtopics Covered:** _First century of Quran, Umayyad administration, Ancient schools of law, Early systematic reasoning of the second century._

Following the Prophet’s death, the **First century of Quran** (roughly the era of the Rashidun Caliphs and early Umayyads) saw the need for expansion. As Islam spread beyond Arabia, Muslims encountered complex civilizations (Persian, Byzantine). The Quran and Sunnah were not exhaustive for these new situations.

- **Umayyad Administration (661–750 AD):** The Umayyad Caliphs appointed judges (_Qadis_) to settle disputes. Since there was no compiled code, these Qadis relied on their own discretion and reasoning (_Ra'y_) alongside local customs, provided they did not blatantly contradict the Quran. This administrative practice laid the groundwork for a distinct legal system.
    
- **Ancient Schools of Law:** Distinct centers of legal learning arose in Medina, Kufa, Basra, and Mecca. These were not yet the "schools" we know today but were geographical groups of jurists.
    
    - _Medina:_ Focused heavily on "Tradition" (practices of the Prophet’s city).
        
    - _Kufa (Iraq):_ Focused more on "Reason" (_Ra'y_) due to the lack of prophetic traditions circulating there and the complex cosmopolitan environment.
        
- **Early Systematic Reasoning:** In the second century of Islam, jurists began to systematize how laws were derived. They started using _Qiyas_ (analogical deduction) to apply Quranic principles to new cases.
    

### Phase III: The Era of Consolidation and Conflict (Abbasids)

**Subtopics Covered:** _The first Abbasids legislation and administration, Opposition movements and the traditionists, Schools of classical theory._

The **Abbasid Revolution (750 AD)** brought a shift from the purely administrative law of the Umayyads to a law based more strictly on religious scholarship.

- **Opposition Movements and the Traditionists (_Ahl al-Hadith_):** A conflict arose between the "People of Reason" (_Ahl al-Ra'y_, mostly in Iraq) and the "People of Tradition" (_Ahl al-Hadith_). The Traditionists opposed the free use of human reason (_Ra'y_) in law, arguing that law should be based solely on the text (Hadith). They began a massive movement to collect and verify the sayings of the Prophet to supersede human reasoning.
    
- **Schools of Classical Theory:** This conflict was synthesized, largely by Imam Shafi'i, leading to the crystallization of the four Sunni schools (_Madhhabs_):
    
    1. **Hanafi (Imam Abu Hanifa):** Known for liberal use of juristic preference (_Istihsan_).
        
    2. **Maliki (Imam Malik):** Rooted in the practice of Medina.
        
    3. **Shafi'i (Imam Shafi'i):** Architect of _Usul-al-Fiqh_; prioritized Hadith over reason but validated analogy (_Qiyas_).
        
    4. **Hanbali (Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal):** Strict adherence to Hadith; the most literalist.
        
- **Abbasid Legislation:** While the jurists developed private law (family, contracts), the Abbasid state handled public/criminal law through _Mazalim_ courts, creating a duality between religious law and state administration.
    

### Phase IV: Stagnation, Reaction, and Empire

**Subtopics Covered:** _Purist reactions, Islamic law under the Ottoman Empire._

After the 10th century, the "Gate of Ijtihad" (independent reasoning) was widely considered closed, leading to the era of _Taqlid_ (imitation).

- **Purist Reactions:** Scholars like Ibn Taymiyyah (and later the Wahhabi movement) reacted against the stagnation and the blind imitation of schools. They called for a return to the purity of the Quran and Sunnah, rejecting the accrued scholastic glosses.
    
- **Islamic Law under the Ottoman Empire:** The Ottomans were Hanafis. A major milestone in legal history occurred in the 19th century with the **Majalla** (Majallat al-Ahkam al-Adliyya). This was the first attempt to _codify_ Islamic law (specifically contract and civil law) into European-style numbered articles, making it enforceable by secular courts.
    

### Phase V: The Colonial Transformation (British India)

**Subtopics Covered:** _Introduction of Islamic law into British India._

This is the most immediate historical antecedent for Bangladeshi law. When the British took power in India, they pledged not to interfere with religious laws.

- **Warren Hastings' Plan (1772):** Declared that in matters of "inheritance, marriage, caste, and other religious usages," the laws of the Quran would apply to Muslims.
    
- **Anglo-Muslim Law:** The British judges, unfamiliar with Arabic texts, relied on translations (like the _Hedaya_) and precedents. This created a hybrid system known as "Anglo-Muslim Law"—Islamic principles interpreted through English common law methodology (precedent, stare decisis).
    
- **Legislation:** Gradually, the British codified aspects of the law or overrode it, culminating in the **Shariat Application Act of 1937**, which formally enforced the Sharia over customary practices in India (except in agricultural land).

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Key Distinction:** Remember the difference between _Sharia_ (the divine path/Law as it exists in God's mind) and _Fiqh_ (Human understanding/Jurisprudence). The history of Muslim law is essentially the history of _Fiqh_.
    
- **British Influence:** For exams in Bangladesh/India/Pakistan, the **1772 Hastings Plan** and the **1937 Shariat Act** are vital dates. They mark the transition from traditional Qadi justice to the modern court system.
    
- **The Four Schools:** You do not need to know every difference between the schools, but know that **Hanafi** is the dominant school in Bangladesh and was the official school of the Mughal and Ottoman empires.
    
- **Reason vs. Revelation:** The core historical conflict is _Ahl al-Ra'y_ (Reason) vs. _Ahl al-Hadith_ (Text). Imam Shafi'i is the "Master Architect" who bridged this gap by creating the systematic rules of interpretation (_Usul al-Fiqh_).
