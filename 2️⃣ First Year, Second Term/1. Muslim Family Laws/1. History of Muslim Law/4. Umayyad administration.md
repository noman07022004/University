## 1.4 Umayyad Administration (661–750 AD) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This era explains the structural split between "State Law" and "Religious Law." It helps you understand why, historically, independent scholars (Imams) were often at odds with the government—a dynamic that influenced how the schools of law developed independently of the state.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3.5/5):** Questions often focus on the "Appointment of Qadis," the "Influence of foreign customs," or the criticism of the Umayyads by the "Pious Opposition."

---

**The Age of the Qadi and Politics**

Following the assassination of Caliph Ali, the capital moved from Medina (the center of religion) to **Damascus** (a center of Byzantine administration). The **Umayyad Dynasty** transformed the Caliphate from a patriarchal religious leadership into a **Dynastic Monarchy (_Mulk_)**.

---

This period is characterized by the **Dual Nature of Law**: the theoretical _Sharia_ (religious law) and the practical _Siyasah_ (political administration).

### 1. The Appointment of the Qadi (Judge)

The most significant legal contribution of the Umayyads was the formal institutionalization of the Judiciary.

- **Delegation of Power:** The Umayyad Caliphs, occupied with expanding the empire and managing politics, could no longer personally sit as judges for every dispute (unlike the Rashidun Caliphs). They began appointing **Qadis** (judges) to major garrison cities (Kufa, Basra, Cairo).
    
- **Unrestricted Discretion:** At this stage, there were no "Law Books" or "Codes."
    
    - The Qadi was expected to decide based on the Quran and Sunnah.
        
    - However, where texts were silent, the Qadi relied heavily on his own **Personal Opinion (_Ra'y_)** and common sense.
        
    - **Result:** Law became "Judge-made Law." A Qadi in Egypt might rule differently on a divorce case than a Qadi in Iraq, leading to legal inconsistency.
        

### 2. Incorporation of Foreign Customs (_Urf_)

The Umayyad Empire ruled over non-Arabs (Persians, Syrians, Egyptians) who had their own sophisticated legal systems (Roman/Byzantine and Sassanid).

- **The Principle of Retention:** The Umayyad administrators generally allowed local customs to continue unless they directly conflicted with Islam.
    
- **Administrative Adoption:** They adopted Byzantine market regulations and military organization. This infused Islamic law with foreign administrative concepts, particularly in the realm of taxation and land law.
    

### 3. Distinctive Legal Features of the Era

- **The Registry of Decisions (_Diwan_):** The Umayyads established central registries to record judicial decisions, creating the first rudimentary system of "Case Law" (precedents).
    
- **Political Justice (_Mazalim_):** The Qadi handled private disputes (family, contracts). However, crimes against the state or complaints against powerful governors were handled by the Caliph himself in separate "Grievance Courts" (_Mazalim_). This effectively placed the ruler above the ordinary law.
    

### 4. The "Mawali" Problem and Fiscal Law

A major legal controversy of this era was the treatment of **Mawali** (non-Arab converts to Islam).

- **Taxation Inequality:** According to early Islamic principles, Muslims pay _Zakat_ (lower tax) and non-Muslims pay _Jizya_ (poll tax). The Umayyads, fearing a loss of revenue, continued to tax new non-Arab converts as if they were non-Muslims.
    
- **Legal Backlash:** This violation of the Islamic principle of "Equality of all Believers" fueled the opposition movements that eventually overthrew the dynasty.
    

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Comparison Tip:**
    
    - **Rashidun Era:** Judge = Caliph. Law = Quran/Sunnah + Shura (Consultation).
        
    - **Umayyad Era:** Judge = Appointed Official (_Qadi_). Law = Quran/Sunnah + _Ra'y_ (Individual Opinion) + Political Policy.
        
- **Critical Perspective:** In traditional Islamic history, the Umayyads are often viewed negatively as "Godless Kings" who corrupted the purity of the Caliphate. From a _legal history_ perspective, however, they were pragmatic innovators who built the necessary administrative machinery for a global empire.
    
- **Bangladeshi Context:** The concept of the **"Separation of the Judiciary"** (separated from the Executive) is a modern struggle in Bangladesh (Masdar Hossain case). The Umayyad era is an early example of the _opposite_: the Judiciary (Qadis) served at the pleasure of the Executive (Caliph), often leading to biased rulings—a historical lesson on the necessity of judicial independence.
