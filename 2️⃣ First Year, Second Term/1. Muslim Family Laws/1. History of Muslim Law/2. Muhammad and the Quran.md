## 1.2 Muhammad and the Quran ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the absolute bedrock of Muslim Law. All other sources (Sunnah, Ijma, Qiyas) derive their authority from the Quran. You cannot understand the "why" of the law without understanding the revelation.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** High frequency. Questions often ask about "The legislative functions of the Prophet," "The characteristics of Quranic legislation," or "The distinction between Meccan and Medinan verses in legal context."

---

**The First Period (609–632 AD)**

This specific period marks the transition from the age of custom (Jahiliyya) to the age of divine law (_Sharia_). It spans the 23 years of the Prophetic mission of **Muhammad (PBUH)**.

---
### 1. The Role of Muhammad (PBUH)

In this legal era, the Prophet possessed a dual capacity which is unique in legal history:

1. **The Messenger (_Rasul_):** He was the conduit for the **Quran**. When he spoke the Quran, it was the direct Word of God (_Kalamullah_), not his own opinion. This law was immutable.
    
2. **The Supreme Judge and Legislator:** Apart from reciting the Quran, the Prophet had to interpret it and apply it to daily disputes. His own rulings, sayings, and conduct formed the **Sunnah**.
    
    - _Authority:_ The Quran explicitly established his legal authority: _"Obey Allah and obey the Messenger"_ (4:59). Thus, obeying the Prophet’s legal judgment became a religious duty.
        
    - _Nature of Legislation:_ The Prophet did not create a rigid code all at once. He legislated **ad-hoc**—meaning laws were revealed or enacted in response to specific social problems or questions asked by followers (_Asbab al-Nuzul_ or Causes of Revelation).
        

### 2. The Quran as the Primary Source

The Quran is the primary, original, and supreme source of Muslim Law. However, legally speaking, the Quran is **not a legal code** (like the Penal Code or Contract Act). It is primarily a book of guidance, ethics, and theology.

**The Legal Verses (_Ayat al-Ahkam_):** Out of over 6,000 verses in the Quran, only a small fraction deal strictly with law (estimates vary from 80 to 500 verses depending on definition). These legal verses regulate:

- **Personal Status:** Marriage, divorce, dower, inheritance (the most detailed section).
    
- **Civil Laws:** Contracts, usury (_Riba_), loans, and evidence.
    
- **Criminal Laws:** Penalties for theft, adultery, and false accusation (_Hudud_).
    
- **Rituals:** Prayer, fasting, pilgrimage (which have legal validity conditions).
    

### 3. Meccan vs. Medinan Verses (Crucial for Legal Analysis)

To understand the Quranic legislation, one must distinguish between the two periods of revelation. This distinction is vital for understanding the _spirit_ vs. the _letter_ of the law.

- **The Meccan Period (609–622 AD):**
    
    - The Prophet was in Mecca, facing persecution.
        
    - **Content:** These verses focus on **Theology (_Tawheed_)**, ethics, the Day of Judgment, and moral rectification.
        
    - **Legal Status:** Very few strict laws were revealed here. The focus was on building the "conscience" of the believer so they would obey the law when it came later.
        
- **The Medinan Period (622–632 AD):**
    
    - The Prophet migrated (_Hijra_) to Medina and established a State.
        
    - **Content:** These verses are **Legislative**. Since there was now a state, laws were needed to regulate society.
        
    - **Key Laws:** The verses regarding punishment (_Hudud_), inheritance (_Faraid_), prohibition of usury (_Riba_), and rules of war were revealed during this period.
        

### 4. Characteristics of Quranic Legislation

The Quran introduced law in a specific manner that differs from modern secular legislation:

1. **Gradualism (_Tadrij_):** The Quran did not enforce all laws instantly to avoid shocking society.
    
    - _Example (Alcohol):_ It was not banned overnight. First, it was described as having "harm and benefit" (2:219); then forbidden during prayers (4:43); and finally, totally prohibited (5:90).
        
2. **Abrogation (_Naskh_):** Since the law was evolutionary, a later verse could repeal or modify an earlier ruling to suit the changing readiness of the community.
    
3. **Reform of Custom:** The Quran dealt with pre-Islamic customs in three ways:
    
    - **Abolition:** Completely banning harmful customs (e.g., Infanticide, Gambling, Usury).
        
    - **Modification:** Accepting the custom but refining it (e.g., Polygamy was restricted to four wives; Divorce was restricted to three pronouncements).
        
    - **Confirmation (Silence):** If the Quran was silent on a beneficial custom (e.g., certain trade practices), it was tacitly approved.
        

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Exam Term - "The First Period":** If an exam asks about the "First Period of Muslim Law," it refers strictly to the lifetime of the Prophet (609–632 AD). The source of law here is purely **Revelation (_Wahi_)**.
    
- **Bangladeshi Context:** In Bangladeshi Family Courts, when there is a conflict between a specific verse of the Quran and a classical opinion of a jurist (like Abu Hanifa), the **Quran prevails**. However, in practice, courts rely on the _interpretation_ of the Quran found in standard texts (like _D.F. Mulla's Principles of Mahomedan Law_), unless the _Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961_ provides a specific statutory override.
    
- **Misconception Alert:** Students often think the Quran covers _all_ laws. It does not. It provides the **General Principles** (Grue-norm). The details were left to the Prophet (Sunnah) and later jurists (Ijtihad).
    
    - _Example:_ The Quran says "Establish Prayer," but doesn't say _how_ to pray. The Sunnah provides the _how_.
        
- **Key Quote for Essays:** "The Quran is the Magna Carta of Muslims." It radically improved the status of women and the poor compared to the Pre-Islamic era.
