## 1.8 The First Abbasids Legislation and Administration (750–850 AD) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This era is critical because it represents the "Golden Age" where Islamic Law became a state-sponsored institution. It explains the structural separation between the "Ruler" and the "Law" which is a unique feature of Islamic constitutional history.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** High frequency. Questions often focus on "The role of Imam Abu Yusuf as the first Chief Justice," "Ibn al-Muqaffa’s proposal for codification," or "The patronage of the Abbasids towards the jurists."

---

**The Golden Age of Patronage**

The **Abbasid Revolution (750 AD)** was not just a change of dynasty; it was a promise to return to the "True Sharia" and dismantle the "godless" administration of the Umayyads. To legitimize their rule, the Abbasids actively patronized the religious scholars (_Ulama_), integrating them into the state machinery.

This period marks the transition from **Judge-Made Law** (individual Qadis deciding cases) to **Jurist-Made Law** (State following specific legal schools).

---

### 1. The Separation of Powers (Theory vs. Practice)

The Abbasids acknowledged that they were not the source of law; God was. This created a theoretical separation:

- **Legislative Power:** Belonged to the Scholars (_Mujtahids_), who interpreted the Quran/Sunnah. The Caliph could not "make" religious law; he could only enforce it.
    
- **Executive Power:** Belonged to the Caliph.
    
- **Judicial Power:** Belonged to the _Qadis_, who were now drawn from the class of professional scholars.
    

### 2. The Office of the Chief Justice (_Qadi al-Qadat_)

The most significant administrative innovation of this era was the creation of the office of the **Chief Justice of the Empire**.

- **Imam Abu Yusuf:** The most famous disciple of Abu Hanifa became the first _Qadi al-Qadat_ under Caliph Harun al-Rashid.
    
- **Function:** He had the power to appoint and remove judges across the empire.
    
- **Impact on the Hanafi School:** Because Abu Yusuf was a Hanafi, he appointed Hanafi judges almost exclusively. This state patronage is the primary reason why the **Hanafi School** became the dominant school in the Islamic world (and subsequently in Bengal/Bangladesh), while other schools like the Awza'i school faded away.
    

### 3. Ibn al-Muqaffa’s Proposal for Codification

In the early Abbasid period, a secretary of state named **Ibn al-Muqaffa** wrote a famous treatise (_Risala fi al-Sahaba_) to Caliph Al-Mansur.

- **The Problem:** He observed that court rulings were chaotic. One judge in Basra would rule differently from a judge in Kufa based on their personal _Ra'y_ (opinion).
    
- **The Proposal:** He urged the Caliph to review all these conflicting opinions, select the best ones, and enact them as a **Uniform Written Code** that all judges must follow.
    
- **The Rejection:** The Caliph rejected this proposal. The scholars argued that Sharia cannot be "frozen" by a state decree; it must remain flexible in the hands of the jurists.
    
    - _Significance:_ This rejection meant Islamic Law remained **Uncodified** for another 1,000 years (until the Ottoman _Majallah_). It preserved the independence of the law from the state but maintained the problem of uncertainty.
        

### 4. The "Inquisition" (_Mihna_)

While the Abbasids patronized scholars, they also tried to control them. This led to the _Mihna_ (833–848 AD), an inquisition where the Caliphs tried to force judges to accept the rationalist doctrine that the "Quran is created" (Mutazila theology).

- **Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal:** He famously resisted this state interference, was tortured, and imprisoned. His victory marked the permanent triumph of the **Traditionists** over the Rationalists and established that the **Law is above the State**.

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Bangladeshi Context:**
    
    - **The Dominance of Hanafi Law:** If you are asked why Bangladesh is Hanafi, trace it back here. It wasn't just theological preference; it was **Abbasid Administrative Policy** via Abu Yusuf that entrenched Hanafi law in the eastern provinces of the empire (which later influenced the Mughals).
        
- **Exam Key Figure:** **Imam Abu Yusuf**. He is the bridge between pure theory (Abu Hanifa) and practical statecraft. His book _Kitab al-Kharaj_ (Book of Taxation) is the first example of Fiqh being written specifically for a ruler to guide state policy.
    
- **Critical Analysis:** Contrast "Codification" with "Interpretation." Ibn al-Muqaffa wanted stability (Code); the Ulema wanted flexibility (Interpretation). This tension exists today: Bangladeshi Family Courts prefer clear statutes (MFLO 1961), but Ulema often prefer classical flexibility.
