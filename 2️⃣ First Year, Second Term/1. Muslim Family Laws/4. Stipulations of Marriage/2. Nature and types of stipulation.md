## 4.2 Nature and Types of Stipulation ⭐⭐⭐⭐½

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This topic bridges the gap between theory and practice. It teaches you how to draft a marriage deed (_Kabinnama_) that actually protects a client's rights.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Very frequent in problem-based questions. Example: _"A husband stipulated he would never marry a second wife. He did. What is the wife's remedy?"_
    

---


Since Muslim marriage is a **Civil Contract**, the parties (bride and groom) have the legal freedom to negotiate terms and insert specific clauses into the contract. These clauses are called **Stipulations** (_Shart_).

However, unlike a pure business contract, the freedom to stipulate in marriage is not absolute. It is limited by the **Sharia**. You cannot agree to something that God has forbidden, nor can you agree to something that defeats the very purpose of marriage (like agreeing to never have children or never live together).

The legal validity of these stipulations is judged by a simple test: **Does the condition violate the primary objectives or "natural consequences" of the marriage contract?**

### Classification of Stipulations

In the **Hanafi School** (applicable in Bangladesh), stipulations are divided into three distinct categories based on their enforceability:

#### 1. Valid Stipulations (_Sahih_)

These are conditions that are reasonable, lawful, and consistent with the object of marriage. They are fully enforceable in a court of law. If the husband violates a valid stipulation, the wife typically has the right to dissolve the marriage (if that power was delegated) or sue for damages/breach.

**Common Examples of Valid Stipulations:**

- **Restriction on Polygamy:** A condition that the husband shall not take a second wife during the continuance of the first marriage.
    
    - _Legal Effect:_ If he marries again, the first wife cannot "void" the second marriage, but she can claim the right to divorce herself (if delegated) or live separately while claiming maintenance.
        
- **Delegated Divorce (_Tafweez-e-Talaq_):** The most powerful stipulation in Bangladesh. The husband delegates his power of Talaq to the wife, allowing her to divorce herself in specific circumstances (e.g., if he hits her).
    
    - _Note:_ This is found in **Column 18** of the standard Bangladeshi _Kabinnama_.
        
- **Residence:** A condition that the husband shall not remove the wife from her "conjugal domicile" (her home city) or must provide a separate house from his quarreling relatives.
    
- **Maintenance:** A condition fixing a specific monthly allowance for the wife, provided it is not excessive.
    

#### 2. Void Stipulations (_Batil_)

These are conditions that are contrary to the _nature_ of the contract or violate a specific rule of Sharia, but they are **not fatal** to the marriage itself.

- **The Rule:** The **Stipulation is Void**, but the **Marriage remains Valid**. The law treats the contract as if the condition was never written.
    

**Common Examples:**

- **No Dower:** A condition that "I marry you without any Dower (_Mahr_)."
    
    - _Result:_ The condition is ignored. The wife is still entitled to "Proper Dower" (_Mahr-e-Mithl_) because Dower is a mandatory legal requirement of Nikah.
        
- **No Maintenance:** A condition that the wife will maintain herself and not claim food/clothing from the husband. (Void because maintenance is the husband's absolute duty).
    
- **No Cohabitation:** A condition that the husband and wife shall not live together or have sexual relations. (Void because it defeats the primary biological purpose of marriage).
    
- **Forfeiture of Rights:** A condition that the wife shall have no right of inheritance from the husband. (Void because inheritance is fixed by Quranic law).
    

#### 3. Illegal / Fatal Stipulations (Voiding the Marriage)

Some conditions are so repugnant to the definition of marriage that they invalidate the entire contract.

- **The Rule:** Both the **Stipulation is Void** and the **Marriage is Void**.
    

**Primary Example:**

- **Temporary Marriage (_Mut'a_):** In Sunni Law, marriage is by definition a permanent union. If a condition limits the duration (e.g., _"I marry you for 2 years"_), the condition destroys the essence of the contract.
    
    - _Result:_ The entire marriage is void (_Batil_) or irregular (_Fasid_) depending on the jurist, but it does not create a valid Sunni marriage.
        
    - _Contrast:_ In **Shia Law** (Ithna Ashari school), such a stipulation is valid and creates a temporary marriage (_Mut'a_).

### 📝 Important Notes & Tips

- **Bangladeshi Context (The Kabinnama):**
    
    - In Bangladesh, the **Nikahnama (Form 1601)** is the battleground for stipulations.
        
    - **Column 18 (Tafweez-e-Talaq):** This is where valid stipulations are usually entered. If a lawyer writes "The wife can divorce if husband marries again," this is a valid binding stipulation.
        
    - **Column 19:** Often used to restrict the husband's right of divorce, though legally, the husband's inherent right to Talaq cannot be completely removed, only made financially difficult.
        
- **Exam Strategy (Problem Questions):**
    
    - _Scenario:_ "A marries B on the condition that B will not claim Dower."
        
    - _Answer:_ State that the marriage is Valid, but the condition is Void. B can still claim Proper Dower. Cite the principle that Dower is an essential incident of marriage.
        
- **Key Distinction:**
    
    - **Valid Condition:** Enforceable.
        
    - **Void Condition:** Ignored (Marriage stands).
        
    - **Illegal Condition:** Destroys Marriage.
