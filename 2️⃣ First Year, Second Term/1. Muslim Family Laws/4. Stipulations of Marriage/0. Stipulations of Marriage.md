## Stipulations of Marriage ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is a cornerstone concept. Understanding that Muslim marriage is a **civil contract** (not a sacrament) changes how you view every other aspect of the law—divorce, dower, and inheritance. It explains why stipulations (conditions) can be added to the marriage deed.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** A very frequent exam question is: _"Muslim marriage is a civil contract, but it has spiritual elements. Discuss."_ Also, questions regarding specific conditions in the _Kabinnama_ (marriage deed) are common in problem-solving scenarios.
    

### Introduction

In many religious systems (like Hindu or Canon law), marriage is a permanent, indissoluble sacrament. In contrast, Muslim law treats marriage (_Nikah_) primarily as a **civil contract** (_Aqd_). It is a legal agreement between two parties to legalize sexual relations and procreation. Because it is a contract, the parties have the freedom to negotiate terms and conditions (_Stipulations_) before or at the time of marriage, provided these conditions do not violate the core principles of Islam.

### 1. Contractual Nature of Marriage

**Subtopics Covered:** _Contractual nature of marriage._

The view of Muslim marriage as a contract is derived from its formation and characteristics. Justice Mahmood in the landmark case _Abdul Kadir v. Salima (1886)_ famously observed that "Marriage among Muslims is not a sacrament, but purely a civil contract."

**Why is it considered a Civil Contract?**

- **Proposal and Acceptance:** Like any commercial contract, it requires _Ijab_ (Offer) and _Qabul_ (Acceptance).
    
- **Consideration (_Mahr_):** Just as a contract requires consideration to be valid, a Muslim marriage requires Dower (_Mahr_) to be paid or promised by the husband to the wife.
    
- **Free Consent:** The free consent of both parties (who are adults/sane) is mandatory. Forced marriages are void.
    
- **Dissolution:** Unlike a sacrament which is eternal, this bond can be dissolved by Divorce (_Talaq_), similar to the termination of a contract.
    

**Is it _only_ a Civil Contract?** While legally it functions as a contract, religiously it is a devotional act (_Ibadat_). The Prophet (PBUH) described it as "half of faith." Therefore, it is often described as **"a civil contract with spiritual and moral consequences."**

### 2. Nature and Types of Stipulations

**Subtopics Covered:** _Nature and types of stipulation._

Since marriage is a contract, the parties can agree to certain terms (_Shart_). These are usually recorded in the marriage deed (_Kabinnama_). However, not all conditions are valid. The law classifies stipulations into three categories:

#### A. Valid Stipulations (_Sahih_)

These are conditions that are lawful and enforceable because they do not contradict the basic purpose of the marriage contract.

- **Examples:**
    
    - A condition that the husband shall not remove the wife from her "conjugal domicile" (her city/town).
        
    - A condition that the wife will have the delegated right to divorce herself (_Talaq-i-Tafwid_) if the husband beats her or takes a second wife.
        
    - A stipulation fixing a specific amount of Dower (_Mahr_).
        
- **Effect:** If breached, the wife has legal remedies (e.g., executing the divorce or suing for damages), but the marriage remains valid.
    

#### B. Void Stipulations (_Batil_)

These are conditions that directly contradict the very purpose or essence of the marriage contract.

- **Examples:**
    
    - A condition that the couple will _not_ have sexual intercourse. (Contradicts the core purpose: legalization of intimacy).
        
    - A condition that the husband will not pay any Dower. (Dower is a mandatory legal right).
        
    - A condition that the marriage is temporary (in Sunni Law).
        
- **Effect:** The **stipulation is void**, but the **marriage itself remains valid**. The law ignores the condition as if it was never written.
    

#### C. Voidable/Irregular Conditions (Leading to Invalidity)

Some conditions are so fundamentally flawed that they might render the marriage itself irregular (_Fasid_) or void depending on the school of law.

- **Example:** A condition establishing a marriage for a fixed period (_Mut'a_) is void in Sunni law and makes the marriage void.

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **The _Kabinnama_:** In Bangladesh, the standard Marriage Registration Form (Form 1601) contains specific columns for stipulations. **Column 18** is famous for asking: _"Is the husband giving the wife the power to divorce herself?"_ This is a prime example of a valid stipulation (_Talaq-i-Tafwid_).
    
- **Distinction:** Remember the difference between a **Void Stipulation** (Condition dies, Marriage lives) and a **Void Marriage** (Marriage dies). In Sunni law, illegal conditions usually just drop off, leaving the marriage intact.
    
- **Case Law:** The case of _Abdul Kadir v. Salima (1886)_ is the "Gold Standard" citation for the nature of Muslim marriage. Memorize Justice Mahmood's quote.
    
- **Practical Application:** In problem questions, if a husband agrees to a condition (e.g., "I will live in my wife's parents' house") and then breaks it, the wife usually cannot force him to live there (as he has the right to decide the domicile), but she _can_ claim breach of contract if there was a financial penalty attached or use it as a ground for divorce if that power was delegated.
