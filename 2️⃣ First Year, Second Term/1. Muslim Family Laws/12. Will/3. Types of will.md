## 12.3 Types of Will ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (4/5):** Essential for identifying valid versus void bequests. You must understand why a "Conditional Will" in Muslim law operates differently than in English law (where conditions are often binding).
    
- **Exam (4/5):** Frequently tested. Problem questions often feature a "Death-bed gift" (_Marz-ul-Maut_) or a "Conditional Bequest" (e.g., "I give you this house but you cannot sell it") to test your knowledge of validity.
    

---


Muslim Law classifies Wills based on their form (how they are made) and their nature (conditions attached).

#### 1. Classification by Form (Oral vs. Written)

Unlike other legal systems that mandate strict documentation, Muslim Law is flexible regarding the form of a Will.

**A. Oral Will**

- **Validity:** An oral Will is **perfectly valid** under Muslim Law. No writing is required.
    
- **Requirements:**
    
    1. The intention of the testator must be clear and unequivocal.
        
    2. The burden of proof is very heavy. The exact words, time, and place must be proved with precision.
        

**B. Written Will (_Wasiyatnama_)**

- **Validity:** A written Will is valid and preferred for evidentiary purposes.
    
- **Formalities:** It does **not** strictly require a signature or attestation (witnesses) to be valid under Sharia, as long as it is written by the testator. However, for practical enforcement in Bangladesh courts, it is highly advisable to follow the **Succession Act, 1925** standards (written, signed, and attested by 2 witnesses) to avoid disputes.
    

**C. Will by Gestures**

- **The Rule:** If a person is unable to speak (e.g., dumb or struck by illness), they can make a valid Will by **clear and intelligible gestures**.
    

---

#### 2. Classification by Nature (Conditional & Contingent)

This is a technical area where Muslim Law differs significantly from general contract law.

**A. Contingent Will (Void)**

- **Definition:** A Will that is made dependent on the happening of a future uncertain event.
    
- **Example:** "If my son graduates from Oxford, I bequeath 1/3 of my estate to him."
    
- **Status:** **VOID**. The bequest takes no effect. A Will must be an unconditional transfer of ownership taking effect upon death.
    

**B. Conditional Will (The "Golden Rule")**

- **Definition:** A Will that grants property but attaches a condition that restricts the legatee's use or ownership.
    
- **Example:** "I leave this house to my friend, _on the condition that he never sells it_."
    
- **The Rule:** **The Bequest is VALID, but the Condition is VOID.**
    
    - **Result:** The friend gets the house absolutely. He _can_ sell it. The law ignores the restriction because it derogates from the completeness of the grant.
        

**C. Alternative Bequest**

- **Definition:** "I leave this to A or B."
    
- **Status:** Generally valid. The legacy goes to the person deemed most appropriate or is split, depending on interpretation.
    

---

#### 3. The Special Case: _Marz-ul-Maut_ (Will during Death Illness)

This is a hybrid concept—part Gift (_Hiba_), part Will (_Wasiyyat_). It is one of the most critical topics for exams.

**Definition:** _Marz-ul-Maut_ is a malady which induces a genuine apprehension of death in the sufferer, eventually resulting in death.

**Essential Conditions:**

1. **Proximate Danger:** The illness must be serious with a high probability of death.
    
2. **Apprehension:** There must be a subjective fear of death in the mind of the patient.
    
3. **Inability:** The patient is unable to attend to ordinary avocations (daily duties).
    
4. **Result:** Death must actually occur from that illness. (If they recover, it becomes a normal Gift/Will).
    

Legal Effect (The 1/3rd Rule applies):

Any transaction (Gift or Will) made during Marz-ul-Maut is treated as a Will.

- **Restriction:** It is valid **only up to 1/3rd** of the estate.
    
- **Heir Rule:** It cannot be made to an heir without the consent of other heirs.
    
- _Exam Tip:_ If a dying man gifts his _entire_ house to his daughter, it is valid only for **1/3rd** (if heirs consent) or might be totally invalid if heirs refuse (since she is an heir).

### Important Notes & Tips for Exams

- **Suicide and Wills:**
    
    - **Sunni Law:** A Will made by a person who commits suicide is generally **VALID**.
        
    - **Shia Law:**
        
        - If made _before_ doing any act towards suicide $\rightarrow$ **Valid**.
            
        - If made _after_ doing an act (e.g., drinking poison) $\rightarrow$ **VOID**.
            
- **The "Usufruct" Bequest:** A bequest of the _use_ of a property (e.g., "A can live in my house for free, but B owns the title") is **VALID** in Muslim Law. This is a bequest of _usufruct_, not _corpus_.
