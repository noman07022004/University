## Will (_Wasiyat_) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** A Will represents the tension between an individual's property rights and the family's communal rights. Understanding the "one-third limit" is essential to grasping the Islamic philosophy of protecting heirs from disinheritance.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Highly frequent in problem questions. Scenarios often involve a testator giving "all my property to my friend" or "one-third to my eldest son." You must apply the limits to determine the validity.
    

### Introduction

In Western legal systems, a person generally has "testamentary freedom"—the right to will away their entire fortune to a cat, a charity, or a stranger, disinheriting their children. Islamic Law strictly prohibits this. A Will (_Wasiyat_) in Islam is a "divine compromise." It allows a Muslim to bequeath a portion of their property for charitable purposes or to non-heirs (friends, distant relatives) to earn spiritual merit, but it strictly limits _how much_ and to _whom_ this can be given to protect the lawful heirs. It is defined as a transfer of ownership for no consideration that takes effect _after_ the death of the legator.

### 1. Concept and Essentials

**Subtopics Covered:** _Concept._

A Will is a legal declaration of a person's intention with respect to their property, which they desire to be carried into effect after their death.

- **The Parties:**
    
    - **The Legator (_Musi_):** The testator. Must be a sane, adult Muslim.
        
    - **The Legatee (_Musa Lahu_):** The beneficiary. Can be a non-Muslim, a minor, or a juristic person (like an institution), but must be in existence at the time of the legator's death.
        
- **Formality:**
    
    - Unlike Gifts (which now require registration in Bangladesh), a **Will can be oral or written**.
        
    - An oral will is valid if proved by two witnesses, though a written will is preferred for evidence.
        
    - No specific wording is required, as long as the intention is clear.
        

### 2. Restrictions (The "Golden Third" & The Anti-Heir Rule)

**Subtopics Covered:** _Restrictions._

This is the most critical part of the topic. A Muslim's power to make a will is restricted in two ways:

#### A. Restriction on Quantity (The 1/3 Rule)

- **The Rule:** A Muslim cannot bequeath more than **one-third (1/3)** of their _net_ estate (after paying funeral expenses and debts).
    
- **Effect of Excess:** If a person wills 50% of their property to a stranger:
    
    - The Will is valid up to **1/3**.
        
    - The remaining excess (17%) is **invalid**, _unless_ the legal heirs consent to it after the testator's death.
        

#### B. Restriction on Person (The Anti-Heir Rule)

- **The Rule (Sunni Law):** "No bequest to an heir." A bequest made to a person who is already a legal heir (e.g., son, wife, daughter) is **invalid**, even if it is within the 1/3 limit.
    
    - _Reason:_ Heirs already have fixed shares in the Quran. Giving them extra via a Will would upset the balance and cause jealousy among siblings.
        
- **Exception:** It becomes valid if **all other heirs consent** to it _after_ the death of the testator.
    

### 3. Types of Will

**Subtopics Covered:** _Types._

1. **Conditional Will:** A will made with a condition (e.g., "I give this house to X if he acts as my executor").
    
    - _Rule:_ Generally, in Islamic law, if a condition is repugnant to the grant (e.g., "I give you the house but you cannot sell it"), the condition is void, but the bequest is valid.
        
2. **Contingent Will:** A will that takes effect only if a certain event happens (e.g., "If my son dies before me, then my property goes to X").
    
    - _Rule:_ These are generally void in Islamic law.
        
3. **Future Will:** A bequest of property that does not exist yet (e.g., "The crops that will grow next year").
    
    - _Rule:_ Void. The subject matter must exist at the time of death.
        

### 4. Statutory Provisions

**Subtopics Covered:** _Statutory provisions._

Unlike Marriage and Divorce, the substantive law of Wills has **not** been significantly altered by statutes in Bangladesh (like the MFLO 1961). It largely remains pure Sharia. However, procedural statutes apply:

- **The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937:** Explicitly lists "Wills" as a subject where the rule of decision shall be Muslim Personal Law, overriding any local customs.
    
- **The Succession Act, 1925:**
    
    - Muslims are **not** required to obtain "Probate" (judicial certification of the Will) to establish their right, unlike Christians.
        
    - However, if a debt needs to be recovered using the Will, a probate or succession certificate might be procedurally necessary.
        
- **Registration Act, 1908:** Registration of a Will is **optional**, not mandatory (unlike Gifts).

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Sunni vs. Shia (The Critical Difference):**
    
    - **Sunni:** Bequest to an heir is **Invalid** (unless heirs consent).
        
    - **Shia:** Bequest to an heir is **Valid** (up to 1/3), even _without_ the consent of other heirs.
        
    - _Exam Tip:_ If the problem says "A Shia Muslim leaves 1/3 of his estate to his daughter," the will is **Valid**. If he is Sunni, it is **Invalid**.
        
- **Timing of Consent:**
    
    - In Sunni Law, heirs must consent **after the death** of the testator. Consent given _during_ the lifetime is not binding (they can change their minds).
        
- **The "Murderer" Bar:** A legatee who kills the testator is barred from taking the bequest.
    
- **Sec 4 MFLO vs. Will:** Do not confuse Section 4 of MFLO (Inheritance for orphaned grandchildren) with a "Will." Section 4 is a _statutory inheritance right_, not a bequest.
