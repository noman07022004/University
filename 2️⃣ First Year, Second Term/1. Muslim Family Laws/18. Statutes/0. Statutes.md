### Introduction

The application of Muslim Law in Bangladesh is not based solely on ancient Arabic texts. It is heavily mediated by statutes enacted during the British period, the Pakistan period, and the Bangladesh period. These statutes serve two purposes: **Codification** (writing down unwritten rules) and **Reform** (changing rules to meet social needs). The history of these statutes is a history of the shift from "Customary Law" to "Sharia Law," and then from "Traditional Sharia" to "Modern Statutory Muslim Law."

### 1. The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937

**Subtopics Covered:** _The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937._

**Historical Context:** Before 1937, British courts in India often enforced local "customs" rather than strict Islamic law. For example, in many rural communities (like the Khojas or Memons), women were excluded from inheritance based on Hindu customary practices, even though they were Muslim.

**Key Provisions:**

- **Supremacy of Sharia (Section 2):** It declared that in all questions regarding intestate succession, special property of females, marriage, divorce, dower, guardianship, family relations, trust, and waqf, the rule of decision shall be the **Muslim Personal Law (Shariat)**.
    
- **Abolition of Custom:** It effectively abrogated all local customs that were contrary to the Sharia.
    

**Significance:** This Act was the "Declaration of Independence" for Muslim Law in the subcontinent. It restored the rights of Muslim women to inherit property as per the Quran, which custom had denied them.

### 2. The Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939

**Subtopics Covered:** _The Dissolution of Muslim Marriages 1939._

**Historical Context:** Under the dominant **Hanafi school** of law, a woman had almost no right to seek a divorce from her husband, even if he was cruel or missing, unless he was impotent. This led to a crisis where Muslim women were renouncing Islam just to get their marriages annulled by British courts. To stop this and provide relief, the legislature adopted the more liberal **Maliki school** view.

**Key Provisions (Section 2):** It codified nine grounds on which a woman can sue for divorce (_Faskh_) in court:

1. Whereabouts of husband unknown for **4 years**.
    
2. Failure to provide maintenance for **2 years**.
    
3. Sentence of imprisonment for **7 years** or more.
    
4. Failure to perform marital obligations for **3 years**.
    
5. Impotence (continuing since marriage).
    
6. Insanity (2 years) or leprosy/venereal disease.
    
7. Repudiation of marriage (Option of Puberty).
    
8. **Cruelty** (physical or mental).
    
9. Any other ground recognized by Muslim Law.
    

**Significance:** This is widely regarded as one of the most progressive enactments for Muslim women's rights in the 20th century.

### 3. The Muslim Family Laws Ordinance (MFLO), 1961

**Subtopics Covered:** _The Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961._

**Historical Context:** Following the recommendations of the Marriage Commission (1955), President Ayub Khan enacted this Ordinance to reform family law in Pakistan (and by extension, Bangladesh). It is the most frequently cited statute in this course.

**Salient Features & Reforms:**

- **Section 3 (Overriding Effect):** This Ordinance overrides any other law or custom or interpretation of Muslim Law that conflicts with it.
    
- **Section 4 (Succession):** Introduced the doctrine of **Representation** for orphaned grandchildren, allowing them to inherit the share of their deceased parent (a major departure from classical Sharia).
    
- **Section 5 (Registration):** Made registration of marriages mandatory.
    
- **Section 6 (Polygamy):** Restricted polygamy by requiring the permission of an Arbitration Council.
    
- **Section 7 (Talaq):** Regulated divorce by requiring notice to the Chairman and a 90-day cooling-off period, effectively abolishing "Instant Triple Talaq."
    
- **Section 10 (Dower):** Established that if the mode of payment is unspecified, the entire dower is presumed "Prompt" (payable on demand).
    

### 4. Pita-Matar Bharan-Poshan Ain, 2013 (Parents Maintenance Act)

**Subtopics Covered:** _Pita-Matar Bharan-Poshan Ain 2013._

**Historical Context:** With the breakdown of the joint family system in modern Bangladesh, many elderly parents were being neglected by their children. To enforce the moral duty of _Ihsan_ (kindness) as a legal duty, the Bangladesh government enacted this law.

**Key Provisions:**

- **Duty to Maintain:** It is the legal duty of every child to provide maintenance (food, clothing, shelter, medical care) to their parents.
    
- **Residence:** Children must live with their parents or ensure they have suitable accommodation. They cannot force parents into "Old Age Homes" against their will.
    
- **Income Share:** Children are legally bound to set aside a portion of their income for their parents.
    
- **Penalty:** Failure to comply is a cognizable and non-bailable offense, punishable by imprisonment up to **3 months** or fine up to **1 Lakh Taka**.
    
- **Forum:** Offenses are tried by the Judicial Magistrate of the First Class. Conciliation is preferred before punishment.

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Chronology Matters:** Memorize the years: 1937 -> 1939 -> 1961 -> 1985 (Court) -> 2013. This shows the progression from "Identity" (1937) to "Women's Rights" (1939/1961) to "Procedure" (1985) to "Elderly Rights" (2013).
    
- **Section 4 MFLO Controversy:** Be aware that Section 4 (Grandchildren's inheritance) is controversial among traditional Islamic scholars because it mimics the Western concept of "representation" which contradicts the classical rule that "the nearer excludes the more remote." However, in Bangladesh, **Section 4 is the valid law** and must be applied in exams.
    
- **Statute vs. Sharia:** If a problem question presents a conflict (e.g., "Under Hanafi law X is true, but under MFLO Y is true"), **always apply the MFLO**. Section 3 explicitly says it overrides other interpretations.
