## Parentage ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** The concept of Legitimacy is foundational because, in Islamic Law, only legitimate children inherit. Illegitimacy is a severe stigma with harsh legal consequences (total exclusion from the father's family).
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** High priority. Problem questions often involve a "tug-of-war" for custody (_Hizanat_) between a mother (who may have remarried) and a father. You must balance traditional rules with the "Welfare of the Minor" principle.
    

### Introduction

In Muslim Law, Parentage (_Nasab_) establishes the legal relationship between a father and his child. It is the source of the child's right to inheritance, maintenance, and guardianship. Islamic law is fiercely protective of legitimacy and "abhors bastardy." Therefore, the law goes to great lengths to presume that a child is legitimate if there is even a shadow of a valid marriage. Unlike Western laws where adoption can create parentage, traditional Islamic law does not recognize adoption as a mode of creating legal filiation (though "guardianship" of orphans is encouraged).

### 1. Legitimacy

**Subtopics Covered:** _Legitimacy._

Legitimacy depends entirely on the existence of a valid marriage between the parents at the time of conception (or birth, depending on the rule applied).

- **Presumption of Legitimacy:**
    
    - **Traditional Sharia Rule:** A child is legitimate if born:
        
        - At least **6 months** _after_ the marriage (indicates conception after marriage).
            
        - Within **2 years** (Hanafi) or **10 months** (Shia) _after_ the dissolution of marriage (death/divorce).
            
    - **Statutory Rule (Evidence Act, 1872, Sec 112):** This statute overrides the traditional long gestation periods.
        
        - A child is conclusively presumed legitimate if born **during the valid marriage** or within **280 days** after its dissolution (provided the mother remains unmarried).
            
- **Acknowledgment (_Iqrar_):**
    
    - If direct proof of marriage is missing, parentage can be established by the father's "Acknowledgment."
        
    - **Condition:** The paternity must not be physically impossible (e.g., the age gap between "father" and "son" is too small), and the relationship must not be adulterous (_Zina_).
        
- **Illegitimacy:** An illegitimate child belongs to the mother only. They inherit from the mother but have no claim on the father’s name or property.
    

### 2. Guardianship (_Wilayat_)

**Subtopics Covered:** _Guardianship, Statutory laws of guardianship._

Guardianship is the legal authority to make decisions for a minor. It is distinct from physical custody.

- **Guardian of the Property:**
    
    - In Muslim Law, the **Father** is the natural and legal guardian of the minor's property.
        
    - **Hierarchy:** Father -> Executor appointed by Father -> Father's Father -> Executor appointed by Father's Father.
        
    - **Crucial Note:** The **Mother is NOT** the legal guardian of the property. If she manages it, she is a _de facto_ guardian and has very limited powers (she cannot sell the child's land except in dire emergency, and even then, it's risky).
        
- **Guardian of the Person:**
    
    - This refers to the right to physical care (_Hizanat_), discussed below.
        

### 3. Custodial Care (_Hizanat_)

**Subtopics Covered:** _Custodial care._

While the Father is the legal guardian (paying for the child), the **Mother** is the primary entitlement holder for physical custody (_Hizanat_) during the child's tender years.

- **The Mother's Right:**
    
    - **Son:** Entitled to custody until the age of **7 years** (Hanafi law).
        
    - **Daughter:** Entitled to custody until **Puberty** (Hanafi law).
        
- **The Father's Right:**
    
    - After these ages (7 for boys, puberty for girls), physical custody reverts to the Father, who is responsible for their education and marriage.
        
- **Disqualification of Mother:** The mother may lose custody if:
    
    1. She marries a **stranger** (a man not related to the child within prohibited degrees).
        
    2. She leads an immoral life.
        
    3. She resides at a distance where the father cannot visit the child.
        

### 4. Statutory Provisions (The "Welfare" Principle)

**Subtopics Covered:** _Statutory laws of guardianship._

In Bangladesh, the strict rules of Sharia regarding custody ages are heavily modified by the **Guardians and Wards Act, 1890**.

- **Section 17 (Welfare of the Minor):**
    
    - This is the "Golden Rule." The court must consider the **"Welfare of the Minor"** above all else.
        
- **Application:**
    
    - Even if the boy is over 7, the court may give custody to the mother if the father is unfit (e.g., drug addict, abusive).
        
    - **Leading Case:** _Abu Bakar Siddique v. S.M.A. Bakar (1986)_. The Appellate Division of Bangladesh ruled that the specific ages mentioned in Hanafi law are permissive, not mandatory. If the child's welfare is better served with the mother, she can keep custody even after the child crosses the age limit (7/puberty).

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Mother vs. Property:** A frequent exam trap is: _"Can a mother sell her minor son's land to pay for his food?"_
    
    - **Answer:** Generally, **NO**. She is not the legal guardian of property. She should apply to the court to be appointed guardian first. If she sells it without court permission, the sale is often _void_ or _voidable_.
        
- **Custody vs. Guardianship:** Distinguish clearly:
    
    - **Custody (_Hizanat_):** Physical care (feeding, bathing). Mother's domain.
        
    - **Guardianship (_Wilayat_):** Legal control (money, marriage, education decisions). Father's domain.
        
- **Remarriage Exception:** While marrying a "stranger" usually disqualifies the mother, courts often ignore this if the father is totally absent or unfit, prioritizing the _Welfare_ doctrine.
    
- **Adoption:** Remember to state that legal adoption does not exist in Sharia, but the _Guardians and Wards Act_ allows people to be appointed "Guardians" of orphans, which functions similarly in practice (except for inheritance).
