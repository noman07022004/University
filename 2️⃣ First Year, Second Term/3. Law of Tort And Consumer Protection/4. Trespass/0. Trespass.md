## 4. Trespass ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Trespass is the oldest form of tort and is crucial for understanding the protection of civil liberties (person) and property rights. It introduces the concept of liability _without_ proof of actual damage (_actionable per se_).
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** "Trespass to Person" (Battery/Assault/False Imprisonment) is a frequent exam topic, often appearing as problem scenarios involving police arrests or security guards. "Trespass to Land" is also common in property-related disputes.
    

---

### Introduction: Definition and Nature

**Trespass** is defined as a direct and intentional interference with the person, land, or goods of another.

The defining characteristic of Trespass that distinguishes it from Negligence is that it is **Actionable _Per Se_**. This means the plaintiff does not need to prove they suffered any actual financial or physical damage. The mere act of interference (e.g., walking on someone's lawn, touching someone without consent) is enough to establish liability because the _legal right_ itself has been violated.

---

### 1. Forms of Trespass

The syllabus lists **Forms of trespass**. Academically, these are categorized into three distinct types:

#### A. Trespass to the Person

This protects personal integrity and freedom. It has three main sub-forms:

1. **Battery:** The intentional and direct application of force to another person without lawful justification.
    
    - _Key:_ There is no minimum threshold for "force." Even touching someone's arm unwantedly can be battery. Hostility is usually required.
        
    - _Example:_ Throwing water on someone.
        
2. **Assault:** An act which causes another person to apprehend the infliction of immediate, unlawful force.
    
    - _Key:_ Assault is the _fear_ of being hit; Battery is the _hit_. Pointing a gun is an assault; shooting it is a battery.
        
3. **False Imprisonment:** The infliction of bodily restraint which is not expressly authorized by law.
    
    - _Key:_ The restraint must be **total**. If there is a reasonable means of escape, it is not false imprisonment (_Bird v Jones_).
        

#### B. Trespass to Land

This constitutes an unjustifiable interference with the possession of land.

- **Vertical Extent:** The maxim _Cuius est solum, eius est usque ad coelum et ad inferos_ applies (whoever owns the soil, it is theirs up to heaven and down to hell). However, modern law limits this (e.g., airplanes flying at a reasonable height are not trespassers).
    
- **Trespass Ab Initio:** If a person enters land with lawful authority (e.g., a police officer) but then abuses that authority, they are treated as a trespasser _from the beginning_ (retroactively).
    

#### C. Trespass to Goods

This involves wrongful interference with goods (chattels) in the possession of another.

- It includes touching, moving, or damaging goods.
    
- **Conversion:** A more serious form where the defendant deals with goods in a way that is inconsistent with the rights of the owner (e.g., selling someone else's car).
    

---

### 2. Title of Claimant

A critical, often misunderstood concept is the **Title of claimant**. Who can sue for trespass?

- **Possession vs. Ownership:** Trespass is a wrong against _possession_, not _ownership_.
    
- **The Rule:** To sue for trespass to land or goods, the claimant must have **immediate possession** at the time of the trespass.
    
    - _Example:_ If a landlord rents a house to a tenant, and a stranger trespasses, the **tenant** (who has possession) is the proper person to sue, not the landlord (who only has ownership). The landlord can only sue if there is permanent damage to the property structure (reversionary interest).
        

---

### 3. Defenses to Trespass

A defendant can use specific **Defenses to trespass** to avoid liability:

1. **Consent (Volenti):** If the claimant agreed to the act (e.g., a patient consenting to surgery, a boxer consenting to a fight), there is no trespass.
    
2. **Self-Defense / Defense of Property:** Reasonable force may be used to defend oneself or one's land/goods. The force must be _proportional_ to the threat.
    
3. **Necessity:** Trespass is justified if committed to prevent a greater evil (e.g., breaking into a burning house to save a child).
    
4. **Lawful Authority:** Police and other officials often have statutory powers to enter land or arrest persons.
    
5. **Inevitable Accident:** If the interference was involuntary and unavoidable (e.g., A is pushed onto B's land by C; A is not liable, C is).
    

---

### 4. Remedies for Trespass

The **Remedies for trespass** generally include:

1. **Damages:**
    
    - _Nominal Damages:_ Awarded to recognize the violation of the right, even if no harm occurred.
        
    - _Aggravated Damages:_ Awarded if the trespass caused injury to dignity or feelings.
        
2. **Injunction:** A court order to stop a continuing trespass (e.g., forcing a neighbor to remove a wall built on your land).
    
3. **Self-Help (Extra-judicial remedy):**
    
    - _Ejection of Trespasser:_ A landowner may use "reasonable force" to eject a trespasser, but this is risky; excessive force makes the landowner liable.
        

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Case Law Essential:** For Trespass to Land and the concept of "Trespass Ab Initio," memorize the **Six Carpenters Case (1610)**. It established that abusing legal authority makes the initial entry illegal.
    
- **Distinction:** In exams, clearly distinguish between **Assault** (creating fear) and **Battery** (physical contact). In common language, we use "Assault" for physical attacks, but in Tort law, "Assault" is purely psychological (the apprehension).
    
- **Police Powers:** Many problem questions involve wrongful arrest. If the police fail to follow proper procedure (e.g., failing to inform the suspect of the grounds for arrest), the arrest becomes **False Imprisonment**.
