>**Rating Justification:**

>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Examiners frequently test these foundational principles either through theoretical essays (e.g., "Is there a Law of Tort or Law of Torts?") or by requiring you to apply general defenses and remedies in problem scenarios.

### Introduction

The "Law of Tort" is a branch of civil law that deals with civil wrongs. Derived from the Latin term _tortum_, meaning "to twist," a tort represents conduct that is twisted or crooked—a deviation from right conduct. Unlike criminal law, which aims to punish the offender, the primary objective of tort law is to compensate the victim for the harm suffered. It governs the rules of liability for non-contractual harms, meaning the parties do not need a prior agreement to sue one another. In essence, it enforces a general duty of care we all owe to our neighbors not to cause them harm.

---

### 1. Conceptual Framework and Objectives

To understand the **Concept of Tort**, one must view it as an instrument of social engineering. It shifts the burden of loss from the victim to the wrongdoer (tortfeasor).

The **Objectives** of tort law are multifaceted:

- **Compensation:** The primary goal is to return the victim to the position they were in before the tort occurred (restitutio in integrum) through monetary damages.
    
- **Deterrence:** By imposing liability, the law discourages individuals and companies from engaging in risky or harmful behavior.
    
- **Protection of Interest:** Tort law recognizes and protects specific interests, such as:
    
    - _Personal Security:_ Freedom from physical harm (protected by Battery, Assault).
        
    - _Property:_ Freedom from interference with land or goods (protected by Trespass, Nuisance).
        
    - _Reputation:_ Protected by Defamation.
        
    - _Economic Interests:_ Protected by torts like Deceit or Inducing Breach of Contract.
        

**Knowledge Gap Fill (The "Pigeon-hole" Theory):** Academic debate often centers on whether there is a "Law of Tort" (a general principle of liability for any harm) or a "Law of Torts" (a specific set of named wrongs like "pigeon-holes").

- _Winfield’s Theory:_ All unjustified harm is tortious (Law of Tort).
    
- _Salmond’s Theory:_ Liability only exists if the harm fits into a specific category (Law of Torts).

### 2. Elements of Tort Liability

For a successful action in tort, the plaintiff generally must prove three essential **Elements**:

1. **A Wrongful Act or Omission:** The defendant must have done something the law prohibits or failed to do something the law requires.
    
2. **Legal Damage (_Injuria_):** There must be a violation of a legal right.
    
3. **Legal Remedy:** The wrongful act must fall under a category where the law provides a remedy.
    

#### The Distinction between _Damnum_ and _Injuria_

This is critical for the "Elements" subtopic.

- **Injuria sine damno:** Violation of a legal right without actual physical/monetary damage. This **is actionable** (e.g., trespassing on land without breaking anything).
    
    - _Case Ref:_ _Ashby v. White_ (Voting rights violation).
        
- **Damnum sine injuria:** Actual damage without the violation of a legal right. This **is not actionable** (e.g., a competitor opens a shop nearby and causes you to lose profit).
    
    - _Case Ref:_ _Gloucester Grammar School Case_.
        

---

### 3. Variations of Tort

Tort liability is not uniform; it comes in different **Variations** based on the mental state of the wrongder:

1. **Intentional Torts:** The defendant intended the consequences of their act (e.g., Assault, Battery, False Imprisonment).
    
2. **Negligence:** The defendant caused harm through carelessness or a breach of duty, without specific intent to harm.
    
3. **Strict Liability:** The defendant is liable regardless of intent or negligence (e.g., _Rylands v. Fletcher_ rule regarding dangerous substances).
    

---

### 4. Legal Mechanisms: Defenses, Remedies, and Limitation

Once a tort is established, the narrative moves to how a defendant can escape liability or what the claimant receives.

#### General Defenses

Even if the elements are met, a defendant may avoid liability using these defenses:

- **Volenti non fit injuria:** Consent to the risk of harm (e.g., a boxer consents to being hit).
    
- **Act of God (Vis Major):** Natural events completely beyond human foresight and control (e.g., a sudden, unprecedented storm).
    
- **Inevitable Accident:** Harm that could not be avoided despite reasonable care.
    
- **Necessity:** Committing a smaller harm to prevent a greater harm.
    
- **Statutory Authority:** The act was authorized by a specific law (e.g., a railway company causing noise vibration).
    

#### Remedies

If the plaintiff wins, the court grants remedies:

1. **Damages (Unliquidated):** The court calculates the monetary compensation based on the harm suffered. This distinguishes tort from contract (where damages are often liquidated/pre-agreed).
    
    - _Nominal Damages:_ For rights violations without loss.
        
    - _Compensatory Damages:_ To cover actual loss.
        
    - _Exemplary/Punitive Damages:_ To punish outrageous conduct.
        
2. **Injunction:** A court order to stop a continuing tort (e.g., stopping a noisy factory).
    
3. **Self-Help:** Actions the victim takes themselves (e.g., ejecting a trespasser), though this is restricted by law.
    

#### Limitation

This refers to the _time limit_ within which a lawsuit must be filed. In Bangladesh, the **Limitation Act, 1908** governs this.

- For example, a suit for compensation for a false imprisonment must usually be filed within **one year**. If the victim waits too long, the claim is time-barred.

### 5. Boundaries of Tort in Bangladesh

This subtopic requires analyzing the practical application of tort law in the local jurisdiction.

- **Underdevelopment:** Tort law is largely undeveloped in Bangladesh compared to the UK or USA. Most cases that could be torts are filed as criminal cases (under the Penal Code) or writ petitions (Constitutional Tort).
    
- **Reasons:** High court fees, delays in civil litigation, lack of awareness, and the tendency to seek punishment (criminal law) rather than compensation.
    
- **Constitutional Tort:** Interestingly, the "boundaries" are expanding through public interest litigation (PIL) under Article 102 of the Constitution, where the High Court Division grants compensation for state negligence (e.g., custodial deaths or medical negligence in state hospitals).

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Tip for Exams:** When defining Tort, memorize the "Unliquidated Damages" distinction. A breach of contract usually leads to liquidated (pre-determined) damages, whereas a tort leads to unliquidated (court-determined) damages.
    
- **Case Law Strategy:** You cannot pass a Tort exam without case law. For "General Principles," memorize _Ashby v. White_ (Legal Right) and _Donoghue v Stevenson_ (Neighbor Principle) as your primary weapons.
    
- **Key Concept:** "Vicarious Liability" is often confused with General Principles. Remember, General Principles deal with the _wrongdoer's_ liability; Vicarious Liability (Topic 2) deals with _someone else's_ liability for that wrongdoer.
