### 📌 Remedies ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

> **Why Important**: The pursuit of a remedy is the entire purpose of a tort claim. This topic details the legal relief a successful plaintiff can obtain. A thorough understanding of the types of damages and other remedies is essential for analyzing the consequences of a tort and is a core component of any legal examination. 🏆

When a tort is committed, the law provides the injured party with a remedy or relief. These remedies can be broadly divided into two categories: **judicial remedies** (those granted by a court of law) and **extra-judicial remedies** (those available to a party through self-help, without court intervention).

---

### 🟢 Judicial Remedies

These are the most common remedies, obtained by filing a lawsuit in court.

#### 1️⃣ Damages

Damages are the primary remedy in the law of tort. It is a monetary award paid by the defendant to the plaintiff as compensation for the harm suffered. The main types of damages are:

##### 1. Compensatory, Substantial or Ordinary Damages: 
This is the most common form. The objective is to place the injured party in the same position they would have been in had the tort not been committed (_restitutio in integrum_). They cover both:
    
- **Pecuniary losses**: Quantifiable financial losses like medical bills, lost wages, and property repair costs.
	
- **Non-pecuniary losses**: Non-financial losses that are harder to quantify, such as pain and suffering, emotional distress, and loss of amenity (loss of the ability to enjoy life).

 ###### Example: 
 A speeding car hits a CNG auto-rickshaw. The CNG driver's leg is broken. The court orders the car driver to pay compensation. This will include:

- **Pecuniary loss**: Money to cover the hospital bills, the cost of repairing the CNG, and the income the driver lost while he was unable to work.
    
- **Non-pecuniary loss**: An additional amount for the pain and suffering he endured because of the broken leg.
##### 2️. Nominal Damages: 
A small, token amount of money awarded when the plaintiff's legal right has been infringed, but they have suffered no actual financial loss (_injuria sine damno_). The purpose is not to compensate, but to **vindicate the plaintiff's right**. The decision in **_Ashby v White_** is a classic example where nominal damages would be appropriate.

**Example**: Your neighbor takes a shortcut by walking across your front lawn. He doesn't damage anything, but he has technically trespassed. You sue him. The court agrees your right was violated but recognizes you suffered no real loss. The court might award you a symbolic **100 Taka** just to officially state that your neighbor was wrong.
    
##### 3️. Contemptuous Damages: 
A minimal, derisory sum (e.g., the smallest coin of the realm) awarded when the court recognizes that the plaintiff's rights were technically violated but believes the claim was trivial and brought on frivolous grounds. It signals the court's disapproval of the plaintiff's conduct in bringing the case.

**Example**: Two people have a petty argument, and one lightly shoves the other. The person who was shoved sues for battery. The court finds that while it was technically a battery, the incident was so trivial and the lawsuit was so unnecessary that it was a waste of the court's time. The judge awards the claimant **one Taka** to show the court's disapproval of the lawsuit.
    
##### 4️. Exemplary, Vindictive or Punitive Damages: 
These are awarded in addition to compensatory damages. Their purpose is not to compensate the plaintiff but to **punish** the defendant for outrageous, oppressive, or malicious conduct and to **deter** similar conduct in the future. They are awarded only in exceptional circumstances.

**Example**: A large factory knowingly dumps toxic chemicals into a river to save money, even after being warned that it is making people in a nearby village sick. The villagers sue. The court will order the factory to pay compensatory damages for their medical bills. In addition, the court may order the factory to pay a very large extra sum as **punishment** for its outrageous and deliberate disregard for public health, and to deter other factories from doing the same.
    

#### 2️⃣ Injunction

An injunction is a court order that requires a person to either do a specific act or, more commonly, to refrain from doing a specific act. It is an equitable remedy granted at the discretion of the court.

##### 1. Prohibitory Injunction: 
Orders a defendant to stop doing something (e.g., stop committing a nuisance like playing loud music late at night).

**Example**: Your neighbor starts a workshop in his garage and the noisy machinery runs late every night, making it impossible for your family to sleep. You sue for nuisance. The court issues a **prohibitory injunction**, ordering your neighbor to stop operating the machinery between 9 PM and 8 AM.
    
##### 2. Mandatory Injunction: 
Orders a defendant to perform a positive act (e.g., to demolish a structure built in violation of the plaintiff's rights).

**Example**: Your neighbor builds a new wall that extends one foot onto your property. You sue for trespass. The court issues a **mandatory injunction**, ordering your neighbor to demolish the part of the wall that is on your land.
    
##### 3. Temporary (Interlocutory) and Perpetual Injunction: 
A temporary injunction is granted for a specific period or until the case is decided, to maintain the status quo. A perpetual injunction is granted after the final hearing of the case.

**Example**: A magazine plans to publish a private photo of you. You find out before it goes to print. You go to court and get a **temporary injunction** to stop the publication until a full trial can be held. After the trial, if you win, the court will issue a **perpetual injunction**, permanently forbidding the magazine from ever publishing that photo.
    

#### 3️⃣ Specific Restitution of Property

This is a remedy where the court orders the defendant to return specific property to the plaintiff who was wrongfully dispossessed. This applies to both movable property (goods) and immovable property (land).

**Example**: You lend a unique, antique piece of family jewelry to a friend. The friend later refuses to give it back. Money is not an adequate substitute because the item has special sentimental value. You sue, and the court orders the friend to return the **specific piece of jewelry** to you.

### 🟢 Extra-Judicial Remedies (Self-Help)

These are remedies that an aggrieved person can take without recourse to a court. The law allows for self-help in limited situations, and it must always be exercised reasonably.

##### 1️⃣ Self-Help/Defense: 
A person may use reasonable and proportionate force to protect themselves or another person from an imminent threat.

**Example**: Someone tries to snatch your wallet on the street. You are legally allowed to use reasonable force, like pushing the person away, to protect yourself and your property.
    
##### 2️⃣ Re-caption of Goods: 
A person who has been wrongfully deprived of their goods may lawfully retake them.

**Example**: Your bicycle is stolen. The next day, you see it parked unlocked outside a local shop. You are entitled to walk over and take your bicycle back.

##### 3️⃣ Re-entry on Land: 
A person wrongfully dispossessed of land may, in certain circumstances, re-enter it, provided they do so in a peaceable manner.

**Example**: You own a small, empty plot of land. While you are away, a stranger puts up a temporary fence and starts using it. You have the right to peacefully go onto your land and remove the fence yourself.
    
##### 4️⃣ Abatement of Nuisance: 
An individual may take reasonable steps to remove a nuisance. For example, one can trim tree branches that overhang from a neighbour's property and encroach upon their land.

**Example**: Your neighbor’s tree has a branch that is growing over your property and blocking sunlight to your garden. You are allowed to cut the branch yourself, but only the part that is on your side of the property line.

##### 5️⃣ Distress damage feasant: 
*What the Name Means*
The term is old Law French:
- **Distress**: This means to seize or detain someone's property.
- **Damage Feasant**: This means "doing damage."
So, the whole phrase literally means **"seizing something that is doing damage."** 🐮

If somebody's household animals get into the courtyard of other and cause some damage, the person suffering such damage may detain the animals and this right is not limited to animals alone. Cycles, motor vehicles etc. may also come under the same principle. If an unauthorised engine is used in the rail-line of a rail company, in can detain it for realisation of damages and the court has supported the action so taken.

**Example**: Your neighbor's goats repeatedly enter your vegetable garden and eat your plants. You have the right to peacefully capture the goats and keep them safely enclosed on your property until your neighbor pays you for the damage they caused to your vegetables.

In **Bangladesh**, all these remedies are recognized. **Damages** are the most common relief sought. The law relating to **injunctions** and **specific restitution** is primarily governed by the **Specific Relief Act, 1877**.
