### Introduction

This section steps back from specific rules to analyze the **social and political context** of Tort Law. In developed nations, Tort is a massive industry ("Compensation Culture"). In Bangladesh, traditional Tort litigation is almost non-existent due to delays and costs. Consequently, Bangladeshi jurisprudence has had to invent new theoretical pathways—primarily **Constitutional Tort**—to provide justice where traditional Civil Courts have failed.

---

### 1. Tort as a Compensation Culture

The concept of **Tort as a compensation culture** acts as a critique of modern tort law, particularly in the UK and USA.

- **Definition:** It refers to a perceived "blame culture" where citizens are eager to sue for every minor misfortune, believing that "where there is blame, there is a claim".
    
- **The Criticism:** Critics argue that this culture leads to:
    
    - **Defensive Practice:** Doctors or schools stopping beneficial activities (like school trips) for fear of being sued.
        
    - **Fraudulent Claims:** An increase in staged accidents or exaggerated injuries.
        
- **The Reality:** Academic research often suggests this is a **myth** created by the media. In reality, proving negligence is difficult and expensive, and strict liability is rare.
    

---

### 2. Compensation Statute in Bangladesh: Necessity and Feasibility

Given that the "Civil Suit for Damages" takes 10-20 years in Bangladesh, scholars debate the **Necessity and feasibility of a compensation statute**.

- **Necessity:**
    
    - **Victim Support:** Currently, victims of road accidents or crimes rarely receive financial support. A specific statute could create a "Victim Compensation Fund".
        
    - **Bypassing Delay:** A statute could establish special tribunals (like the Motor Accidents Claim Tribunal in India) to award compensation quickly, bypassing the clogged Civil Courts.
        
- **Feasibility:**
    
    - While feasible, implementation faces hurdles: lack of insurance infrastructure and the state's reluctance to accept financial liability for its failures. However, recent proposals for a "Crime Victims Compensation Act" show legislative interest.
        

---

### 3. Negligence and Malpractice: Medical Liability in Bangladesh

**Medical malpractice and liability in Bangladesh** is a contentious area where law meets ethics.

- **The Legal Standard (The _Bolam_ Test):** In Bangladesh, a doctor is not negligent if they acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical men. This makes it very hard to sue doctors.
    
- **Current Avenues for Redress:**
    
    1. **BMDC Act 2010:** The Bangladesh Medical and Dental Council can suspend a doctor's license for professional misconduct, but this does not compensate the victim.
        
    2. **Consumer Protection Act:** Increasingly, patients are suing hospitals as "service providers" in Consumer Courts to get quicker remedies than Civil Courts.
        
    3. **Constitutional Remedy:** Due to the failure of other mechanisms, the High Court has started intervening in gross negligence cases (e.g., _CCB Foundation Case_).
        

---

### 4. Constitutional Tort in Bangladesh

This is the most dynamic area of Bangladeshi law. **Constitutional Tort** refers to the High Court awarding monetary compensation under **Article 102** of the Constitution for the violation of Fundamental Rights (like Right to Life).

- **The Shift:** Traditionally, Writ Petitions (Article 102) only granted "injunctions" or "declarations" (e.g., declaring an arrest illegal). Now, courts are awarding **Public Law Compensation**.
    
- **Key Case - _Bangladesh Beverage Industries v Rowshan Akhter_:** The Appellate Division upheld a massive compensation award for a death caused by a negligent state vehicle, effectively bypassing the need for a separate civil suit.
    
- **Key Case - _CCB Foundation v Govt of Bangladesh_:** The High Court awarded 20 Lac Taka compensation for a child killed due to the negligence of Railway and Fire Service authorities, applying the tort principle of _Res Ipsa Loquitur_ within a Constitutional Writ.
    
- **Rationale:** The State cannot hide behind "Sovereign Immunity" when it violates the Right to Life (Article 32).

### 5. Feminist Perspectives

**Feminist perspectives** critique Tort Law as being inherently male-centric.

- **The "Reasonable Man":** Feminists argue the "Reasonable Man" standard ignores the lived experiences of women. It values "economic loss" (traditionally male earning power) higher than "care-giving work" or "emotional distress" (traditionally female roles).
    
- **Sexual Disclosure and Privacy Law:**
    
    - Feminist scholars like Janice Richardson argue that privacy law often fails to protect women from "revenge porn" or the non-consensual disclosure of sexual history.
        
    - They advocate for a Tort of Privacy that recognizes the _dignity_ harm of such exposures, rather than just treating it as a breach of confidence or property.

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Essay Power-Move:** If asked about "Tort in Bangladesh," argue that **"Constitutional Tort is the new Tort Law."** Explain that because Civil Courts are too slow, the High Court's Writ Jurisdiction is effectively becoming the primary venue for major tort claims (like custodial death or medical negligence).
    
- **Case Name to Memorize:** ** _Bangladesh Beverage Industries Ltd v Rowshan Akhter_ (2010)**. This is the _Donoghue v Stevenson_ of Bangladesh. It established that the High Court _can_ award compensation for tortious acts.
    
- **Exam Strategy:** For "Medical Negligence," mention that while the _Bolam_ test protects doctors, the _Consumer Rights Protection Act 2009_ is increasingly used because it is faster and more consumer-friendly.
