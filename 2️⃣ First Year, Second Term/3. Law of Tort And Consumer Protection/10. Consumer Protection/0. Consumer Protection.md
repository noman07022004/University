## 10. Consumer Protection ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This topic marks a shift from Common Law Tort (judge-made law) to Statutory Law (parliament-made law). It is essential for understanding how modern Bangladesh regulates the market to protect weaker parties.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Questions here are usually direct and statute-based. You are often asked to list "Consumer Rights" or explain the "Institutional Framework" (Council/Directorate) under the 2009 Act.
    

---

### Introduction

**Consumer Protection** is a socio-legal movement that seeks to protect the rights of consumers in the marketplace. Historically, the law was governed by the maxim _Caveat Emptor_ ("Let the buyer beware"), which assumed buyers and sellers were on equal footing.

However, in the modern industrial age, consumers often lack the technical knowledge to judge products (e.g., ingredients in food or safety of electronics). Therefore, the legal philosophy has shifted to _Caveat Venditor_ ("Let the seller beware"). In Bangladesh, this is primarily governed by the **Consumer Rights Protection Act, 2009 (CRPA)**.

---

### 1. Effect of Laissez Faire

To understand the need for this law, one must understand the **Effect of laissez faire**.

- **The Concept:** _Laissez-faire_ (French for "let do" or "leave alone") is an economic theory advocating minimal government intervention in trade.
    
- **The Problem:** Under strict laissez-faire, the state would not interfere if a seller sold low-quality goods, assuming the "invisible hand" of the market would eventually punish bad sellers (people would stop buying).
    
- **The Reality:** This doctrine failed to protect consumers from monopolies, adulteration, and dangerous products. The "Effect" was widespread exploitation. Consumer Protection laws are the direct _rejection_ of laissez-faire, mandating state intervention to ensure fairness.
    

---

### 2. Definitions: Who is a Consumer?

The application of the law depends entirely on **Definitions**, specifically who qualifies as a "Consumer." Under Section 2(19) of the CRPA 2009:

A **Consumer** is a person who:

1. **Buys Goods:** Purchases any product for a price (paid or promised) for _consumption_ or _use_.
    
2. **Hires Services:** Hires any service (e.g., medical, transport, telecom) for a price.
    

**Crucial Exception (The "Commercial Purpose" Rule):** A person is **NOT** a consumer if they purchase goods for **resale** or for **commercial purposes**.

- _Example:_ If you buy a laptop for your home, you are a consumer. If a shopkeeper buys 10 laptops to sell in their shop, they are _not_ a consumer under this Act (they are a trader).
    

---

### 3. Rights of a Consumer

The **Rights of a consumer** are universally recognized (inspired by President JFK's Consumer Bill of Rights) and codified in Bangladesh. They generally include:

1. **Right to Safety:** Protection against goods/services hazardous to life and property (e.g., exploding batteries, adulterated food).
    
2. **Right to Information:** The right to be informed about the quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard, and price of goods to protect against unfair trade practices.
    
3. **Right to Choose:** Assurance of access to a variety of goods at competitive prices.
    
4. **Right to be Heard:** Representation of consumer interests in policy-making bodies (via the Consumer Council).
    
5. **Right to Redress:** The right to receive compensation for misrepresentation or shoddy goods.

### 4. Institutional Framework: The Consumer Council

The Act establishes a dual structure to enforce these rights, centered on the **Consumer council**:

1. **National Consumer Rights Protection Council (The Council):**
    
    - **Role:** This is the _policy-making_ body. It does not hear individual cases.
        
    - **Function:** It advises the government on consumer policy, creates awareness, and monitors the market situation.
        
    - **Composition:** It is a high-powered body including ministers, bureaucrats, and consumer representatives.
        
2. **Directorate of National Consumer Rights Protection (DNCRP):**
    
    - **Role:** This is the _executive_ body that enforces the law.
        
    - **Function:** They conduct market raids, fine non-compliant shops, and handle consumer complaints.
        

---

### 5. Areas of Consumer Protection Laws

The **Areas of consumer protection laws** in Bangladesh cover specific "anti-consumer rights practices" that are punishable crimes (Sections 37-53 of CRPA 2009):

1. **Adulteration:** Mixing dangerous or inferior substances into food or medicine.
    
2. **Hoarding:** Creating an artificial crisis by stockpiling goods to drive up prices.
    
3. **Deceptive Pricing:** Selling goods at a higher price than the fixed price or the price tag/MRP.
    
4. **False Advertising:** Making false claims about the benefits or quality of a product.
    
5. **Weight Manipulation:** Using false weights or measures to give the customer less than they paid for.
    
6. **No Price List:** Failure to display a price list in the shop is a punishable offense.

### Important Notes & Tips

- **The "25% Rule" (Exam Gold):** This is unique to Bangladesh law. If a consumer files a complaint with the DNCRP (Directorate) and the allegation is proven true, the shop is fined. The complainant (consumer) receives **25% of that fine** as an incentive. This is a very common exam MCQ or short note.
    
- **Limitation Period:** A complaint must be filed within **30 days** of the cause of action arising. This is a very short window compared to Tort (years).
    
- **Service Inclusion:** Remember that "Services" (Doctors, WASA, DESCO) are included. You can file a consumer complaint against a hospital for negligence under this Act, which is often faster than a civil tort suit.
