## 3. Negligence ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Negligence is the most dominant tort in modern legal systems. It forms the structural backbone for most accident and personal injury law.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is virtually guaranteed to appear in exams. You will likely face a complex problem question requiring you to walk through the "Duty $\rightarrow$ Breach $\rightarrow$ Causation $\rightarrow$ Damage" analysis.
    

---

### Introduction: General Principles

**Negligence** is not merely "carelessness" in the abstract; it is a specific legal tort consisting of a breach of a legal duty to take care, resulting in unintended damage to the claimant.

To succeed in a negligence claim, the plaintiff must prove four core elements, often visualized as a chain:

1. **Duty of Care:** The defendant owed a legal duty to the claimant.
    
2. **Breach of Duty:** The defendant failed to meet the required standard of care.
    
3. **Causation:** The breach factually and legally caused the harm.
    
4. **Damage:** The claimant suffered actual loss or injury (negligence is not actionable _per se_).
    

---

### 1. The Duty of Care

The first hurdle is establishing that a **Duty of care** exists.

- **The Neighbor Principle:** Established in the landmark case _Donoghue v Stevenson_ (1932) (the "snail in the ginger beer" case). Lord Atkin stated: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbor."
    
- **The Caparo Test:** In modern law (_Caparo Industries plc v Dickman_), a three-stage test determines if a duty exists:
    
    1. **Foreseeability:** Was damage reasonably foreseeable?
        
    2. **Proximity:** Was there a close relationship between the parties?
        
    3. **Fair, Just, and Reasonable:** Is it fair to impose a duty in this situation?
        

#### Complex Duty Scenarios

The syllabus highlights specific areas where establishing a duty is difficult:

- **Omission:**
    
    - _General Rule:_ There is no general duty to act or "rescue." A bystander can watch a child drown in a shallow pond and not be liable in tort (though morally repugnant).
        
    - _Exceptions:_ A duty to act exists if there is a special relationship (e.g., Parent-Child, Lifeguard-Swimmer) or if the defendant created the danger.
        
- **Economic Loss:**
    
    - _Consequential Economic Loss:_ Financial loss resulting from physical injury (e.g., lost wages after a broken leg) **is recoverable**.
        
    - _Pure Economic Loss:_ Financial loss _without_ physical injury (e.g., a factory shuts down because a power cable was cut, losing profit but suffering no physical damage) is generally **not recoverable** in negligence (Policy: "Floodgates argument").
        
- **Third Parties and Public Authorities:**
    
    - **Third Parties:** Generally, you are not liable for the acts of third parties unless you had a high degree of control over them (e.g., a prison letting a dangerous inmate escape).
        
    - **Public Authorities:** Courts are often reluctant to impose duties on police or local councils for policy reasons, fearing it would lead to defensive practices. However, they may be liable if they "assumed responsibility" for a specific individual.
        

---

### 2. Breach of Duty

Once a duty is established, the plaintiff must prove **Breach of duty**.

- **The Reasonable Man Test:** The defendant is judged not by their own competence, but by the objective standard of the "reasonable man" (_Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks_).
    
- **Factors considered:**
    
    - _Magnitude of Risk:_ Higher risk requires greater care.
        
    - _Social Utility:_ Emergency services taking risks to save lives may be excused.
        
    - _Cost of Precautions:_ The court balances the risk against the cost of eliminating it.
        
- **Medical Negligence:**
    
    - Doctors are not judged by the "reasonable man" standard but by the standard of their professional peers.
        
    - **The Bolam Test:** A doctor is not negligent if they act in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical men skilled in that particular art.
        
- **Proof of Negligence:**
    
    - The burden of proof usually lies on the claimant.
        
    - **Res Ipsa Loquitur:** "The thing speaks for itself." In some cases (e.g., a barrel falls from a warehouse window), the accident could not have happened without negligence. This doctrine shifts the burden to the defendant to prove they were _not_ negligent.
        

---

### 3. Causation and Remoteness

Even if the defendant was negligent, they are not liable unless that negligence caused the damage. This falls under **Causation and remoteness of damage**.

1. **Factual Causation (The "But For" Test):** _But for_ the defendant's breach, would the damage have occurred?
    
    - _Example:_ In _Barnett v Chelsea Hospital_, a doctor negligently sent a patient home. The patient died of arsenic poisoning. Evidence showed the patient would have died _anyway_ even with treatment. Thus, "but for" failed; the doctor was not liable for the death.
        
2. **Legal Causation (Remoteness):** The damage must not be too remote.
    
    - **The Wagon Mound Test:** The defendant is only liable for damage that was **reasonably foreseeable**. If the damage is of a completely different "kind" than expected, it is too remote.
        
    - _Eggshell Skull Rule:_ "You take your victim as you find them." If the victim has a pre-existing condition (e.g., a thin skull) that makes the injury worse, the defendant is liable for the full extent of the injury, even if it wasn't foreseeable.
        

---

### 4. Defense to Negligence

Finally, the defendant may plead a **Defense to negligence** to reduce or eliminate liability:

- **Contributory Negligence:** The claimant was also careless (e.g., not wearing a seatbelt). This is a _partial defense_. The court reduces damages by a percentage (e.g., 25% reduction).
    
- **Volenti non fit injuria (Consent):** The claimant voluntarily accepted the specific risk. This is a _complete defense_.
    
- **Illegality (_Ex turpi causa_):** If the claimant was committing a crime when injured, they may be denied compensation.
    

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Exam Strategy:** In problem questions, **never** skip the "Duty" stage. Even if it seems obvious (like a driver hitting a pedestrian), you must briefly state: "A duty of care exists between road users (_Nethermere (St Neots) Ltd v Gardiner_)."
    
- **Medical Negligence Note:** While the _Bolam_ test protects doctors, the newer _Bolitho_ gloss allows the court to reject medical opinion if it is not "logical." This is crucial for modern analysis.
    
- **Terminology:** Distinguish between _Novus Actus Interveniens_ (a new intervening act that breaks the chain of causation) and standard causation. If a third party does something totally unpredictable after the defendant's negligence, the defendant might not be liable for the new damage.
