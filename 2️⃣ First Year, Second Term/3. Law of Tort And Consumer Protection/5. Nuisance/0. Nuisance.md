## 5. Nuisance ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Nuisance is the primary tort for resolving neighbor disputes and environmental issues (noise, smell, pollution). It requires a nuanced understanding of "reasonableness," balancing one person's right to do what they like on their land against another's right to enjoy theirs.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is a staple exam topic. Examiners love it because it tests your ability to weigh competing interests (e.g., "Is the factory's noise reasonable in an industrial zone?"). Distinguishing between Public and Private Nuisance is also a common short-question format.
    

---

### Introduction: Definition and Concept

**Nuisance** is derived from the French word _nuire_ (to hurt or annoy). In law, it refers to an unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or with some right over, or in connection with it.

The guiding maxim is _Sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas_ ("So use your own property as not to injure your neighbor's"). unlike Trespass, which is actionable _per se_ and requires direct entry, Nuisance is usually indirect (e.g., smoke drifting over a fence) and generally requires proof of damage.

---

### 1. Categories of Nuisance

Nuisance is legally divided into two distinct categories, which are effectively different torts:

#### A. Public Nuisance

This is primarily a **crime**, but it can be a tort under specific circumstances.

- **Definition:** An act or omission which materially affects the reasonable comfort and convenience of a class of Her Majesty's subjects (the public). Examples include obstructing a highway, selling contaminated food, or keeping a brothel.
    
- **When is it a Tort?** An individual can only sue for Public Nuisance if they can prove they suffered **"Special Damage"**—damage that is distinct and more severe than that suffered by the general public.
    
    - _Example:_ If a truck blocks a road, the general public suffers inconvenience (delay). If a shopkeeper on that road loses all their customers for the day because of the blockage, that is "special damage," and they can sue.
        

#### B. Private Nuisance

This is the classic tort between neighbors.

- **Definition:** Unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or some right over or in connection with it.
    
- **Who can sue?** Only a person with an interest in the land (e.g., owner or tenant). A guest or family member without a proprietary interest generally cannot sue (_Malone v Laskey_).
    
- **Three Forms:**
    
    1. **Encroachment:** e.g., tree roots crossing a boundary.
        
    2. **Physical Damage:** e.g., vibrations causing cracks in a wall.
        
    3. **Interference with Comfort (Amenity Nuisance):** e.g., noise, smells, dust, fumes.
        

---

### 2. Nuisance and Fault: The Test of Reasonableness

The subtopic **Nuisance and fault** revolves around the concept of "unlawful interference." "Unlawful" here doesn't mean "criminal"; it means "unreasonable."

To determine if an annoyance is a nuisance, the court balances the defendant's right to act and the claimant's right to comfort. This is not a strict liability offense; the court looks at several factors:

1. **Locality:** "What would be a nuisance in Belgrave Square would not necessarily be so in Bermondsey" (_Sturges v Bridgman_). You expect more noise in an industrial zone than a residential one.
    
2. **Duration:** A one-off party is rarely a nuisance; a party every night is. (Exceptions exist for substantial isolated events like a firework display causing a fire).
    
3. **Sensitivity of the Claimant:** The standard is the "ordinary reasonable person." If the claimant is abnormally sensitive (e.g., specialized paper drying in a hot room), they cannot complain if ordinary use affects them (_Robinson v Kilvert_).
    
4. **Malice:** If the defendant acts out of spite, even an otherwise lawful act can become a nuisance (_Christie v Davey_ – banging trays to annoy a neighbor giving music lessons).
    

---

### 3. Defenses to Nuisance

A defendant may avoid liability using specific **Defenses to Nuisance**:

1. **Prescription:** If the nuisance has continued openly and peacefully for **20 years** without the claimant suing, the defendant acquires a legal right to continue it (e.g., a factory operating for 20 years).
    
2. **Statutory Authority:** If a statute authorizes the activity (e.g., an oil refinery authorized by an Act of Parliament), there is no liability for inevitable nuisance resulting from it.
    
3. **Ineffective Defenses (What doesn't work):**
    
    - _Coming to the Nuisance:_ It is **no defense** to say "I was here first, and the plaintiff moved next to me." (_Sturges v Bridgman_).
        
    - _Public Benefit:_ It is generally no defense to argue that the activity (e.g., building causing noise) is useful for the public, though this may influence the _remedy_ (awarding damages instead of shutting down the activity).
        

---

### 4. Remedies for Nuisance

If Nuisance is proven, the court may grant **Remedies for Nuisance**:

1. **Abatement:** A "self-help" remedy. The victim removes the nuisance themselves (e.g., cutting off overhanging branches from a neighbor's tree). Note: They must not cause unnecessary damage and usually need to give notice.
    
2. **Injunction:** The most common remedy. A court order stopping the activity.
    
3. **Damages:** Monetary compensation for the loss of value to the land or discomfort suffered.
    

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- **Key Case:** _Sturges v Bridgman_ (1879) is the "Holy Grail" of Nuisance cases. It teaches two things: 1. Locality matters ("What is a nuisance in X is not in Y"), and 2. "Coming to the nuisance" is not a defense.
    
- **Exam Trap:** Don't confuse Nuisance with Negligence. Nuisance focuses on the **harm to the land/enjoyment**, not necessarily the _carelessness_ of the defendant. You can take all reasonable care and still be liable in Nuisance if the interference is unreasonable.
    
- **Rylands v Fletcher Connection:** While often treated as a separate strict liability rule, _Rylands v Fletcher_ (escaping dangerous things) is historically an offshoot of Nuisance. If an exam question involves something "escaping" (water, gas, chemicals), check if it fits _Rylands_ as well as Nuisance.
