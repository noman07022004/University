## 6. Defamation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This topic addresses the fundamental tension between two major legal rights: the right to reputation and the right to freedom of speech. Understanding this balance is critical for legal practice.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Defamation is a high-frequency exam topic. Problem questions often involve complex scenarios with newspapers or social media posts, requiring you to identify "innuendo" and apply defenses like "fair comment" or "privilege."
    

---

### Introduction: General Concept

**Defamation** is the publication of a statement which reflects on a person's reputation and tends to lower them in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally, or tends to make them shun or avoid them.

Unlike other torts that protect physical safety or property, Defamation protects **reputation**—an intangible but highly valuable asset. The law presumes that every person has a good character until the contrary is proved.

---

### 1. Forms of Defamation: Libel and Slander

The law distinguishes between two forms of defamation, known as **Libel and slander**.

- **Libel:** A defamatory statement in a **permanent form**.
    
    - _Examples:_ Writing, printing, pictures, statues, films, and broadcasting (radio/TV are treated as permanent by statute).
        
    - _Legal Status:_ Libel is actionable **per se** (no need to prove actual financial loss) and can also be a crime.
        
- **Slander:** A defamatory statement in a **transient (temporary) form**.
    
    - _Examples:_ Spoken words, gestures.
        
    - _Legal Status:_ Slander is **not** actionable _per se_; the claimant must prove "special damage" (actual financial loss).
        
    - _Exceptions:_ Slander is actionable without proof of damage in four specific cases:
        
        1. Imputing a criminal offense punishable by imprisonment.
            
        2. Imputing a contagious disease (e.g., leprosy, HIV).
            
        3. Imputing unchastity/adultery to a woman (Slander of Women Act 1891).
            
        4. Imputing incompetence in the claimant's office, profession, or trade.
            

---

### 2. Essentials of Defamation

To succeed in a defamation suit, the plaintiff must prove three elements:

#### A. The Statement must be Defamatory

The statement must lower the plaintiff's reputation.

- **The Test:** Would the words tend to lower the plaintiff in the estimation of "right-thinking members of society generally"? (_Sim v Stretch_).
    
- **Innuendo:** Sometimes a statement appears innocent on its face but carries a hidden defamatory meaning.
    
    - _True/Legal Innuendo:_ Requires special knowledge to understand. (e.g., "Mr. X is a frequent visitor at house Y." This looks innocent, but if people know house Y is a brothel, it is defamatory). The plaintiff must prove the audience had this special knowledge (_Cassidy v Daily Mirror_).
        
    - _False/Popular Innuendo:_ The words themself imply a secondary meaning to an ordinary person (e.g., a caricature).
        

#### B. The Statement must Refer to the Claimant

The plaintiff must be identifiable.

- It is not necessary to name the person; a description is enough if reasonable people would understand it refers to the plaintiff.
    
- _Group Defamation:_ Saying "All lawyers are thieves" is generally not actionable because the class is too large. Saying "The lawyers in this specific firm are thieves" is actionable.
    

#### C. Publication

**Publication** means making the defamatory matter known to some person other than the person defamed.

- Communication to the plaintiff alone is **not** defamation (because reputation is what _others_ think of you, not what you think of yourself).
    
- _Husband & Wife:_ Communication between spouses is _not_ publication. However, a third party telling a husband about his wife _is_ publication.
    

---

### 3. Defenses to Defamation

Even if the statement is defamatory, the defendant can escape liability using these **Defenses**:

1. **Justification (Truth):**
    
    - Truth is a complete defense. The law will not protect a reputation the plaintiff does not deserve to have. The burden is on the defendant to prove the statement is substantially true.
        
2. **Fair Comment:**
    
    - Protects honest expressions of opinion on matters of public interest (e.g., reviewing a book, criticizing a politician's policy).
        
    - _Requirements:_ It must be an opinion (not fact), based on true facts, and not malicious.
        
3. **Privilege:**
    
    - _Absolute Privilege:_ Complete immunity regardless of malice. Applies to statements made in Parliament, judicial proceedings, or official state communication.
        
    - _Qualified Privilege:_ Protects statements made where the speaker has a legal/moral duty to speak and the receiver has an interest in hearing it (e.g., an employer writing a job reference). This protection is lost if the plaintiff proves **malice**.
        
4. **Apology:**
    
    - Offering an apology is not a complete defense but can mitigate (reduce) damages.
        

---

### 4. Remedies

The **Remedies** for defamation include:

1. **Damages:** The primary remedy to vindicate reputation.
    
2. **Injunction:** To prevent further publication of the libel.
    
3. **Offer of Amends:** Under modern statutes, a defendant who defamed innocently can offer to publish a correction and apology to avoid a lawsuit.
    

---

### Important Notes & Tips

- **The "Right-Thinking" Test:** In exams, always use the phrase "right-thinking members of society." Mere insults (vulgar abuse) spoken in the heat of an argument are often not considered defamation because the audience realizes they are just angry outbursts, not factual claims.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** While this course focuses on Tort (Civil), defamation is also a crime in Bangladesh under **Section 499 of the Penal Code**. In practice, many defamation cases in Bangladesh are filed as criminal cases to harass the defendant with arrest, rather than civil suits for compensation.
    
- **Internet Defamation:** Publication rules apply to the internet. Every time a webpage is accessed, a new "publication" occurs (_Duke of Brunswick_ rule applied to the digital age). This is crucial for modern problem questions.
