
## 9.6: Agent's authority (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the most critical, central concept in the entire Law of Agency. The principal's liability is 100% dependent on the scope of the agent's authority. This topic explains _what_ that authority is and _how_ it is created.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the absolute core of any agency problem question. You will _always_ be asked to analyze whether an agent's act was "within their authority" to determine if the principal is bound.


An **agent's authority** is the power they possess to act on behalf of their principal and bind that principal in legal relations with third parties. If an agent acts _within_ this authority, the principal is bound. If they act _outside_ it, the principal is generally not bound (as seen in Topic 9.3).

This authority can be created in several different ways:

### 1. Actual Authority

This is the "real" authority the principal has _actually_ given to the agent. It is based on the agreement between the principal and the agent. As per **Sections 186 and 187 of the Contract Act, 1872**, this authority can be:

- **a) Express Authority (Section 187):**
    
    - **Definition:** Authority that is "given by words spoken or written."
        
    - **What it is:** This is a clear, direct instruction from the principal to the agent.
        
    - **Example:** "I hire you to be my agent to sell my house in Dhaka for no less than 1 crore Taka." A **Power of Attorney (Topic 9.2)** is the most formal example of express authority.
        
- **b) Implied Authority (Section 187):**
    
    - **Definition:** Authority that is "to be inferred from the circumstances of the case." This includes the "ordinary course of dealing" or the customs of a particular trade.
        
    - **What it is:** This is the authority that is _not_ explicitly stated but is _understood_ to exist so the agent can perform their job.
        
    - **Example (from Act, Illus. to S. 187):** A (the principal) lives in Dhaka but owns a shop in Mymensingh. B manages the shop and is in the habit of ordering goods from C on A's behalf and paying from A's funds, all with A's knowledge. B has an _implied authority_ from A to order those goods.
        

### 2. Extent of Actual Authority

**Section 188** defines how _far_ an agent's authority (especially implied authority) goes:

1. **To Do an Act:** An agent with authority to do an act has implied authority to do **"every lawful thing which is necessary in order to do such act."**
    
    - **Example:** An agent hired to "sell a car" has the implied authority to advertise it and allow test drives.
        
2. **To Carry on a Business:** An agent with authority to "carry on a business" has implied authority to do **"every lawful thing necessary for the purpose, or usually done in the course** of conducting such business."
    
    - **Example (from Act, Illus. (b) to S. 188):** An agent hired to run a ship-building business has the implied authority to buy timber and hire workers.
        

### 3. Authority in an Emergency (Section 189)

This is a special type of implied authority.

- **The Rule:** In an emergency, an agent has authority to do **"all such acts for the purpose of protecting his principal from loss"** as a person of ordinary prudence would do in their own case.
    
- **Example (from Act, Illus. (b) to S. 189):** A sends perishable food to B in Chittagong, with orders to send it to C in Dhaka. If the food will spoil on the journey, B has emergency authority to sell it in Chittagong to prevent a total loss for A.
    

### 4. Apparent (or Ostensible) Authority

This is a crucial concept, also known as **"Agency by Estoppel"** and governed by **Section 237**. This is _not_ real authority. It is the _appearance_ of authority.

- **Definition:** Apparent authority exists where the **principal** (not the agent) **by their words or conduct** leads a _third party_ to reasonably believe that the agent has authority, even when the agent _does not_.
    
- **The Rule (Section 237):** If a principal "has by his words or conduct induced such third persons to believe that such acts and obligations were within the scope of the agent's authority," the principal **is bound** by those acts.
    
- **Example:** P (principal) tells T (a third-party supplier) that A is their agent for buying timber. P later _privately_ tells A, "Do not buy any more timber." A, ignoring this, goes to T and orders more timber. T does not know about the private instruction. **P is bound by this contract.** P's _conduct_ created an "apparent authority" in T's mind, and P is "estopped" (prevented) from denying it.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Actual vs. Apparent Authority:** This is the most important distinction in this subtopic.
    
    - **Actual Authority:** A legal relationship between the **Principal and the Agent**.
        
    - **Apparent Authority:** A legal relationship between the **Principal and the Third Party**, created by the Principal's representation.
        
- **Secret Instructions:** Private or secret instructions from the principal to the agent (like "don't sell for less than 1 lakh") **do not affect third parties**. If the agent has the _apparent_ authority to sell, the third party is not bound by secret limits they don't know about. (This is seen in Illus. (a) to S. 237).
    
- **Agent's Liability:** If an agent acts _beyond_ their actual authority, but the principal is _not_ bound (e.g., there was no apparent authority), the agent becomes personally liable to the third party for "breach of warranty of authority."
