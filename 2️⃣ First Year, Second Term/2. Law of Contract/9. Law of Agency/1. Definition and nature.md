
## 9.1: Definition and nature (of Agency) (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the foundational concept for all of Topic 9. The definition and nature of agency explain _what_ this relationship is, _who_ is involved, and _why_ it's a cornerstone of business law. All other subtopics (authority, liability, termination) are built upon this core definition.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. It is very common as a "short note" or as the mandatory first part of a longer essay question (e.g., "Define agency and explain the different types of agents").



### 1. Definition

The concept of "Agency" is defined by the legal relationship between the two parties involved.

**Section 182 of the Contract Act, 1872,** defines the parties:

- **"Agent":** "An 'agent' is a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings with third persons."
    
- **"Principal":** "The person for whom such act is done, or who is so represented, is called the 'principal'."
    

**In simple terms:** The agent (A) is a _conduit_ or _link_. The agent's job is to create a direct legal relationship (like a contract) between the principal (P) and a third party (T).

### 2. Nature of the Agency Relationship

The "nature" of agency describes its unique characteristics, which go beyond a simple contract.

**a) A Fiduciary Relationship** This is the most important characteristic. Agency is a **fiduciary relationship**, which means it is a relationship built on **utmost trust and confidence**.

- The agent (A) has a _fiduciary duty_ to act in the _best interests_ of the principal (P).
    
- The agent must be loyal, avoid conflicts of interest, and not make any secret profits. This is a higher duty than a normal contractual duty.
    

**b) The Power to Bind the Principal** The core function and "nature" of an agent is their _power to bind the principal_.

- **Test of Agency:** The true **test of agency (Subtopic 9.5)** is not what the parties call themselves, but whether one person (the agent) has the legal authority to act on behalf of another (the principal) and create a binding legal relationship between the principal and a third party.
    
- **Consequence (Section 226):** The primary consequence of this is found in **Section 226**, which states: "Contracts entered into through an agent... may be enforced in the same manner, and will have the same legal consequences, as if the contracts had been entered into... by the principal in person."
    
- **The Maxim:** This reflects the Latin maxim: **_"Qui facit per alium, facit per se"_** (He who acts through another, acts himself).
    

**c) No Consideration is Necessary (Section 185)** A unique feature of agency is that **no consideration is necessary** to create the agency itself.

- A person can agree to be an agent for free (a "gratuitous agent").
    
- While consideration (i.e., paying a commission) is _usual_, it is not _essential_ to create the legal relationship of principal and agent.

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Agent vs. Servant (Employee):** This is a critical distinction.
    
    - A **servant** (employee) acts under the _direct control and supervision_ of the master (employer). The master says _what_ to do and _how_ to do it. A servant does not typically have the power to create contracts with third parties.
        
    - An **agent** has the _authority to represent_ the principal and _create legal relations_ with third parties. The principal directs _what_ to do, but the agent often has discretion on _how_ to do it.
        
- **Agent vs. Independent Contractor:**
    
    - An **independent contractor** (e.g., a builder, a plumber) is hired to produce a _result_. The principal has no control over the _manner_ in which the work is done.
        
    - An **agent** acts _on behalf of_ and _subject to the control_ of the principal.
        
    - **Note:** An independent contractor _can sometimes also_ be an agent (e.g., a lawyer or an auctioneer), but not always. The key is the "test of agency": can they bind the principal in a contract?
