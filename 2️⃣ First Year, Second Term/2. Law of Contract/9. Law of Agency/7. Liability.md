
## 9.7: Liability (in Agency) (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the most important practical concept in agency law. The entire purpose of the agency relationship is to create liability for the principal. Understanding _when_ the principal is liable, and, more importantly, _when the agent becomes personally liable_, is the central point of this whole topic.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high importance. Almost every agency problem question revolves around liability. "Is the principal bound by this contract?" "Can the third party sue the agent?" These are the classic exam questions.
    


This subtopic explores _who_ (the principal or the agent) is legally bound by the contracts made by the agent. The rules for liability change depending on whether the agent acted _with_ or _without_ authority, and whether the principal's existence was _disclosed_.

### 1. Liability of the Principal (The General Rule)

The general rule is that the **Principal is liable, and the Agent is not.**

- **When Agent Acts _Within_ Authority (Section 226):** As long as the agent acts within their (actual or apparent) authority, their acts are legally the acts of the principal. The contract is enforceable _by_ and _against_ the principal. The agent is merely a "conduit" and is not personally liable.
    
- **When Agent _Exceeds_ Authority (Section 227 & 228):**
    
    - If the unauthorized part of an act is **separable**, the principal is liable _only_ for the authorized part.
        
    - If the unauthorized part is **inseparable**, the principal can reject the _entire_ transaction.
        
- **Liability for Agent's Fraud (Section 238):** A principal is liable for any fraud or misrepresentation committed by their agent _while acting in the course of the agency business_.
    

### 2. Personal Liability of the Agent (The Exceptions)

The most important part of this topic is knowing when the "shield" protecting the agent disappears, making them **personally liable** to the third party.

The main rule is **Section 230 of the Contract Act, 1872**, which states that an agent is **NOT** personally liable for contracts made on behalf of their principal.

However, **Section 230** provides **three exceptions** where a contract to the contrary is _presumed_ to exist, and the **agent is personally liable**:

**Exception 1: Agent for a Foreign Principal**

- **Rule:** "Where the contract is made by an agent for the sale or purchase of goods for a merchant resident abroad."
    
- **Reason:** In the 19th century, it was impractical for a local third party (in Bangladesh) to sue a principal in London. The law presumes the third party intended to hold the local, accessible agent liable.
    

**Exception 2: Undisclosed Principal**

- **Rule:** "Where the agent does not disclose the name of his principal."
    
- **This has two scenarios:**
    
    - **Unnamed Principal:** The agent tells the third party, "I am an agent," but does _not_ say who the principal is.
        
    - **Undisclosed Principal:** The agent _does not even mention_ they are an agent. The third party believes the agent is the principal.
        
- **Consequence (Section 233):** In this case, the third party has a _choice_. When they discover the true principal, they may sue **either the agent, or the principal, or both.**
    

**Exception 3: Principal Cannot Be Sued**

- **Rule:** "Where the principal, though disclosed, cannot be sued."
    
- **Reason:** The third party must have _someone_ to hold liable.
    
- **Examples:** The principal is a minor (who lacks contractual capacity), a foreign government or ambassador (who has diplomatic immunity), or a company that has not been legally incorporated yet (a "promoter").
    

### 3. Liability of a "Pretended Agent" (Section 235)

This is a separate and very important category of liability.

- **The Rule:** A person who **"untruly represents"** himself to be an authorized agent is personally liable to the third party for any loss or damage.
    
- **What it means:** This is not a real agent; it's someone _pretending_ to be one. If the "principal" does not ratify the act, the third party cannot sue the principal. Their only remedy is to sue the _pretended agent_ for compensation.
    
- **This is called "breach of warranty of authority."** The agent is liable, not for the contract itself, but for the _lie_ that they had authority.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **The "Big 3" Exceptions (Section 230):** For any exam question on agent liability, your first step is to check **Section 230**. Is the principal foreign, undisclosed, or unable to be sued? If yes, the agent is personally liable.
    
- **Undisclosed Principal Rule (Section 233):** Remember that when the principal is undisclosed, the third party gets the "best of both worlds": they can choose to sue the agent (who they thought they were contracting with) _or_ the principal (who they discovered later).
    
- **Agent vs. Pretender:** Do not confuse an _agent who exceeds authority_ (S. 227/228) with a _pretended agent_ (S. 235).
    
    - **Exceeds Authority:** Is a real agent, but went too far. The principal _might_ still be liable (if the act is separable or there was apparent authority).
        
    - **Pretended Agent:** Is a fake. The principal is _not liable_ (unless they ratify). The pretender is _personally liable_ for the breach.
