## 9.8: Misrepresentation and fraud by agent (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This topic establishes the principle of **vicarious liability** in contract law. It explains _why_ a principal, who may be completely innocent, is still held responsible for the wrongful acts (like lying or fraud) of their agent.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** High importance. This is a classic problem question scenario. A third party is tricked by an agent, and the question is whether the innocent principal is bound by the contract or liable for the fraud.


This subtopic deals with the principal's liability for the wrongful acts—specifically misrepresentation and fraud—committed by their agent.

The governing rule is **Section 238 of the Contract Act, 1872**.

**The Rule (Section 238):** The section states:

> "Misrepresentations made, or frauds committed, by agents **acting in the course of their business for their principals**, have the **same effect** on agreements made by such agents as if such misrepresentations or frauds had been made or committed by the principals..."

**Breaking Down the Rule:**

1. **The Act:** The agent must have committed **misrepresentation** (an innocent false statement) or **fraud** (an intentional deception).
    
2. **The Condition:** The act must have been done **"in the course of their business for their principals."** This is the key test. It doesn't matter if the principal _authorized_ the fraud, or even _forbade_ it. If the agent was performing their authorized job (e.g., selling a product) and committed fraud _while doing that job_, the principal is liable.
    
3. **The Consequence:** The legal effect is the **same as if the principal had committed the fraud**. This means the third party who was deceived has the right to **rescind the contract** (as it is voidable under Section 19) and can also sue for damages.
    

**Example (from Act, Illus. (a) to S. 238):**

- A is B's agent for the sale of goods.
    
- A induces C to buy the goods by making a **misrepresentation** (a lie about the goods' quality).
    
- B (the principal) did **not** authorize A to make this statement.
    
- **Result:** The contract is **voidable** at the option of C (the third party). C can cancel the contract with B.
    

**The Exception (Section 238, second half):** The section also states the opposite:

> "...but misrepresentations made, or frauds committed, by agents, in matters which **do not fall within their authority**, do not affect their principals."

- **Meaning:** If the agent's lie or fraudulent act has _nothing_ to do with the job the principal hired them for, the principal is not liable.
    
- **Example:** A hires B, a real estate agent, to sell a house. B, while showing the house, also tricks a client into buying his (B's) own personal car by lying about its engine. The principal (A) is _not_ liable for the fraud related to the car, as that was not part of the agency business.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Vicarious Liability:** This is the core concept. The principal is held responsible for the agent's wrongful acts based on the principle of _respondeat superior_ ("let the master answer") and the maxim _qui facit per alium facit per se_ ("he who acts through another, acts himself").
    
- **Principal's Benefit is Irrelevant:** It does not matter if the principal received any benefit from the fraud. The principal is liable even if the agent committed the fraud for their _own_ personal gain.
    
- **Landmark Case:** The leading case for this principle is **_Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co._ (1912)**.
    
    - **Facts:** A solicitor's firm employed a managing clerk (an agent) to handle property transactions. A client, Mrs. Lloyd, went to the firm and the clerk tricked her into signing documents that transferred her property to _himself_. He then sold the property and kept the money.
        
    - **Held:** The firm (the principal) was held **liable** for the agent's fraud. Even though the fraud was for the agent's _own benefit_ and not the firm's, it was committed _in the course of the agency business_ (the job he was hired to do).
