## 9.5: Test of agency (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐)

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This is the practical "test" a court applies to a set of facts to see if an agency relationship _actually exists_. It's the legal tool for distinguishing a true agent from a mere servant or independent contractor, which has massive consequences for liability.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐):** Less important as a standalone question, but critically important for _applying_ the law. You won't be asked "What is the test of agency?" but you will be given a problem and must _use_ this test to determine if a principal is liable for someone else's actions.

The **"test of agency"** is not a single, simple test but a set of principles used by a court to determine if one person (the "agent") was, in fact and in law, acting on behalf of another (the "principal").

Parties cannot just put a label on their relationship. Calling someone an "independent contractor" in a contract does not stop a court from finding that they were _actually_ an agent, if the facts support it. The court looks at the _substance_ of the relationship, not the _form_.

The two primary tests are:

### 1. The Power to Bind the Principal (The Legal Test)

This is the most important legal test, derived from the definition in **Section 182** of the Contract Act.

- **The Test:** Does the person have the authority to "represent another in dealings with third persons"? Can they make a contract or perform an act that **creates, modifies, or terminates legal relations** between their principal and a third party?
    
- **Application:**
    
    - If **Yes** (e.g., a procurement officer can sign purchase orders that bind the company), it is an agency.
        
    - If **No** (e.g., a factory worker cannot sign a sales contract on behalf of the company), it is not an agency.
        

### 2. The "Right to Control" Test (The Factual Test)

This test is used to determine the _degree_ of the relationship, which helps distinguish an agent from a servant or an independent contractor.

- **vs. Servant (Employee):** A master has the right to control _what_ the servant does and _how_ they do it (the "manner and means"). The servant has very little discretion.
    
- **vs. Agent:** A principal has the right to direct _what_ the agent's ultimate task is, but the agent often has the _discretion_ to choose _how_ to accomplish that task.
    
- **vs. Independent Contractor:** The principal/employer has _no right to control_ the _method_ of the work. They can only specify the _final result_.
    

**Example:**

- If you hire a driver as your **servant**, you can tell them what to do (drive to the market) and how to do it (take this specific route, drive at 40 km/h).
    
- If you hire a **real estate agent** to sell your house, you tell them _what_ to do (sell the house for 1 crore), but you don't control _how_ they do it (how they advertise, who they call, etc.).
    
- If you hire a **taxi driver** (independent contractor), you only control the _result_ (take me to this address). You do not control the _method_ (the route they take, the speed they drive, etc.).
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Substance Over Form:** The court is not bound by the labels the parties use. If a principal _exercises a high degree of control_ over an "independent contractor," a court may find that an agency relationship actually exists, making the principal liable for the contractor's actions.
    
- **Why this Test Matters:** The test is critical for determining **liability**.
    
    - If the person is an **agent**, the principal is bound by their contracts (Section 226) and can be liable for their fraud (Section 238).
        
    - If the person is a **servant**, the master is vicariously liable for their torts (wrongful acts) in the course of employment.
        
    - If the person is a true **independent contractor**, the employer is generally _not_ liable for their contracts or their torts.
