## 📌 4.2: Liability to perform (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This subtopic answers the fundamental question of "Who must perform?" It's crucial for understanding how contractual obligations are assigned, especially in cases involving death, agents, or personal skills.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. This is a common part of problem questions. You'll often be asked to determine if a contract is terminated by a promisor's death or if the promisor's son/agent can legally complete the job.
    

This subtopic explores _who_ is legally bound to fulfill the promises in a contract. The general rule is simple: the **promisor** is the one with the liability to perform. However, this rule changes based on the nature of the contract.

The law is primarily governed by **Section 40 of the Contract Act, 1872**. This section divides all contracts into two types: personal and impersonal.

### 1️⃣ Personal Contracts (Promisor Must Perform)

If the contract's nature implies that it was the **"intention of the parties"** that the promise must be performed by the **promisor himself**, then it must be performed by him personally.

- **What it is:** This applies to any contract that depends on the promisor's unique **personal skill**, taste, expertise, or personal confidence.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - A promise to paint a picture.
        
    - A promise to sing at a concert.
        
    - A promise to act in a film.
        
    - A promise to marry.
        
- **Consequence of Death:** In a personal contract, the death of the promisor **terminates the contract**. The promisor's legal representatives (heirs) are _not_ liable to perform, and the promisee cannot demand performance from them.
    

### 2️⃣ Impersonal Contracts (Others May Perform)

**Section 40** states that "in other cases," the promisor or their representatives may **employ a competent person to perform it.**

- **What it is:** This applies to the vast majority of contracts where the identity of the person performing is _not_ the main point. The promisee just wants the _result_.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - A promise to pay a sum of money.
        
    - A promise to deliver 100 bags of rice.
        
    - A promise to build a standard boundary wall.
        
- **Who Can Perform:**
    
    - **The Promisor:** Can perform personally.
        
    - **An Agent:** The promisor can employ a competent agent to perform. (e.g., A can pay B's debt by sending the money via a delivery agent).
        
    - **Legal Representatives:** If the promisor dies before performing, their **legal representatives** (heirs) are **legally bound** to perform the contract, _unless_ a contrary intention is in the contract. They are, however, only liable up to the value of the property they inherit from the deceased.
        

### 3️⃣ Accepting Performance from a Third Party

A closely related and critical rule is found in **Section 41 of the Contract Act, 1872**.

- **The Rule:** "When a promisee accepts performance of the promise from a third person, he cannot afterwards enforce it against the promisor."
    
- **Example:** A owes 5,000 Taka to B. A's friend, C, goes to B and offers to pay 4,000 Taka in "full and final settlement" of A's debt. If B _accepts_ this payment from C, B cannot later sue A for the remaining 1,000 Taka. By accepting the third party's performance, B has discharged the original promisor (A) from the liability.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Intention is Key:** For any exam problem, the first question you must ask is: "Was it the _intention_ of the parties that only the promisor could perform?" If yes, it's a personal contract. If not, it's impersonal.
    
- **Personal Skill = Terminates on Death:** This is a fundamental rule. The death of a famous singer terminates their concert contract; the death of a building contractor does _not_ terminate the contract to build a house (their legal heirs must complete it, using the estate's funds).
    
- **Section 41 is a Full Discharge:** Remember, accepting performance from a third party (Section 41) is a complete defense for the original promisor. The promisee gets one chance to accept performance; they can't take it from a third party and then ask for more from the original promisor.
