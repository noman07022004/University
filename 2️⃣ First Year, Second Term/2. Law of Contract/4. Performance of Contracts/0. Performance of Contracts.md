## 📌 Main Topic 4: Performance of Contracts (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the ultimate goal of every contract. Formation is the "birth," but performance is the "life" and "purpose." This topic explains how, when, and by whom promises must be fulfilled. It's the core of a contract's practical execution.  
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high importance. Questions on performance, especially tender (4.1), reciprocal promises (4.4), and time as the essence (4.8), are very common. This topic is directly linked to "Breach of Contract" (5.11) and "Remedies" (Topic 6).
    


**Performance of a contract** is the fulfillment of the respective legal obligations created under it by the parties. When both parties have done what they promised to do, the contract is successfully "discharged by performance," and it comes to a complete and natural end.

This entire topic is governed by **Chapter IV (Sections 37-61) of the Contract Act, 1872**.

The fundamental rule is stated in **Section 37**: Parties to a contract **must** either perform or _offer to perform_ their respective promises, unless the law excuses them (e.g., by mutual agreement or impossibility). This single section introduces the two key concepts: the _liability_ to perform and the _offer_ to perform.

### 1️⃣ Who Must Perform? (The Liability)

The primary **liability to perform (Subtopic 4.2)** rests with the **promisor** themselves.

- ***Personal Contracts:*** If the promise relies on the promisor's personal skill, taste, or credit (e.g., a promise to paint a picture, to sing, or to marry), the promisor _must_ perform it personally. Their death terminates the contract.
    
- ***Impersonal Contracts:*** For most contracts (e.g., a promise to pay money or deliver goods), performance is not personal. The promisor can employ a competent person (an agent) to perform. If the promisor dies, their **legal representatives** (heirs) are bound to perform, unless a contrary intention appears in the contract.
    
- ***Joint Liability (Subtopic 4.3):*** When two or more people make a **joint promise** (co-promisors), the law (Sections 42-44) states they are **jointly and severally liable**. This means the promisee (the person owed the promise) can demand full performance from _any one_ of them. That promisor can then later claim a fair contribution from the other co-promisors.
    

### 2️⃣ How is Performance Done? (The Offer)

Performance isn't always a one-sided act. Sometimes, a promisor is ready and willing to perform, but the promisee refuses to accept. This is called an **"offer to perform" (Subtopic 4.1)** or, in common law, a **"tender."**

As per **Section 38**, if a promisor makes a _valid_ offer to perform and the promisee _rejects_ it, the promisor is **not responsible for non-performance**. A valid tender must be:

1. **Unconditional**;
    
2. Made at a **proper time and place**; and
    
3. Must give the promisee a **reasonable opportunity** to inspect the goods or ascertain that the promisor is able and willing to perform the _whole_ promise.
    

### 3️⃣ Order and Timing of Performance

- ***Reciprocal Promises (Subtopic 4.4):*** Most contracts consist of "promises which form the consideration for each other" (e.g., A promises to sell his car to B, and B promises to pay A). Sections 51-54 govern their performance, defining the order. Some are performed simultaneously (like paying for goods at a shop), while others require one party to perform first (like a builder building a house before the owner makes the final payment).
    
- ***Time and Place (Subtopic 4.7):*** The rules (Sections 46-50) are crucial. If the contract specifies a time and place, performance must be at that time and place. If **no time is specified**, the promise must be performed within a **reasonable time** (Section 46).
    
- ***Performance Within Stipulated Time (Subtopic 4.8):*** This is a critical exam topic. If the parties intended for time to be a critical condition ("**time is of the essence of the contract**"), a failure to perform on time makes the contract **voidable** at the option of the innocent party (**Section 55**). In commercial contracts (like for the supply of goods), time is _usually_ presumed to be of the essence.
    

### 4️⃣ Special Performance Rules

- ***Appropriation of Payments (Subtopic 4.9):*** This (Sections 59-61) deals with a specific situation: a debtor owes _multiple distinct debts_ to the same creditor and makes a payment.
    
    1. **Debtor's Choice:** The debtor (payer) has the first right to "appropriate" or designate which debt the payment is for (Section 59).
        
    2. **Creditor's Choice:** If the debtor doesn't specify, the creditor can apply it to any lawful debt (Section 60).
        
    3. **By Law:** If neither party appropriates, the law applies the payment to the debts in **chronological order** (oldest first) (Section 61).
        
- ***Assignment of Contracts (Subtopic 4.6):*** This refers to the transfer of contractual rights and/or liabilities. The general rule is that contractual **rights (benefits)** are assignable (e.g., the right to receive payment), but contractual **liabilities (obligations)** are not, especially in personal contracts. You cannot force a promisee to accept performance from a new person they did not agree to.
    
---

Finally, there are situations where **Contracts Need Not Be Performed (Subtopic 4.5)**. Section 37 itself states performance is not required if it is "dispensed with or excused" by law. This provides a direct link to the next main topic (Termination), as performance is excused if the contract is terminated by:

- **Mutual Agreement:** Novation, alteration, or rescission (Section 62).
    
- **Impossibility/Frustration:** The act becomes impossible (Section 56).
    
- **Voidability:** The contract is voidable and the party rescinds it (Section 64).

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- ***Governing Law:*** In Bangladesh, this entire topic is governed by **Chapter IV (Sections 37-61) of the Contract Act, 1872**. Familiarity with these specific sections is essential.
    
- ***Performance vs. Breach:*** This topic is the direct opposite of "Breach of Contract." A successful performance discharges the contract, while a failure to perform (without a valid excuse) constitutes a breach, leading to remedies.
    
- ***Substantial Performance:*** While the Contract Act implies complete performance, common law (and commercial practice) often recognizes **"substantial performance."** This means a party has performed the _essential_ terms of the contract, even with minor, non-material defects. The other party must still perform (e.g., pay) but can claim damages for the minor defects.
    
- ***Tender of Money vs. Tender of Goods:*** An "offer to perform" by paying money has a different effect. If a valid tender of money is refused, the debtor is **not discharged** from the obligation to pay the debt. However, they are no longer liable for any _interest_ on the debt from the date of the valid tender.
