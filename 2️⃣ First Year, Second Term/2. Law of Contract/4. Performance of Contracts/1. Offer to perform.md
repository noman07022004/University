## 📌 4.1: Offer to perform (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This concept, also known as **"tender,"** is the legal mechanism that protects a promisor who is ready, willing, and able to perform their side of the bargain, but is prevented from doing so by the promisee's refusal.  
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This is a common element in problem questions about breach of contract. You'll often be asked to determine if a party's "offer to perform" was valid and what the consequences are for both parties if it was rejected.


An **"offer to perform,"** or **"tender,"** is a formal, valid attempt by the promisor (the person who made the promise) to fulfill their contractual obligation.

This topic is governed by **Section 38 of the Contract Act, 1872**. The core idea is simple: You can't be blamed for "non-performance" if the only reason you didn't perform is that the other party wouldn't let you.

If a promisor makes a valid offer to perform and the promisee _rejects_ it, the law provides a crucial protection:

> **The promisor is not responsible for non-performance, nor do they lose their rights under the contract.**

This means the promisor is "excused" from their obligation and can, in fact, sue the promisee for breach of contract.

---

### 🔷 Conditions for a Valid Tender

For this legal protection to apply, the "offer to perform" can't be casual or incomplete. **Section 38** lays down strict conditions for a valid tender.

1. ***It must be unconditional:*** The promisor cannot attach new conditions to their performance. For example, if A is contracted to deliver 100 bags of rice, A cannot say, "Here are the 100 bags, but I will only give them to you if you also sign this new, separate agreement."
    
2. ***It must be at a proper time and place:*** If the contract specifies "at B's warehouse on November 14th," the tender must be made there and then. If no time is specified, it must be at a "reasonable hour" (i.e., during business hours, not at 3 AM).
    
3. ***It must be for the whole obligation:*** A tender for partial performance is not a valid tender. If A owes B 100,000 Taka, an offer to pay 80,000 Taka is not a valid tender.
    
4. ***It must provide a reasonable opportunity to inspect:*** The promisee must be given a fair chance to check that what is being offered is what was actually agreed upon.
    
    - ***For Goods (Section 38(3)):*** If A offers to deliver 100 bales of cotton, B must have a reasonable opportunity to _see_ that the goods are indeed 100 bales of cotton of the correct quality.
        
    - ***For Performance (Section 38(2)):*** The promisee must have a reasonable opportunity to be sure that the promisor is _able and willing_ to do the whole thing they promised.
        

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Tender of Goods vs. Tender of Money:** This is a crucial distinction.
    
    - **Tender of Goods:** If a promisor makes a valid tender of goods (e.g., delivers the cotton) and the promisee rejects it, the promisor is **completely discharged** from their obligation. They no longer have to deliver the goods and can sue for breach.
        
    - **Tender of Money:** If a debtor makes a valid tender of money (e.g., offers the correct cash) and the creditor rejects it, the debtor is **not** discharged from the debt. The debtor must still pay the money. However, the valid tender **stops any future interest** from accruing on the debt.
        
- **"Attempted Performance":** "Offer to perform" is often called "attempted performance." In the eyes of the law, a valid _attempt_ that is _rejected_ is legally equivalent to full performance for the purpose of discharging the promisor and holding the promisee liable.
    
- **Section 38 is Your Checklist:** For any exam problem on this, go through the conditions of Section 38 one by one. Was it unconditional? Was it at the right time? Was inspection allowed? If any condition is missing, it is not a valid tender, and the promisor is not protected.
