## 📌 4.4: Reciprocal promises (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. Most real-world contracts (like sales or employment) are not one-sided. They are built on reciprocal promises—"you do this, _and_ I will do that." This topic explains the rules of "who goes first" and what happens if one party fails.  
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. The rules for performance, especially default (Section 54) and order (Section 52), are a common source of problem questions (e.g., "A builder stopped work because the owner didn't supply materials. What happens now?").



A **reciprocal promise** is the legal term for the promises that make up a two-sided bargain.

**Section 2(f) of the Contract Act, 1872,** defines them as:

> "Promises which form the consideration or part of the consideration for each other, are called reciprocal promises."

**Example:** A agrees to sell his car to B for 500,000 Taka.

- A's promise (to deliver the car) is the consideration for B's promise.
    
- B's promise (to pay the 500,000 Taka) is the consideration for A's promise.
    
- These two promises are "reciprocal."
    

The law on _how_ to perform these promises is governed by **Sections 51-54 of the Contract Act, 1872**.

---

### 🔷 Types and Order of Performance

For a court to analyze performance, it first classifies the promises into three types.

***1. Mutual and Independent Promises:*** These are rare. The promises are independent, and each party must perform their side regardless of whether the other has.

- **Example:** A promises to deliver goods to B on November 15, and B promises to pay A on November 10. B's promise to pay is independent of A's delivery. Even if B fails to pay on the 10th, A must still deliver on the 15th (though A can then sue B for the payment).
    

***2. Mutual and Dependent (or Conditional) Promises:*** This is very common. The performance of one promise **depends on** the prior performance of the other.

- This is the rule of **Section 54**, which states that if a promisor (who must perform _first_) fails to do so, they **cannot** claim performance from the other party.
    
- **Example (from Act, Illus. (b) to S. 54):** A contracts to build a house for B, with B agreeing to supply the necessary timber. B _refuses_ to supply the timber. A's promise (to build) is dependent on B's promise (to supply timber). A is excused from building and can sue B for compensation.
    
- **Order of Performance (Section 52):**
    
    - If the contract **expressly fixes** the order, that order must be followed.
        
    - If not fixed, the order is that "which the nature of the transaction requires." (e.g., building the house _before_ the final payment is made).
        

***3. Mutual and Concurrent (or Simultaneous) Promises:*** These promises are meant to be performed at the **exact same time**.

- This is the rule of **Section 51**, which states that no promisor needs to perform their promise _unless_ the other party is **"ready and willing"** to perform theirs.
    
- **Example (from Act, Illus. (a) to S. 51):** A agrees to sell goods to B _for cash on delivery_.
    
    - A does not have to deliver the goods unless B is "ready and willing" to pay.
        
    - B does not have to pay unless A is "ready and willing" to deliver.
        

---

### 🔷 Consequences of Default or Prevention

- **Default in First Promise (Section 54):** As seen above, if the party who is supposed to go _first_ fails, they not only can't demand performance, but they **must make compensation** to the other party for the loss.
    
- **One Party Prevents Performance (Section 53):** If one party _prevents_ the other from performing their promise, the contract becomes **voidable** at the option of the party who was prevented. That party is also entitled to **compensation** for any loss.
    
    - **Example:** A contracts to paint B's house. B (the owner) repeatedly locks the doors and refuses to let A's painters inside. B has _prevented_ A from performing. A can now rescind the contract and sue B for compensation.
        

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **"Ready and Willing":** This is a key legal phrase from Section 51. It doesn't mean you must have the cash _literally_ in your hand. It means you must show that you have the financial capacity and the genuine intention to perform.
    
- **Order is Key:** For any problem question, the first step is to determine the _order_ of performance (Section 52). Is it simultaneous (51), or is one dependent on the other (54)? The answer to this question determines who is in breach.
    
- **Legal vs. Illegal Promises (Section 57):** If there's a reciprocal promise to do legal things and, in other circumstances, illegal things, the legal set is a contract, but the illegal set is a void agreement.
    
    - **Example (from Act, Illus. to S. 57):** A agrees to sell his house to B for 10,000 Taka, but if B uses it as a gambling house, B must pay 50,000 Taka. The first part (sell for 10,000) is a valid contract. The second part (paying 50,000 to use it for gambling) is a void agreement.
