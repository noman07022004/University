## 📌 4.6: Assignment of contracts (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This concept is critical for modern business. It explains how contractual _benefits_ (like the right to receive money) can be treated as assets and transferred. It's essential to distinguish this from transferring _duties_.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐):** Less common. While fundamental to commercial law, it's not as frequently tested in introductory problem questions as topics like "Breach" or "Offer/Acceptance." It's more likely to be a short note or part of a larger question.


**Assignment of a contract** refers to the process of transferring a party's rights and/or obligations under an existing contract to a third party.

This topic is more complex than it appears because it involves two separate concepts:

1. **Assignment of Rights (Benefits)**
    
2. **Delegation of Duties (Liabilities/Obligations)**
    

The general rule of contract law, and a principle embedded in the **Contract Act, 1872**, is that these two are treated very differently.

### 1️⃣ Assignment of Rights (Benefits) - Generally Allowed

A party can generally transfer (assign) their _rights_ under a contract to a third party without the consent of the other party.

- **What are rights?** The _benefits_ of the contract. The most common right is the right to receive payment, services, or goods.
    
- **Example:** A is a building contractor. B owes A 500,000 Taka for a completed project. A needs cash now, so he "assigns" his right to receive that 500,000 Taka to a bank (the "assignee") in exchange for an immediate loan. The bank can now collect the 500,000 Taka directly from B.
    

### 2️⃣ Delegation of Duties (Obligations) - Generally Not Allowed

A party generally **cannot** transfer (delegate) their _duties_ or _liabilities_ to a third party without the _consent_ of the promisee.

- **Why?** The promisee (B) entered into the contract with the promisor (A) based on A's specific reputation, skill, and trustworthiness. The law will not force B to accept performance from a random third party (C) they never agreed to.
    
- **Exception: Impersonal Contracts:** If the duty is purely "impersonal" or "ministerial" (i.e., it requires no special skill, like paying money or delivering standard goods), it can often be delegated.
    
- **Prohibition: Personal Contracts:** Delegation is **strictly prohibited** in contracts based on **personal skill, confidence, or credit**.
    
    - **Example:** If you hire a famous architect to design your house, he cannot "assign" the contract to a new, unknown architect. The contract is personal to his skill.
        

### 🔷 Assignment vs. Novation (A Critical Distinction)

This is a key concept often confused with assignment.

- **Assignment (Delegation):** The original promisor (the "delegator") transfers their _duty_ but **remains liable**. If the new party (the "delegatee") fails to perform, the promisee can still sue the _original_ promisor.
    
    - _Example:_ A (contractor) delegates the painting work to C. If C does a terrible job, B (the homeowner) sues A, not C.
        
- **Novation (Section 62 of the Contract Act):** This is _not_ a transfer; it's a **substitution**. All three parties (A, B, and C) must agree to **end the original contract** and create a _new one_ between B and C.
    
    - _Effect:_ The original promisor (A) is **completely released** from all liability. B's only contract is now with C.
        

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **"Rights are assigned; duties are delegated."** This is the key phrase to remember.
    
- **Governing Law:** The **Contract Act, 1872**, does not have a single "assignment" chapter. The concept is built from Section 37 (obligation of parties), Section 40 (who must perform), and Section 62 (Novation). The **Transfer of Property Act, 1882** (specifically Section 130) contains the formal rules for assigning a "chose in action" (like the right to collect a debt).
    
- **Anti-Assignment Clauses:** Because assignment can be complex, many modern contracts include an **"anti-assignment clause"** that explicitly _prohibits_ either party from transferring their rights or duties without the written consent of the other.
