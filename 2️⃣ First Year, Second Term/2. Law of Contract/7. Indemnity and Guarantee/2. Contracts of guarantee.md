
## 7.2: Contracts of guarantee (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This is a fundamental commercial instrument. It's the legal basis for bank guarantees, personal guarantees for loans, and performance bonds. You must understand its unique three-party structure.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** High importance. This topic is a major source of exam questions, especially when distinguished from "indemnity" (7.3) or when analyzing the surety's liability (7.5) and rights (7.6).


A **Contract of Guarantee** is a contract to ensure a debt is paid or a promise is kept. It's an undertaking by one person to be _secondarily_ responsible for another person's primary liability.

The **Contract Act, 1872,** defines it in **Section 126**:

> "A 'contract of guarantee' is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his **default**."

This contract is **tripartite** (involves three parties), and Section 126 defines them all:

1. **The Principal Debtor:** The person who has the _primary_ liability to pay the debt or perform the promise. (e.g., the borrower).
    
2. **The Creditor:** The person to whom the guarantee is given. (e.g., the bank or lender).
    
3. **The Surety (or "Guarantor"):** The person who gives the guarantee and promises to pay if the principal debtor defaults. (e.g., the person "co-signing" the loan).
    

**Example:** Ali (Principal Debtor) wants a 100,000 Taka loan from BRAC Bank (Creditor). The bank is hesitant. Ali's friend, Bipu (Surety), tells the bank, "Give Ali the loan. If he fails to pay you, I will."

- There are **three** parties.
    
- The **primary liability** to pay is Ali's.
    
- Bipu's liability is **secondary**—it only activates _if_ Ali **defaults**.
    

---

### Essential Features of a Contract of Guarantee

1. **Tripartite Agreement:** It involves three parties, and there are actually **three separate contracts** (though two are usually express and one is implied):
    
    - **Contract 1:** Between the Principal Debtor and the Creditor (the main loan).
        
    - **Contract 2:** Between the Surety and the Creditor (the guarantee).
        
    - **Contract 3 (Implied):** An _implied_ contract between the Surety and the Principal Debtor, where the debtor has an implied duty to indemnify (reimburse) the surety if the surety is forced to pay the debt (this is covered in **Section 145**).
        
2. **A Principal Debt:** There must be a primary, enforceable debt or promise. If the main debt is **void** (e.g., a loan to a minor), the guarantee is also invalid. The surety cannot be liable for a debt that never legally existed.
    
3. **Secondary Liability:** The surety's liability is _secondary_ (or "collateral") to the principal debtor's. The creditor must first look to the principal debtor for performance. The surety's liability only begins upon "default."
    
    - This is the main difference from a **Contract of Indemnity (7.1)**, where the indemnifier's liability is _primary_ and direct.
        
4. **Consideration (Section 127):** A guarantee, like any contract, needs consideration. However, **Section 127** has a special rule:
    
    > "Anything done, or any promise made, for the **benefit of the principal debtor**, may be a sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee."
    
    - **Meaning:** The surety (Bipu) does not need to receive a personal fee. The act of the bank (Creditor) _giving the loan_ to Ali (Principal Debtor) is the "benefit" that supports Bipu's promise to guarantee it.
        
    - However, "past consideration" is not valid. If the loan was already given, and _then_ the surety promises to guarantee it, that promise is void (as seen in Illus. (c) to S. 127).
        
5. **Can be Oral or Written:** In Bangladesh and India, **Section 126** explicitly states that a guarantee "may be either oral or written." This is a major difference from English law, which requires guarantees to be in writing.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Surety's Liability is "Co-extensive":** This is a key concept from **Section 128** (Subtopic 7.5). It means the surety's liability is for the _exact same amount_ as the principal debtor's, including interest and charges, _unless_ the surety has explicitly limited their liability in the contract.
    
- **Creditor Can Sue Surety Directly:** Once the default occurs, the creditor does _not_ have to sue the principal debtor first. The surety's liability is "co-extensive," so the creditor can immediately sue the surety for the full amount.
    
- **Good Faith:** While not a contract of _uberrimae fidei_ (utmost good faith, like insurance), the law requires that the creditor must not obtain the guarantee by **misrepresentation (Section 142)** or **concealment (Section 143)** of material facts. If they do, the guarantee is **invalid**.
