## 7.6: Rights of sureties (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. The surety's liability (7.5) is a huge burden. This subtopic provides the other side of the coin: the surety's "safety net" or legal protections. These rights are what make a surety's position commercially viable.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** High importance. Problem questions involving a surety who has paid a debt are common. You will be expected to advise the surety on their rights to recover the money from the debtor or get security from the creditor.


The law recognizes that the surety is doing a favor for the principal debtor and is taking on a significant risk. Therefore, if the surety is forced to perform, the law grants them a set of rights to recover their loss and minimize their burden. These rights are grouped into three categories:

### 1. Rights Against the Principal Debtor

This is the surety's most important set of rights, allowing them to recover the money they paid.

- **Right of Subrogation (Section 140):** This is the surety's primary right. "Subrogation" means "stepping into the shoes of another."
    
    - **The Rule:** When the surety pays the guaranteed debt or performs the obligation, they are **"invested with all the rights which the creditor had against the principal debtor."**
        
    - **Meaning:** The surety effectively _becomes_ the new creditor. They get to take over all the rights, remedies, and securities (like a mortgage) that the original creditor (the bank) held against the debtor.
        
    - **Example:** A (debtor) mortgages his house to B (bank) for a loan. C is the surety. A defaults. C pays B the full loan amount. C is now "subrogated" to B's rights and can enforce the mortgage on A's house to recover the money.
        
- **Right to Indemnity (Section 145):** This right exists alongside subrogation.
    
    - **The Rule:** "In every contract of guarantee there is an **implied promise** by the principal debtor to indemnify the surety."
        
    - **Meaning:** The law automatically implies a contract between the debtor and the surety, in which the debtor promises to reimburse the surety for any amount the surety has _rightfully_ paid under the guarantee.
        

### 2. Rights Against the Creditor

- **Right to Benefit of Creditor's Securities (Section 141):** This is a crucial right for the surety's protection.
    
    - **The Rule:** A surety is entitled to the benefit of **"every security"** which the creditor has against the principal debtor at the time the guarantee is given.
        
    - **Crucial Point:** This is true **"whether the surety knows of the existence of such security or not."**
        
    - **Consequence:** If the creditor _loses_ or _gives up_ any of that security without the surety's consent, the **surety is discharged** from liability to the extent of the value of that security.
        
    - **Example (from Act, Illus. (a) to S. 141):** C (bank) lends 2,000 Taka to B (debtor), guaranteed by A (surety). C also takes a mortgage on B's furniture as extra security. C later cancels the mortgage. B becomes insolvent. C sues A. A is **discharged** from liability for the 2,000 Taka, because C gave up the security (the furniture) that A was entitled to.
        

### 3. Rights Against Co-sureties

This applies when there are multiple people (co-sureties) guaranteeing the same debt.

- **Right to Contribution (Section 146):**
    
    - **The Rule:** "Where two or more persons are co-sureties... they are liable, as between themselves, to **pay each an equal share** of the whole debt..."
        
    - **Meaning:** Co-sureties must share the burden equally. If one surety is forced to pay the _entire_ debt, they have the right to demand a proportionate contribution from the other co-sureties.
        
    - **Example:** A, B, and C are co-sureties for a 900,000 Taka loan to D. D defaults. The creditor forces A to pay the entire 900,000 Taka. A can now recover 300,000 Taka from B and 300,000 Taka from C.
        

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Surety's Rights are a "Shield and a Sword":**
    
    - **Shield (Section 141):** The right to securities acts as a "shield." If the creditor messes with the security, the surety is protected (discharged).
        
    - **Sword (Sections 140 & 145):** The rights of subrogation and indemnity are a "sword." Once the surety pays, they can actively _attack_ the principal debtor to recover their money.
        
- **The Three Rights:** For any exam question about a surety who has paid, you must mention their three key rights: **Subrogation** (gets the creditor's rights), **Indemnity** (gets reimbursement from the debtor), and **Contribution** (gets help from other co-sureties).
