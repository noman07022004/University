## 📌 2.8: Free consent (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the moral and ethical core of contract law. The entire "will theory" (from 1.4) rests on the idea that parties are bound _because they freely chose_ to be. This subtopic explains what "free choice" legally means and what happens when it is taken away.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is one of the most important topics for problem questions. You will frequently be given a scenario and asked to determine if the consent was truly free, or if it was "vitiated" (poisoned) by one of these factors, and what the legal consequence is.

"Free Consent" is one of the most essential elements of a valid contract. It's not enough for parties to simply "agree"—their agreement must be genuine, voluntary, and based on a true "meeting of the minds."

First, the law defines **"Consent"** in **Section 13 of the Contract Act, 1872**. It states that two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the **"same thing in the same sense."** This is the legal concept of **_consensus ad idem_** (meeting of the minds).

However, just having _consent_ is not enough. The consent must be **"Free."**

**Section 14 of the Contract Act, 1872**, defines "Free Consent." It states that consent is free when it is **NOT** caused by any of the following five vitiating (poisoning) factors:

1. **Coercion (Section 15)**
    
2. **Undue Influence (Section 16)**
    
3. **Fraud (Section 17)**
    
4. **Misrepresentation (Section 18)**
    
5. **Mistake (Sections 20, 21, 22)**
    

If a party's "yes" to a contract was obtained through any of these methods, the consent is not free, and the contract's validity is compromised.

---

### 🔷 The Vitiating Factors Explained

#### 1️⃣ Coercion (Section 15)

This is the most overt form of non-free consent. Coercion is compelling a person to enter into a contract by using **physical force or threats.**

- **Definition:** Coercion involves:
    
    - Committing or threatening to commit any act **forbidden by the Penal Code** (e.g., a threat of violence, kidnapping, or murder).
        
    - Unlawfully detaining or threatening to detain **any property**.
        
- **Nature:** It is about crude, physical, or violent threats. The pressure is external and obvious.
    
- **Example:** A threatens to shoot B if B does not sell his car to A for 50,000 Taka. B agrees. This consent is obtained by coercion.
    

#### 2️⃣ Undue Influence (Section 16)

This is a more subtle form of pressure. It is **psychological or moral pressure**, not physical.

- **Definition:** Undue influence occurs when:
    
    - One party is in a **position to dominate the will** of the other (e.g., they are in a relationship of trust and confidence).
        
    - That party uses their dominant position to obtain an **unfair advantage**.
        
- **Who is in a dominant position?** The law presumes this in relationships like:
    
    - Parent and Child
        
    - Guardian and Ward
        
    - Doctor and Patient
        
    - Spiritual Guru and Devotee
        
    - Lawyer and Client
        
- **Nature:** It is about abusing a special relationship or the vulnerability of a person (e.g., someone who is old, sick, or in mental distress).
    
- **Example:** An elderly, sick man is completely dependent on his caregiver. The caregiver persuades the man to sell his house to her for a fraction of its value. The court will presume this was undue influence.
    

#### 3️⃣ Fraud (Section 17)

This involves **intentional deception**. It is a "knowing" lie or concealment used to trick someone into a contract.

- **Definition:** Fraud includes any of the following acts done **with the intent to deceive**:
    
    - Suggesting a fact that is **known to be false** (a direct lie).
        
    - **Active concealment** of a fact (e.g., painting over a huge crack in a wall to hide it).
        
    - Making a **promise with no intention of performing it** (e.g., buying goods on credit while already planning to file for bankruptcy).
        
    - Any other act "fitted to deceive."
        
- **Nature:** The key element is **intent (dishonesty)**. The person knows the truth and chooses to lie.
    
- **Example:** A sells a horse to B, falsely stating the horse is "perfectly sound." A _knows_ the horse is lame. This is fraud.
    

#### 4️⃣ Misrepresentation (Section 18)

This involves a false statement, but it is **unintentional**. It is an "innocent" falsehood.

- **Definition:** Misrepresentation occurs when a person makes a false statement **believing it to be true**.
    
- **Nature:** The key element is **innocence**. The person is not trying to cheat; they are simply mistaken.
    
- **Example:** A sells a horse to B, stating it is "perfectly sound." A _genuinely believes_ the horse is sound, but it has a hidden illness. This is misrepresentation, not fraud.
    

#### 5️⃣ Mistake (Sections 20, 21, 22)

This is where one or both parties are operating under a fundamental misunderstanding about the contract.

- **Bilateral Mistake (Section 20):** If **both parties** are mistaken about a **fact essential to the agreement** (e.g., the subject matter), the agreement is **VOID**.
    
    - **Example:** A agrees to sell his car to B. Unknown to both, the car was destroyed in a fire an hour before the contract. The contract is void.
        
- **Unilateral Mistake (Section 22):** If _only one_ party is mistaken about a fact, the contract is **NOT voidable**. A simple one-sided mistake does not invalidate a contract.
    

---

### 🔷 Legal Consequences: Void vs. Voidable

This is the most important legal outcome of this topic.

- **VOIDABLE (Section 19 & 19A):** When consent is caused by **Coercion, Undue Influence, Fraud, or Misrepresentation**, the contract is **voidable** at the option of the party whose consent was not free.
    
    - This means the innocent party has a _choice_:
        
        1. **Rescind (Cancel) the Contract:** They can end the contract and be restored to their original position (e.S., get their money back).
            
        2. **Affirm (Uphold) the Contract:** They can "waive" the defect and go through with the deal.
            
- **VOID (Section 20):** When consent is based on a **bilateral mistake of fact**, the agreement is **void** from the very beginning. There is no contract to "rescind" because no contract ever existed in the eyes of the law.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Key Distinctions for Exams:**
    
    - **Coercion vs. Undue Influence:** Coercion is **physical** pressure; Undue Influence is **psychological/relational** pressure.
        
    - **Fraud vs. Misrepresentation:** This is the most tested distinction. The _only_ difference is **intent**. Fraud is an _intentional_ lie (to deceive); Misrepresentation is an _innocent_ lie (believed to be true).
        
- **Silence is NOT Fraud:** As a general rule, just "keeping quiet" about a fact is not fraud. However, silence _can_ be fraud in two cases:
    
    1. When you have a **duty to speak** (e.g., in an insurance contract, you must disclose all known health issues).
        
    2. When your silence is **equivalent to speech** (e.g., B asks A, "If you don't say anything, I'll assume the horse is sound." A stays silent, knowing the horse is lame. This is fraud).
        
- **The Consequence is Everything:** Always end your analysis by stating the legal consequence. Is the contract **voidable** (giving the victim a choice) or **void** (a nullity from the start)?
