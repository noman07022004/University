## 📌 2.1: Offer (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the starting point of every contract. Without a valid offer, no contract can be formed. Mastering the distinction between an offer and a non-offer (like an invitation to treat) is the most fundamental practical skill in formation.  
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is one of the most frequently tested topics. Exam problem questions are _very_ likely to describe a communication and ask you to determine if it's a valid offer, a counter-offer, or merely an invitation to treat.


An **Offer** (or "Proposal") is the first of the two components required to form an agreement. The concept is defined in **Section 2(a) of the Contract Act, 1872**:

> "When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal."

In simple terms, an offer is a clear, definite promise by the "Offeror" to the "Offeree," which, if accepted, will create a legally binding contract.

The law determines if a statement is an offer not by what the person _secretly intended_ (their subjective intent), but by what a **"reasonable person"** in the offeree's position would have understood from their words and actions. This is the **Objective Principle** of contract law.

---

### 🔷 Key Rules of a Valid Offer

For a proposal to be a valid offer, it must meet several conditions:

1. **Must be Clear, Definite, and Certain:** The terms cannot be vague. An offer to sell "one of my houses" or "some oil" is too uncertain to be a valid offer (**Section 29**).
    
2. **Must be Communicated:** The offer must be brought to the knowledge of the offeree. A person cannot accept an offer they do not know exists. This is established in **Section 4**.
    
3. **Must Intend to Create a Legal Obligation:** The offer must be made with the _intention_ of being legally bound upon acceptance (this is why a social invitation is not a contract).
    

---

### 🔷 The Crucial Distinction: Offer vs. Invitation to Treat

This is the most critical concept to grasp in this subtopic. An "Invitation to Treat" (or "Invitation to Offer") is _not_ an offer. It is merely a first step in negotiations, inviting others to come and make an offer.

An **Offer** is a final, definite promise ("I **will** sell you my pen for 100 Taka"). An **Invitation to Treat** is an expression of willingness to negotiate ("My pen is for sale for around 100 Taka. Are you interested?").

The following are classic examples of **Invitations to Treat**, _not_ offers:

- **Display of Goods in a Shop:** When a shop displays items with price tags, this is an invitation. The **customer** makes the offer when they bring the item to the cash counter. The **shopkeeper** then accepts (or rejects) that offer.
    
    - _Landmark Case:_ _Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v. Boots Cash Chemists_.
        
- **Advertisements, Catalogues, and Price Lists:** These are generally seen as invitations to treat, allowing sellers to manage their stock.
    
- **Auctions:** The auctioneer's call for bids is an invitation to treat. Each **bid** is an offer. The auctioneer **accepts** the highest offer with the fall of the hammer.
    
- **Mere Statements of Price:** Stating the lowest price one would sell for is not an offer to sell at that price; it's just providing information.
    
    - _Landmark Case:_ _Harvey v. Facey_.
        

---

### 🔷 Types of Offer

1. **Specific Offer:** An offer made to a specific, identified person or group of people. Only that person or group can accept it.
    
2. **General Offer:** An offer made to the public or the "world at large." _Anyone_ who knows of the offer and performs the required conditions is considered to have accepted it.
    
    - _Landmark Case:_ **_Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co._** This case established that a general offer in an advertisement ("100 pounds reward to anyone who uses our product and gets the flu") can be a valid offer, and performing the act (using the product) is a valid acceptance.
        
3. **Express Offer:** An offer made in words (either spoken or written).
    
4. **Implied Offer:** An offer made by conduct or the circumstances of the case (e.g., a bus service running on a specific route implies an offer to transport passengers).
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Offer vs. ITT is Key:** For any exam problem, your first step is to analyze the communication. Is it an offer or an ITT? If it's an ITT, no contract can be formed by a simple "yes."
    
- **Communication is Everything:** Remember **Section 4**—the communication of an offer is complete when it comes to the _knowledge of the person to whom it is made_.
    
- **Offer vs. Statement of Intention:** An offer ("I will sell...") is different from a mere statement of future intention ("I am planning to sell..."). The first is binding on acceptance; the second is not.
    
- **Key Cases to Know:**
    
    - **_Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co._** (for general offers and acceptance by conduct).
        
    - **_Harvey v. Facey_** (for the distinction between an offer and a mere supply of information).
        
    - **_Pharmaceutical Society v. Boots_** or **_Fisher v. Bell_** (for display of goods as an invitation to treat).
