## 📌 2.3: Termination of offer (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. An offer doesn't last forever. This topic completes the "lifecycle" of an offer, explaining when and how it "dies." This is essential to know _when_ the offeree's power to accept has been cut off.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This is a common element in problem questions. A typical scenario involves an offeror trying to revoke their offer _after_ the offeree has posted an acceptance. You must be able to determine if the offer was still "alive" when the acceptance occurred.
    

**Termination of an offer** (or "revocation of a proposal") refers to the end or extinguishment of an offer. Once an offer is terminated, it is "dead" and can no longer be accepted to form a binding contract.

The offeror is the "master of his offer," but only up to a certain point. As per **Section 5 of the Contract Act, 1872**, "A proposal may be revoked at any time _before_ the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards."

This means an offeror can take back their offer at any point **until** the offeree has accepted it. For an acceptance by post, this means the offeror must communicate their revocation _before_ the offeree posts the acceptance letter.

---

### 🔷 Modes of Termination (How an Offer Ends)

**Section 6 of the Contract Act, 1872**, outlines the primary ways an offer can be terminated:

#### 1️⃣ By Notice of Revocation (Section 6(1)) 
This is the most direct method. The offeror (proposer) actively withdraws their offer by communicating a notice of revocation to the offeree (acceptor).

- **Key Rule:** The revocation only takes effect when it **comes to the knowledge of the offeree**.
    
- **Communication Rule (Section 4):** "The communication of a revocation is complete... as against the person to whom it is made, when it comes to his knowledge."
    
- **Example:** A offers to sell his car to B. B is thinking about it. A changes his mind and calls B, saying, "I withdraw my offer." The offer is terminated the moment B hears this. If B had _already_ posted an acceptance letter before A's call, it would be too late, and a contract would be formed.
    

#### 2️⃣ By Lapse of Time (Section 6(2)) 
An offer will automatically "expire" if not accepted.

- **Prescribed Time:** If the offeror set a deadline (e.g., "This offer is good until Friday"), it terminates automatically after that deadline.
    
- **Reasonable Time:** If no time is specified, the offer terminates after a "reasonable time." What is "reasonable" depends on the facts, such as the subject matter (e.g., an offer to sell fresh fruit has a very short reasonable time).
    

#### 3️⃣ By Failure of a Condition Precedent (Section 6(3)) 
An offer may be made "subject to" a certain condition being met. If that condition is not fulfilled, the offer lapses.

- **Example:** A offers to sell his car to B, "subject to the car passing its fitness test." If the car fails the test, the offer is automatically terminated.
    

#### 4️⃣ By Death or Insanity of the Offeror (Section 6(4)) 
An offer terminates upon the death or insanity of the offeror, _if_ the offeree comes to know of this fact **before** they accept the offer.

- **Crucial Point:** If the offeree accepts the offer in ignorance (without knowing) of the offeror's death or insanity, a valid contract may be formed, provided the contract is not one of a personal nature (e.g., a promise to paint a portrait).
    

#### 5️⃣ By Rejection (Counter-Offer) 
This is a critical mode, established by common law and linked to **Section 7** (Acceptance must be absolute).

- **Express Rejection:** The offeree can simply say "No," which terminates the offer.
    
- **Counter-Offer:** As discussed in Subtopic 2.2, a counter-offer (where the offeree tries to change the terms) is an _implied rejection_ of the original offer. It "kills" the original offer, which can no longer be accepted.
    
    - _Landmark Case:_ **_Hyde v. Wrench_** (where the £950 counter-offer destroyed the original £1,000 offer).

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Revocation Must Be Received:** This is the most important rule for exams. The postal rule _does not apply_ to revocation. A revocation letter is only effective when the offeree _receives_ it, not when the offeror _sends_ it.
    
- **The "Race":** Exam problems often create a "race" scenario. For example, A posts a revocation letter on Monday. B posts an acceptance letter on Tuesday. A's revocation arrives on Wednesday. A contract **was formed** on Tuesday because the acceptance was complete _before_ the revocation was communicated (received).
    
- **Rejection vs. Request for Information:** A counter-offer ("I'll pay £950") kills the offer. A simple request for information ("Would you consider £950?" or "Is credit available?") does _not_ kill the offer. The offeree can still accept the original offer.
