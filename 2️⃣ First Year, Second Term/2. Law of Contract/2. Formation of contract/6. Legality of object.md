## 📌 2.6: Legality of object (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This is the second half of the critical "legality" test. While 2.5 deals with the legality of the "price," this subtopic deals with the legality of the "purpose." It is essential for understanding the limits of "Freedom of Contract."
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This concept is almost always tested alongside "Unlawful Consideration" (2.5) and "Void Agreements" (2.7). Problem questions will describe a contract and ask you to determine if its _purpose_ makes it void and unenforceable.


This subtopic is the direct counterpart to "Unlawful Consideration." The law requires that _both_ the "price" (consideration) and the "purpose" (object) of an agreement must be lawful. If the **object** of the contract is illegal, immoral, or against public policy, the entire contract is **void**, even if the consideration is perfectly legal.

The term **"object"** simply means the **purpose or design** of the agreement.

The _entire_ legal framework for this is **Section 23 of the Contract Act, 1872**—the exact same section that governs unlawful consideration. It provides a single "blacklist" of what makes _either_ the consideration _or_ the object unlawful.

According to **Section 23**, the **object** of an agreement is unlawful if:

### 1️⃣ It is forbidden by law:

- This means the _purpose_ of the contract is to do something that a specific statute forbids.
    
- **Example:** A rents a house from B for a monthly rent (a legal consideration) for the _express purpose_ of using it as an illegal gambling den. Since the _object_ (running the gambling den) is forbidden by law, the agreement is void. B cannot sue A for the rent if he knew of the illegal purpose.
    

### 2️⃣ It is of such a nature that, if permitted, it would _defeat the provisions of any law_:

- This applies when the _purpose_ of the contract is to "get around" a law or undermine its intended effect.
    
- **Example:** A, a debtor, is about to face insolvency proceedings. He agrees to sell his car to B _for the object of_ hiding that asset from his official creditors. This agreement defeats the provisions of the **Insolvency Act**, so its object is unlawful, and the agreement is void.
    

### 3️⃣ It is fraudulent:

- The _purpose_ of the agreement is to commit a fraud on a third party.
    
- **Example:** A, B, and C agree to form a partnership _with the object of_ defrauding investors. The entire partnership agreement is void.
    

### 4️⃣ It involves or implies _injury to the person or property of another_:

- The _purpose_ of the contract is to cause harm.
    
- **Example:** A hires B to publish a book _for the object of_ defaming C. The purpose of the contract is to commit a tort (libel), which is an "injury to the person" of C. The contract is void.
    

### 5️⃣ The Court regards it as _immoral_:

- The _purpose_ of the agreement is considered morally reprehensible.
    
- **Example:** A lends money to B _for the known purpose of_ B using that money to pay for a divorce from her husband so she can marry A. In the past, this was held to be an agreement with an immoral object and was void.
    

### 6️⃣ The Court regards it as _opposed to public policy_:

- This is the broadest and most important category for "object." The _purpose_ of the agreement is considered harmful to the welfare of society.
    
- **Classic Examples:**
    
    - **Agreement to Trade with an Enemy:** A contract with a citizen of a country at war with Bangladesh is void as its object is against public policy.
        
    - **Agreement to Interfere with Justice:** A pays B, a witness, _for the purpose of_ B giving false testimony in court. The object is to pervert the course of justice, making the agreement void.
        
    - **Agreement in Restraint of Parental Rights:** A parent agreeing to transfer their parental rights to another for money is void, as the _object_ is against public policy.
        

---

### 🔷 Partial Illegality: What if the object is only partly illegal?

This is a crucial related concept governed by **Section 24 of the Contract Act, 1872**.

- **The Rule:** If an agreement contains several objects, and one object is legal while another is illegal, you must ask: **Can the legal part be separated from the illegal part?**
    
- **If Inseparable:** If the legal and illegal objects are "jumbled up" and cannot be separated, the **entire agreement is void**.
    
- **Example:** A promises to B that A will _both_ manage B's legal business _and_ run B's illegal gambling den, all for a single, combined salary of 100,000 Taka per month. Since the legal object (managing the business) cannot be separated from the illegal object (running the den), the _entire_ agreement is void. A cannot even sue for the portion of his salary related to the legal work.

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Section 23 is the Master List:** Remember that **Section 23** covers _both_ 2.5 (Unlawful Consideration) and 2.6 (Unlawful Object). The six categories are the same for both.
    
- **The Key Distinction:**
    
    - **Consideration** = The _price_ (e.g., the bribe money).
        
    - **Object** = The _purpose_ (e.g., the _goal_ of getting a public official to act corruptly).
        
- **Effect is VOID:** The consequence is always that the agreement is **void ab initio** (void from the very beginning). The court will not help either party.
    
- **Partial Illegality (Section 24):** Always remember Section 24. If an exam problem has an agreement with two purposes (one legal, one not), your analysis _must_ include whether they are separable. If not, the whole contract fails.
