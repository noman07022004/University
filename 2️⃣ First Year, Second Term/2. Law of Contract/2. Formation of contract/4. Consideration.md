## 📌 2.4: Consideration (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is one of the most critical and complex pillars of contract law. It is the "price" of the promise and the core of the "bargain theory" (from 1.4) that the **Contract Act, 1872,** is built upon. Without consideration, an agreement (other than a few exceptions) is void.  
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high importance. This is a guaranteed exam topic, both for direct essay questions ("Define consideration. What are the legal rules of consideration?") and as a central element in problem questions (e.g., "Did A provide valid consideration for B's promise?").
    


**Consideration** is the "price" paid for the other party's promise. It is the element of _quid pro quo_ ("something for something") that distinguishes a legally binding _bargain_ (a contract) from a non-binding _gift_ (a gratuitous promise).

The **Contract Act, 1872**, is built on the **Bargain Theory** (from 1.4). Its most fundamental rule, stated in **Section 25**, is that **"an agreement made without consideration is void."**

The Act defines consideration in **Section 2(d)**:

> "When, **at the desire of the promisor**, the **promisee or any other person** **has done** or abstained from doing, or **does** or abstains from doing, or **promises to do** or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise."

This complex definition contains all the key rules. Let's break them down.

---

### 🔷 The Legal Rules of Consideration

#### 1️⃣ Consideration Must Move "at the Desire of the Promisor" 
This is the most fundamental rule. The act or promise must be what the promisor _asked for_. Any action taken voluntarily, without any request from the promisor, cannot be valid consideration.

- **Example:** A sees B's house on fire and puts it out. A cannot later demand payment from B. Why? B never _desired_ or requested A's help (this would be a "non-gratuitous act" under Quasi-Contracts, Topic 3, but it's not _consideration_ for a contract).
    

#### 2️⃣ Consideration May Move from the "Promisee or Any Other Person" 
This is a **critical feature of Bangladeshi (and Indian) law**, and a major difference from English law.

- **Bangladeshi Law:** Consideration can be provided by _anyone_, even a stranger to the contract. If A promises to give B a car, _if C pays A 100,000 Taka_, the payment by C is valid consideration for A's promise to B.
    
- **English Law (for comparison):** English law has a "privity of consideration" rule, meaning consideration must move _from the promisee_ (the person receiving the promise). The Bangladeshi rule is much wider.
    

#### 3️⃣ Consideration May be Past, Present, or Future 
This is clear from the definition's wording:

- **Past Consideration ("has done"):** The consideration was an act performed _before_ the promise was made.
    
    - **Rule:** This is **valid** in Bangladesh _if_ it was done **"at the desire of the promisor."**
        
    - **Example:** A _requests_ B to find his lost wallet. B finds it. A _then_ promises to pay B 1,000 Taka. This is a valid contract. B's past act (finding the wallet) was done at A's request.
        
- **Present Consideration ("does"):** This is "executed" consideration. The act is performed at the same time as the promise (e.g., paying cash for goods at a shop).
    
- **Future Consideration ("promises to do"):** This is "executory" consideration. The parties exchange promises to do something in the future (e.g., A promises to deliver goods next week, and B promises to pay on delivery).
    

#### 4️⃣ Consideration Must be Real, not Illusory (Must have _some_ value)
Consideration must be something of value in the eyes of the law. It cannot be physically or legally impossible, uncertain, or completely illusory.

- **Example:** A promise to pay "a reasonable amount" is often too vague. A promise to "discover treasure by magic" is physically impossible and not real consideration.
    

#### 5️⃣ Consideration Need Not be Adequate 
This is a vital principle. The courts will not ask if you got a "good deal." As long as the consideration is _real_ (see point 4), it does not matter if it is "adequate."

- **Example:** If A freely agrees to sell his 1-crore Taka car to B for only 10,000 Taka, it is a valid contract. The court will not interfere. Adequacy is for the parties to decide, not the judge. (However, a grossly inadequate price _could_ be used as evidence to suggest that consent was not "free," as in Topic 2.8).
    

#### 6️⃣ Consideration Must Not be a Pre-existing Duty 
This is a complex but essential rule. Performing an act that you are _already_ legally bound to do is not "new" consideration for a _new_ promise.

- **Pre-existing Public Duty:** A police officer cannot claim a reward for catching a criminal because it is their public duty.
    
- **Pre-existing Contractual Duty:** This is the classic problem. If a sailor agrees to work on a ship for 5,000 Taka, and halfway through the voyage the captain promises "I'll pay you 1,000 Taka extra to stay," the captain's new promise is generally _not_ binding. The sailor is only doing what he was _already_ bound to do. (The modern English exception of "practical benefit" from _Williams v. Roffey_ is an advanced topic that softens this rule).
    

---

### 🔷 The Exception: When Consideration is Not Needed

The law recognizes that the strict rule of "no consideration, no contract" can be unfair.

#### 💠 Promissory Estoppel (The Reliance Principle): 
This is a key principle from equity (fairness), as discussed in 1.3. If:
    
1. A makes a clear and unambiguous promise to B (e.g., "I will accept less rent");
	
2. A intends for B to rely on that promise;
	
3. B _does_ reasonably rely on the promise (and pays the lower rent); ...then the law may **"estop"** (prevent) A from going back on their promise and demanding the full rent, even though B gave _no consideration_ for the promise to accept less.
        
#### 💠 Statutory Exceptions (Section 25): 
The Contract Act itself lists three key exceptions where a promise _without_ consideration **is** a valid contract:

1. A promise made in writing and registered, based on **natural love and affection** between parties in a near relation.
	
2. A promise to **compensate for a past voluntary act** (an act done _without_ the promisor's request).
	
3. A promise in writing to pay a **time-barred debt** (a debt that is too old to be sued for under the Limitation Act).
	

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Mnemonic for Rules:** Remember: "At the _desire_ of the promisor," "by _any person_," "can be _past, present, or future_," "must be _real_," but "need not be _adequate_."
    
- **Bangladesh vs. English Law:** Know the two big differences: (1) In Bangladesh, consideration can come from "any other person," not just the promisee. (2) In Bangladesh, past consideration _is_ valid, as long as it was at the promisor's request.
    
- **Consideration vs. Motive:** "Consideration" is the _legal_ price. "Motive" is the _emotional_ reason for the promise. Motive is not the same as consideration.
    
- **Section 25 is your key:** Always link a "no consideration" problem back to Section 25. If there's no consideration, the agreement is void _unless_ it fits one of the specific exceptions.
