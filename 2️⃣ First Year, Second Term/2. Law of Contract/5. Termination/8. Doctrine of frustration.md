## 📌 5.8: Doctrine of frustration (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the _theoretical heart_ of the previous subtopic. "Supervening impossibility" (5.7) is the _rule_ (what happens), while the "Doctrine of Frustration" is the _legal theory_ (why it happens). It explains the common law origins and the logic that courts use to relieve parties from unforeseen, radical changes in circumstances.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high importance. This is a major exam topic. You are expected to use the terms "frustration" and "Section 56" interchangeably and to cite the foundational cases (like _Taylor v. Caldwell_) that established the doctrine.


The **Doctrine of Frustration** is the common law principle that a contract is automatically discharged (terminated) when an unforeseen event makes performance **impossible** or **radically different** from what the parties originally intended, without any fault of either party.

This doctrine is the _legal theory_ behind **Section 56 of the Contract Act, 1872**. While Section 56 is the binding _law_ in Bangladesh, it is based on this doctrine developed by English courts.

### 🔷 Development of the Doctrine (The Key Cases)

1. ***The Old Rule (Absolute Liability):*** Originally, the law was extremely harsh (as seen in _Paradine v. Jane (1647)_). If you promised something, you had to do it, even if an unforeseen event made it impossible. You were expected to have provided for such events in the contract.
    
2. ***The Birth of the Doctrine (Taylor v. Caldwell (1863)):*** This is the foundational case.
    
    - **Facts:** Caldwell agreed to rent a music hall to Taylor for a series of concerts. Before the first concert, the music hall was destroyed by an accidental fire. Taylor sued for breach of contract.
        
    - **Held:** The court held the contract was frustrated. The court created a legal fiction: it "implied" a condition into the contract that the parties must have intended for the music hall to continue to exist. Since the subject matter was destroyed without fault, both parties were discharged.
        
3. ***Frustration of Purpose (Krell v. Henry (1903)):*** This case expanded the doctrine beyond physical impossibility.
    
    - **Facts:** Henry rented a flat from Krell for the _sole purpose_ of watching King Edward VII's coronation procession. The King fell ill, and the procession was postponed. Henry refused to pay the rent.
        
    - **Held:** The contract was frustrated. Although performance was _physically possible_ (Henry could still sit in the room), the _fundamental purpose_ (watching the coronation), which was known to both parties, had become impossible.
        

### 🔷 Bangladeshi Context: Section 56

In Bangladesh, the doctrine is not based on a fictional "implied term" as in _Taylor v. Caldwell_. The law is simpler and is stated directly in **Section 56**: the contract simply **becomes void** when the act becomes impossible or unlawful.

The Indian Supreme Court (in _Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram_) clarified that Section 56 is a positive, statutory rule, and the English theories are only useful for guidance. The test is whether the event "radically changes" the obligation, making it something different from what was contracted for.

### 🔷 Limits of the Doctrine (When Frustration does NOT apply)

A contract is **NOT** frustrated if:

- ***Commercial Hardship:*** Performance has just become _more expensive_ or _less profitable_ than expected. This is a normal business risk.
    
- ***Self-Induced Frustration:*** The "impossibility" was caused by the fault, negligence, or choice of the party trying to claim frustration.
    
- ***Foreseen Event*:** The "frustrating" event was something the parties _knew_ might happen and could have provided for in the contract.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Frustration vs. Supervening Impossibility:** These two terms are used interchangeably. "Supervening Impossibility" (5.7) describes _what happens_ (it becomes impossible). "Doctrine of Frustration" (5.8) is the _legal name_ for this concept.
    
- **Automatic Termination:** Frustration is not a choice. The contract becomes **void** (not voidable) automatically at the moment of the frustrating event.
    
- **Consequence (Restitution):** Since the contract becomes void, neither party can sue for breach. However, **Section 65** of the Contract Act applies: any party who received an _advantage_ (like an advance payment) **must restore** it to the other party.
