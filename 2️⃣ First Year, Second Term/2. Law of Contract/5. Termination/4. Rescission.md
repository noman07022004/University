## 📌 5.4: Rescission (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. Rescission is the legal "cancel button" for a contract. It's crucial to understand that this "cancellation" can happen in two ways: either the parties _mutually agree_ to it, or one party _earns the right_ to do it.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. The concept is central to problem questions. While "mutual rescission" is a simple idea, the _right_ to rescind (due to breach or lack of free consent) is a very common exam topic.


**Rescission** is the act of "unmaking" or "cancelling" a contract. When a contract is rescinded, it is legally extinguished, and the parties are, as much as possible, returned to the position they were in _before_ the contract was made (this is called "status quo ante").

It is critical to understand that rescission can occur in two different ways:

### 1️⃣ Rescission by Mutual Agreement (Section 62) 
This is the primary context of this subtopic, as it's grouped with Novation and Alteration under **Section 62 of the Contract Act, 1872**.

- **The Rule:** This section states, "If the parties to a contract agree to... **rescind** it, the original contract need not be performed."
    
- **What it is:** This is a simple, peaceful termination. The parties who mutually agreed to _make_ the contract later mutually agree to _cancel_ it. The "consideration" for this new agreement to rescind is the mutual release of each party from their promises.
    

### 2️⃣ Rescission as a Unilateral Right (A Remedy) 
This is a more common scenario in exams. Rescission is a _remedy_ that the law gives to an innocent party, allowing them to _unilaterally_ (by themselves) cancel the contract. This right arises in specific situations:

- **For Lack of Free Consent (Section 19 & 19A):** As we saw in Topic 2.8, if a contract is **voidable** because consent was caused by coercion, fraud, misrepresentation, or undue influence, the innocent party has the **option to rescind** the contract.
    
- **For Breach of Contract (Section 39 & 55):** When one party breaches a fundamental condition of the contract, the innocent party often gains the right to rescind.
    
    - **Anticipatory Breach (Section 39):** If a party refuses to perform _before_ the due date, the innocent party can rescind.
        
    - **Failure to Perform (Section 55):** If "time is of the essence" and a party is late, the innocent party can **rescind** the contract.
        

---

### 🔷 Consequences of Rescission

When a contract is rescinded (in either of the ways above), it is terminated _ab initio_ (from the beginning), as if it never existed.

The primary consequence is **restitution**, not damages.

- **Section 64:** When a voidable contract is rescinded, the party rescinding it _must_ restore any benefit they received.
    
- **Section 65:** When an agreement is _discovered_ to be void (or _becomes_ void), any party who received an advantage must restore it.
    

**Example:** A is tricked by B (fraud) into a contract to buy a horse. A pays a 10,000 Taka advance.

- A discovers the fraud and chooses to **rescind** the contract.
    
- The contract is cancelled.
    
- A must return the horse to B (if he took delivery).
    
- B must **restore** the 10,000 Taka advance to A. A can then _also_ sue for damages for the fraud.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Distinguish the Types:** In an exam, be very clear about _why_ the rescission is happening. Is it **mutual** (Section 62) or **unilateral** (e.g., Section 19, 39, 55)?
    
- **Rescission vs. Novation/Alteration:**
    
    - **Rescission:** Kills the contract.
        
    - **Novation:** Kills the contract and _replaces_ it with a new one.
        
    - **Alteration:** Modifies the _terms_ of the original contract, discharging the old version.
        
- **Restitution is the Goal:** The primary goal of rescission is not to punish anyone (that's damages) but to restore the "status quo ante"—the state of affairs _before_ the contract was made.
