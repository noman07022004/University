## 📌 5.7: Supervening impossibility (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is one of the most important concepts in termination. It's a key legal "exit ramp" that allows a party to be excused from performance when an unforeseen event makes the contract impossible to perform. It's the core of the **Doctrine of Frustration**.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high importance. This is a major, high-frequency exam topic. Problem questions will often describe a scenario (e.g., a hall burning down, a law changing) and ask you to determine if the contract is "frustrated" and what the consequences are.


**Supervening impossibility** (or "subsequent impossibility") is a primary method of terminating a contract by operation of law. It occurs when a contract, which was **valid and possible to perform** when it was made, _subsequently_ (at a later time) becomes **impossible** or **unlawful** to perform due to an unforeseen event beyond the control of the parties.

This entire concept is governed by the second and third paragraphs of **Section 56 of the Contract Act, 1872**.

**The Rule (Section 56, Para 2):**

> "A contract to do an act which, **after the contract is made**, becomes **impossible**, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, **unlawful**, becomes **void** when the act becomes impossible or unlawful."

This is the legal basis for the **Doctrine of Frustration (Subtopic 5.8)**, which is the common law term for this rule. When a supervening impossibility occurs, the contract is "frustrated" and automatically becomes **void**.

---

### 🔷 What counts as "Supervening Impossibility"?

For the contract to be frustrated, the impossibility must be absolute, not just a minor difficulty or a bad deal. The most common grounds are:

1. ***Destruction of the Subject Matter:*** This is the classic example. If the specific thing essential to the contract is destroyed (without the fault of either party), the contract is frustrated.
    
    - **Example (from Act, Illus. (b) to S. 56):** A and B contract to marry. Before the marriage, A goes mad. The contract becomes void.
        
    - **Example:** A contracts to rent a specific music hall to B for a concert. The day before the concert, the hall is destroyed by fire. The contract is **void**.
        
2. ***Change in the Law (Subsequent Illegality):*** The contract was legal when made, but a new law or government order makes its performance illegal.
    
    - **Example:** A (in Bangladesh) contracts to sell goods to B (in Country X). Before the goods are shipped, the government of Bangladesh passes a law banning all trade with Country X. The contract becomes **void**.
        
3. ***Non-occurrence of an Event (Failure of Object):*** The contract was based on the happening of a specific event, which is the core _purpose_ of the contract. If that event does not happen, the contract may be frustrated.
    
    - **Landmark Case:** **_Krell v. Henry_**. A man rented a room with a balcony for the _specific purpose_ of watching the King's coronation procession. The procession was postponed. The court held the contract was frustrated. The _purpose_ (watching the procession) was impossible, even though "renting the room" was still physically possible.
        
4. ***Death or Incapacity in Personal Contracts:*** As seen in Topic 4.2, if the contract depends on the _personal skill_ of the promisor (e.g., a contract to paint a portrait or sing), the death or serious illness of that person makes the contract **void**.
    

---

### 🔷 What does NOT count as "Impossibility"?

The law is very strict. The following difficulties do _not_ frustrate a contract:

- **Commercial Impossibility:** The contract has just become _more expensive_ or _less profitable_ to perform. A price increase in raw materials is a normal business risk, not a frustrating event.
    
- **Difficulty of Performance:** It's much _harder_ than the promisor thought, but not physically impossible.
    
- **Self-Induced Frustration:** The "impossibility" was caused by the _fault or negligence_ of the promisor. (e.g., you can't get out of a contract to rent a hall by burning it down yourself).
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Consequence is VOID:** A frustrated contract is not "voidable"; it becomes **VOID** automatically for _both_ parties. Neither party can sue the other for breach (because no one was at fault).
    
- **Restitution (Section 65):** Since the contract becomes void, **Section 65** applies. Any party who has received an _advantage_ (like an advance payment) under the contract **must restore** it to the person they received it from.
    
- **Initial vs. Supervening Impossibility:**
    
    - **Initial Impossibility (Section 56, Para 1):** An agreement to do an act "impossible in itself" (e.g., discover treasure by magic) is **void from the very beginning**.
        
    - **Supervening Impossibility (Section 56, Para 2):** The contract was _valid_ at the start but _becomes void later_. This is the focus of this subtopic.
