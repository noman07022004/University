## 📌 6.1: Amount of Damages (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the core principle of contractual remedies. It's the legal and economic "engine" for calculating what a breach _costs_. This subtopic explains the _Hadley v. Baxendale_ rule, which is the foundation of all damage calculations in common law.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high importance. This is a guaranteed exam topic. You will _always_ get problem questions that require you to apply **Section 73** to a set of facts, distinguish between ordinary and special damages, and calculate the amount owed.


This subtopic addresses the question: "How much money does the breaching party have to pay?" The goal of damages is **compensation**, not punishment. The innocent party is entitled to be placed in the _same financial position_ they would have been in if the contract had been performed correctly.

In Bangladesh, the entire rule for calculating this amount is contained in **Section 73 of the Contract Act, 1872**. This section is the statutory version of the famous 19th-century English case, **_Hadley v. Baxendale_**.

**Section 73** breaks damages down into two types (or "limbs") and one key exclusion.

### 1️⃣ Ordinary (General) Damages

This is the compensation the innocent party is _always_ entitled to.

- **The Rule:** The innocent party can recover compensation for any loss or damage "which **naturally arose in the usual course of things** from such breach."
    
- **What it means:** This refers to direct, obvious, and foreseeable losses. It's the loss that _any_ reasonable person would expect to happen from this type of breach.
    
- **The Test:** The test is **"reasonable foreseeability."** The breaching party is held responsible for the losses they _should have foreseen_ as a natural result of their failure.
    
- **Classic Example (Sale of Goods):** A contracts to sell 100 bags of rice to B at 500 Taka per bag, for delivery on January 1. A fails to deliver. On January 1, the market price for the same rice is 600 Taka per bag. B's "ordinary damage" is the extra 100 Taka per bag (the market price minus the contract price) it costs him to buy replacement rice. This is a natural and direct consequence of the breach.
    

### 2️⃣ Special (Consequential) Damages

This is for compensation for _additional_, non-obvious losses.

- **The Rule:** The innocent party can recover compensation for losses "which the parties **knew, when they made the contract, to be likely** to result from the breach of it."
    
- **What it means:** This refers to _indirect_ losses (like "loss of profit") that arise due to the innocent party's _special circumstances_.
    
- **The Test (The Crucial Part):** The innocent party can _only_ recover these special damages if the breaching party **knew about these special circumstances** at the time the contract was made. This "knowledge" makes the special loss _foreseeable_ to them.
    
- **The _Hadley v. Baxendale_ Example:** A mill owner's crankshaft broke. He gave it to a carrier (Baxendale) to deliver for repairs, stressing the need for speed. The carrier delayed delivery. The mill owner sued for the **lost profits** he suffered while his mill was shut down. The court **denied** the lost profits. Why? The carrier _did not know_ the mill was shut down. He didn't know these "special circumstances," so the lost profits were not foreseeable to him and were too "remote."
    

### 🔷 Remoteness and Mitigation

Section 73 also contains two implied principles that _limit_ the amount of damages:

1. ***Remoteness (The Exclusion):*** The Act explicitly states, "Such compensation is **not** to be given for any **remote and indirect loss** or damage...". This is the legal "cutoff point." The _Hadley v. Baxendale_ rules are the test for remoteness:
    
    - Ordinary damages are _not_ remote.
        
    - Special damages _are_ remote... _unless_ the breaching party had knowledge of them.
        
2. ***Duty to Mitigate (The "Explanation" to Section 73):*** The innocent party has a legal "duty to mitigate" their losses. This means they must take **reasonable steps** to _minimize_ the damage caused by the breach. They cannot sit back, let the losses pile up, and then claim the full, inflated amount.
    
    - **Explanation:** The "Explanation" to Section 73 states that in estimating the loss, "the means which existed of remedying the inconvenience caused by the non-performance... must be taken into account."
        
    - **Example:** In the rice example, B (the buyer) has a duty to go to the market on January 1 and buy rice at 600 Taka. He cannot wait a month for the price to go up to 1000 Taka and then sue for the higher amount.
        

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Section 73 is Your Formula:** For any damages problem, your formula is Section 73.
    
    1. Was there a breach?
        
    2. What are the "ordinary damages" that "naturally arose" (Limb 1)?
        
    3. Are there "special damages"? If yes, did the breaching party _know_ about the special circumstances at the time of the contract (Limb 2)?
        
    4. Did the innocent party _mitigate_ their loss?
        
- **Punitive Damages are Not Awarded:** Contract law does _not_ award "punitive" or "exemplary" damages (damages designed to punish the defendant). The _only_ goal is to compensate the victim.
    
- **Nominal Damages:** If a party's _legal right_ is violated (a breach is proven) but they have suffered **no actual financial loss**, the court may award "nominal damages" (e.g., 100 Taka) just to acknowledge the breach.
