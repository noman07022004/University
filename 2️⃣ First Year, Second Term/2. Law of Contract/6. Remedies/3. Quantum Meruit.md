## 📌 6.3: Quantum Meruit (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This is a unique and essential remedy. It's a "safety net" based on fairness (equity) that allows a person to get paid for work they've done, even when a valid contract _doesn't_ exist or has broken down. It's the primary remedy for "unjust enrichment."
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. This is a classic topic for a short note or as a remedy in a problem question where a contract is found to be void or is breached after partial performance.


**Quantum Meruit** is a Latin phrase that literally means **"as much as one has earned"** or **"what one deserves."**

It is a **restitutionary remedy**, not a contractual one. This means it is not a claim for _damages_ (which are based on a broken promise). It is a claim for a **reasonable sum** for services or goods provided, based on the principle of preventing **unjust enrichment**.

**Distinction from Damages:**

- ***Damages (Topic 6.1):*** This is a remedy for _breach of a valid contract_. The goal is to put the innocent party in the position they _would have been in_ (i.e., compensating for lost profits).
    
- ***Quantum Meruit:*** This is a remedy to recover the _reasonable value of work already performed_. It is often used when a valid contract _doesn't_ exist or no longer applies. The goal is to prevent the _other_ party from being unjustly enriched by getting free work.
    

### 🔷 When a Claim for Quantum Meruit Arises

A claim for quantum meruit can be made in several situations:

***1. When a Contract is Discovered to be Void (Section 65):*** If an agreement is discovered to be void (e.g., a bilateral mistake), or becomes void (e.g., frustration), any person who has performed work under that "contract" can claim reasonable compensation.

- **Example:** A hires B, a builder, to renovate a building. Both are unaware that the building was already destroyed by fire. B does some preliminary work (like site plans). The contract is void. B can claim quantum meruit for the reasonable value of the plans.
    

***2. For a Non-Gratuitous Act (Section 70):*** This is the statutory basis for quantum meruit in the **Contract Act, 1872**. As we saw in Topic 3.4, if a person does something for another _not intending to do it for free_ (non-gratuitously), and the other person _enjoys the benefit_, the person enjoying the benefit must pay reasonable compensation.

***3. When One Party Prevents Performance (Breach of Contract):*** If one party starts their work, and the other party then breaches the contract or prevents them from finishing, the innocent party has a choice:

- a) Sue for damages for the breach.
    
- b) Rescind the contract and sue on quantum meruit for the value of the work they _have already done_.
    
- **Example:** A contractor is hired to build a house. After completing half the foundation, the owner (wrongfully) kicks them off the site. The contractor can sue on quantum meruit for the reasonable value of the foundation work.
    

***4. When a Contract is Divisible:*** If a contract is divisible into separate parts (e.g., a contract to deliver goods in installments), and a party performs some parts but not all, they can claim quantum meruit for the parts they _have_ performed.

***5. When a Contract is for a Lump Sum but is Performed Badly:*** If a contract is for a lump sum (e.g., "Build this whole house for 10 Lakh Taka") and the builder completes it, but _badly_, the builder can still claim the lump sum (on quantum meruit) _minus_ the cost the owner has to pay to fix the defects.

***6. When No Price is Fixed:*** If parties agree for work to be done but do not fix a price, the law implies a promise to pay a _reasonable_ sum. This "reasonable sum" is recovered via a claim for quantum meruit.

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Basis is "Unjust Enrichment":** Always remember this. The remedy exists to stop one party from getting a "free ride" at the other's expense.
    
- **Quasi-Contract:** This remedy is firmly rooted in the concept of **Quasi-Contract (Topic 3)**. It is an obligation _imposed by law_ in the absence of a clear contractual promise to pay.
    
- **Reasonable Value:** The court will determine the "reasonable value" of the work. This may or may not be the same as the price listed in the (now void or terminated) contract.
