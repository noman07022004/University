## 📌 6.4: Specific performance (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is an absolutely critical remedy. It is the primary _equitable_ remedy and the main alternative to _legal_ remedies (like damages). You must understand _when_ the court will force a party to do what they promised, rather than just pay money.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high importance. Problem questions involving the sale of land, rare art, or unique items are _specifically designed_ to test this concept. The question will always be whether the innocent party can get damages _or_ demand specific performance.


**Specific Performance** is an equitable, _discretionary_ remedy. It is a court order that commands a breaching party to **"do the very act which he is under an obligation to do."**

Instead of awarding monetary compensation (damages) for the breach, the court orders the promisor to **actually perform** their part of the contract as promised.

**Governing Law in Bangladesh:** This remedy is **not** governed by the Contract Act, 1872. It is governed by the **Specific Relief Act, 1877**.

---

### 🔷 The Key Principle: When is Specific Performance Granted?

The court does **not** grant specific performance for every contract. It is a special remedy granted _only_ when **monetary compensation (damages) is not an adequate relief.**

**Section 12 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877,** outlines when the court will _presume_ that damages are inadequate:

#### 1️⃣ Contracts for the Sale of Land (Immovable Property):

- **The Rule:** The court _always_ presumes that the breach of a contract to transfer immovable property **cannot be adequately relieved by money**.
    
- **Reason:** Land is considered legally "unique." No two plots of land are identical. Even if the buyer is given money, they cannot go out and buy that _exact_ same property.
    
- **Result:** This is the most common and important example. Specific performance is the _usual_ remedy for a breach of a contract for the sale of land.
    

#### 2️⃣ Contracts for Unique or Rare Goods (Movable Property):

- **The Rule:** The court _may_ grant specific performance for movable property (goods) _if_ the goods are **"not an ordinary article of commerce."** This means the item is rare, unique, or has special value to the buyer, and a replacement cannot be easily bought in the market.
    
- **Examples:** A contract to sell a one-of-a-kind painting by a famous artist, a specific antique, or shares in a private company that are not publicly traded.
    
- **Not Granted:** It will _not_ be granted for ordinary goods (like rice, oil, or a common car) because the buyer can easily take the damage money and buy an identical item from someone else.
    

---

### 🔷 When is Specific Performance _Not_ Granted?

**Section 21 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877,** provides a clear list of contracts that **cannot** be specifically enforced. The most important ones are:

#### 1️⃣ Contracts Where Damages are Adequate (Section 21(a)):

- This is the flip side of the main rule. If the court finds that money _is_ an adequate remedy, it will _not_ grant specific performance.
    

#### 2️⃣ Contracts of a Personal Nature (Section 21(b)):

- **The Rule:** The court will **never** grant specific performance for a contract that depends on the **"personal qualifications or volition of the parties."**
    
- **Reason:** This would be "involuntary servitude" or "slavery." Furthermore, the court cannot supervise the _quality_ of the performance. (e.g., How can a court force a singer to sing "well"?).
    
- **Example:** A contracts to sing at B's theatre. If A refuses, B _cannot_ get specific performance to force A to sing. B's only remedy is to sue for damages. (However, as we will see in 6.5, B _can_ get an injunction to stop A from singing _elsewhere_).
    

#### 3️⃣ Contracts Requiring Constant Supervision (Section 21(g)):

- **The Rule:** The court will not enforce a contract if its performance involves a **"continuous duty extending over a longer period than three years."**
    
- **Reason:** The court cannot act as a project manager. It is not feasible for the court to supervise a complex, long-term task like a 5-year construction project to ensure it is done correctly.
    

#### 4️⃣ Contracts That are Vague or Uncertain (Section 21(c)):

- The court cannot order a party to "do" something if the contract's terms are so uncertain that the court doesn't know _what_ to order.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Equitable and Discretionary:** Specific performance is an _equitable_ remedy, meaning it is based on fairness, not an absolute right. **Section 22** of the Act states that the court's power is **discretionary**. The court can refuse it if it would cause _unfair hardship_ to the defendant.
    
- **Damages vs. Specific Performance:** This is the key comparison.
    
    - **Damages (Legal Remedy):** You have a _right_ to it. Available for _all_ contracts.
        
    - **Specific Performance (Equitable Remedy):** You _ask_ for it. It's _discretionary_. Available _only_ when damages are inadequate.
        
- **Landmark Case (Test for Adequacy):** _Falcke v. Gray_ (a case involving rare "China jars") is a classic example of when specific performance _was_ granted for a movable item because it was unique and damages were inadequate.
