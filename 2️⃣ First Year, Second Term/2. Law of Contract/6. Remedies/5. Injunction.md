## 📌 6.5: Injunction (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. An injunction is the "sister" remedy to specific performance. It's a powerful and flexible tool that courts use to prevent harm. It's especially important for understanding how a court can enforce a promise _not_ to do something.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. This is a common part of problem questions, especially those involving personal service contracts (like an actor or musician) where specific performance is not allowed.


An **injunction** is an **equitable, discretionary** remedy. It is a court order that _prohibits_ a person from doing a certain act (a **prohibitory injunction**) or, less commonly, _commands_ them to perform a certain act to restore the status quo (a **mandatory injunction**).

**Governing Law in Bangladesh:** Like specific performance, injunctions are not governed by the Contract Act. They are governed by the **Specific Relief Act, 1877** (specifically **Part III, Sections 52-57**).

- **Discretionary Remedy:** Like all equitable remedies, an injunction is not granted as a right. The court grants it at its discretion if it finds that monetary damages are not an adequate remedy.
    
- **Types of Injunctions (Section 53):**
    
    1. **Temporary Injunction:** Granted _during_ the lawsuit to preserve the "status quo" until the case is decided.
        
    2. **Perpetual (or Permanent) Injunction:** Granted by the final decree _after_ the trial, permanently stopping the person from doing the act.
        

### 🔷 Application in Contract Law

In contract law, an injunction is the primary tool to enforce a **negative promise** (a promise _not_ to do something).

The most famous application is in **personal service contracts**.

- **The Problem:** As we saw in **Topic 6.4**, a court _cannot_ grant specific performance of a personal service contract (e.g., it can't force a musician to sing) (Specific Relief Act, Section 21(b)).
    
- **The Solution:** If the contract includes a **negative promise** (e.g., "and the musician promises _not_ to sing for anyone else"), the court _can_ issue an **injunction** to enforce that negative promise.
    

**Landmark Case: _Lumley v. Wagner_ (1852)**

- **Facts:** A singer, Wagner, was contracted to sing _exclusively_ at Lumley's theatre for a season. She then breached this and agreed to sing at a rival theatre.
    
- **Held:** The court admitted it _could not_ grant **specific performance** to force her to sing for Lumley (that's a personal service). However, the court **did** grant an **injunction** to _prohibit_ her from singing at the rival theatre.
    
- **Logic:** This did not force her to sing for Lumley (she could choose not to sing at all), but it did enforce the negative part of her promise that she had breached.
    

This principle is codified in **Section 57 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877**. It states that if a contract has both a positive and a negative promise, the fact that the court cannot enforce the positive promise (like "to sing") does not stop it from granting an injunction to enforce the negative one (like "not to sing elsewhere").

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Specific Performance vs. Injunction:**
    
    - **Specific Performance:** Enforces a _positive_ promise ("Do this act").
        
    - **Injunction:** Enforces a _negative_ promise ("Do not do this act").
        
- **When Damages are Inadequate:** An injunction is only granted if the breach would cause **irreparable harm** that money cannot fix. For example, stopping a person from releasing trade secrets (a breach of a non-disclosure agreement) is a common use, as the damage from the release cannot be undone with money.
    
- **Bar to Injunction (Section 56):** The Specific Relief Act lists several cases where an injunction _cannot_ be granted. The most important is **Section 56(f)**: an injunction cannot be granted "to prevent the breach of a contract the performance of which would not be specifically enforced."
    
    - **Reconciling with _Lumley v. Wagner_:** This seems like a contradiction, but it's resolved by Section 57, which creates a special exception for contracts that have _both_ a positive and a negative part.
