## 📌 6.2: Liquidated Damages and Penalty (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This topic is a critical, practical aspect of commercial contracts. It explains how parties _try_ to control the financial consequences of a breach in advance, and more importantly, how the law _limits_ their ability to do so.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** High importance. This is a classic exam topic. You are very likely to get a problem question with a clause stating "In case of breach, Party A must pay 1 crore Taka," and you will have to apply **Section 74** to determine the legal effect.


This subtopic covers what happens when the parties themselves, in the contract, specify a sum of money to be paid in the event of a breach.

In English common law, a strict distinction is made between two types of these clauses:

1. ***Liquidated Damages:*** This is a **genuine, good-faith pre-estimate** of the _actual loss_ that the parties expect to suffer from a breach. Because it's a fair and reasonable attempt to set compensation, English courts will generally **enforce** this clause.
    
2. ***Penalty:*** This is a sum that is **excessive, unconscionable, or disproportionate** to any possible loss. Its purpose is not to compensate, but to **punish** the breaching party or _terrorize_ them into performing (this is called _in terrorem_). English courts will **not** enforce a penalty clause; they will strike it down and calculate the actual damages (as per Section 73).

### 🔷 The Bangladeshi Context: Section 74 (The Key Rule)

This is the most important part of the topic for your course. The **Contract Act, 1872,** in **Section 74**, takes a different and much simpler approach.

**The Rule:** In Bangladesh (and India), the law **makes no distinction between a "penalty" and "liquidated damages."** It treats them both the _same way_.

**Section 74** states:

> "When a contract has been broken, if a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach... the party complaining of the breach is entitled, **whether or not actual damage or loss is proved to have been caused thereby**, to receive... **reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named**..."

**Breaking Down Section 74:**

1. **Irrelevant Labels:** It doesn't matter if the contract calls the sum a "penalty" or "liquidated damages." The court ignores the label.
    
2. **No Proof of Loss Required:** The innocent party is entitled to compensation "whether or not actual damage or loss is proved." This is a huge advantage, as they don't have to go through the difficult process of proving their _actual_ loss (as required under Section 73).
    
3. **The Court Decides "Reasonable Compensation":** The court will not automatically award the full amount. It will award what it considers to be **"reasonable compensation."**
    
4. **The Stipulated Sum is a "Cap":** The "reasonable compensation" awarded by the court **cannot be more** than the amount named in the contract. The stipulated sum acts as a _maximum limit_ or "cap" on the damages.
    

**Example (from Act, Illus. (a) to S. 74):**

- A contracts with B to pay B 1,000 Taka if he fails to pay B 500 Taka on a given day.
    
- A fails to pay.
    
- **Analysis:** This is clearly a "penalty" (a 1,000 Taka fee for being late on a 500 Taka payment).
    
- **Result (under Section 74):** B cannot claim the full 1,000 Taka. B is entitled to **"reasonable compensation not exceeding 1,000 Taka,"** which the court will decide (e.g., the 500 Taka plus reasonable interest).
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Section 73 vs. Section 74:**
    
    - **Section 73 (Amount of Damages):** Use this when the contract is _silent_ on the amount. The innocent party must _prove_ their actual, foreseeable loss (ordinary/special damages).
        
    - **Section 74 (This Topic):** Use this when the contract _names a sum_. The innocent party _doesn't_ have to prove their actual loss, and the court will award "reasonable compensation" _up to_ the named sum.
        
- **Exception (Section 74):** The only exception is for government "bail-bonds" or public duties, where the court _will_ award the full penalty amount.
    
- **Acceleration Clause:** A clause stating that on a default of one installment, the _entire_ remaining loan balance becomes due _is not_ a penalty and is perfectly enforceable.
