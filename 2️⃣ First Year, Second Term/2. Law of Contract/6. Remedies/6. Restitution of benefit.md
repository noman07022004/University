## 📌 6.6: Restitution of benefit (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. "Restitution" is a foundational concept of justice, separate from "damages." It's based on the principle of preventing **unjust enrichment**. You must understand this to see how a court "resets" a situation when a contract is void or rescinded.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. This is a common part of problem questions, especially those involving void agreements (like frustration or impossibility) or voidable contracts (like fraud). The question will be, "The contract is void. What happens to the 50,000 Taka advance payment?"



**Restitution** is a legal remedy that means **"to restore."** Its purpose is _not_ to compensate for a loss (like damages), but to **prevent the unjust enrichment of one party** by forcing them to **return a benefit** they have received.

The goal of restitution is to put the parties back in the position they were in _before_ the contract was made (the "status quo ante").

In the **Contract Act, 1872**, this principle is primarily captured in two key sections:

### 1️⃣ For VOIDABLE Contracts (Section 64)

This section applies when an innocent party rightfully **rescinds** (cancels) a **voidable contract** (e.g., a contract formed by fraud, coercion, or undue influence).

- **The Rule (Section 64):** "The party rescinding a voidable contract shall, if he have received any benefit thereunder from another party to such contract, **restore such benefit**, so far as may be, to the person from whom it was received."
    
- **Example:**
    
    - A is tricked by B's **fraud** into buying a horse for 100,000 Taka.
        
    - A pays B the money and takes the horse.
        
    - A discovers the fraud and decides to **rescind** the contract.
        
    - **Restitution (Section 64) applies:** A must **restore** the horse to B, and B must **restore** the 100,000 Taka to A.
        

### 2️⃣ For VOID Agreements (Section 65)

This section applies when an agreement is **discovered to be void** _after_ it was made, or when a valid contract **becomes void** _later_.

- **The Rule (Section 65):** "When an agreement is discovered to be void, or when a contract becomes void, any person who has **received any advantage** under such agreement or contract is **bound to restore it**, or to make compensation for it, to the person from whom he received it."
    
- **This applies to two scenarios:**
    
    1. **"Discovered to be void":** The contract was void from the beginning (_void ab initio_), but the parties didn't know it.
        
        - **Example (from Act, Illus. (a) to S. 65):** A pays B 1,000 Taka for B's promise to marry A's daughter, C. At the time of the promise, C is already dead. The agreement is **void**, but the parties didn't know. B must **restore** the 1,000 Taka to A.
            
    2. **"Becomes void":** The contract was valid when made, but later became void.
        
        - **Example (Frustration):** A contracts to sing for B for 1,000 Taka, which B pays in advance. On the day of the concert, A is too sick to sing. The contract has **become void** (by supervening impossibility). A must **restore** the 1,000 Taka advance to B.
            

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Restitution vs. Damages:** This is the key distinction.
    
    - **Damages (Section 73):** Compensates for a _loss_ (e.g., lost profits). It looks _forward_ to where you _would have been_.
        
    - **Restitution (Section 64/65):** Returns a _benefit_ to prevent _unjust enrichment_. It looks _backward_ to restore you to where you _were_.
        
- **Restitution vs. Quantum Meruit:** These are very similar. "Restitution" is the _principle_ (preventing unjust enrichment). "Quantum Meruit" is one of the _methods_ of claiming restitution, specifically for the reasonable value of _services_ performed.
    
- **Does not apply to Minors:** The principle of restitution under Section 65 generally does **not** apply against a minor. A minor who enters a void agreement and receives a benefit (e.g., takes a loan) is not bound to restore it. This is part of the law's strict protection of minors.
