## 📌 3.2: Incapable persons (Overall: ⭐⭐⭐)

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. This subtopic is a direct application of the "unjust enrichment" principle. It shows how the law provides a remedy for suppliers _even when_ a valid contract is impossible (due to the person's incapacity).
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. This is a common topic for a short note or a simple problem question (e.g., "A supplies X to a minor. Can A recover the cost?"). It's a specific, self-contained rule.
    

This subtopic refers to the quasi-contractual obligation for **supplying "necessaries"** to a person who is legally incapable of contracting. The governing law is **Section 68 of the Contract Act, 1872**.

Recall from Topic 1.5 that an agreement with an "incapable person" (like a **minor** or a **person of unsound mind**) is **void ab initio**. This creates a problem: what if someone provides essential goods or services (like food or medical care) to a minor? The supplier cannot sue under contract law because the contract is void.

Section 68 provides the solution based on quasi-contract (unjust enrichment).

**The Rule (Section 68):** The law states that if a person supplies **"necessaries"** to an incapable person (or to someone whom the incapable person is legally bound to support, like their child), the supplier is entitled to be **reimbursed** for the value of those necessaries.

---

### 🔷 Key Components of Section 68

#### 1️⃣ Who is an "Incapable Person"? 
This refers to anyone who is not competent to contract under **Section 11**:

- A **minor** (under 18 in Bangladesh).
    
- A **person of unsound mind**.
    
- Any other person disqualified by law.
    

#### 2️⃣ What are "Necessaries"? 
This is the most important part. "Necessaries" is a flexible term. It doesn't just mean bare essentials for survival. It includes goods and services that are **"suited to his condition in life."**

- **This means:** What is a "necessary" for a wealthy minor might be a "luxury" for a poor minor.
    
- **Examples of Necessaries:**
    
    - Food and clothing.
        
    - Housing/shelter.
        
    - Medical care.
        
    - Basic education or vocational training.
        
- **Examples of Non-Necessaries:**
    
    - Luxury goods (e.g., jewelry, sports cars).
        
    - Goods for trading (if the minor is running a business).
        
    - A loan of money (though money _used_ to buy necessaries can sometimes be recovered).
        

#### 3️⃣ The Liability: Reimbursement from Property 
This is a critical distinction. The incapable person is **not personally liable**. The supplier cannot have the minor arrested or force them to work to pay the debt.

The law _only_ allows the supplier to be reimbursed from the **"property" or "estate"** of the incapable person.

- **If the minor has property:** The supplier can file a suit, and the court can order the property to be sold to pay for the necessaries.
    
- **If the minor has no property:** The supplier is out of luck. They cannot recover the money.
    

This rule fairly balances two competing goals:

- It **protects** minors from being exploited in bad bargains.
    
- It **encourages** people to provide essential goods and services to minors, knowing they have _some_ legal way to get paid back (if the minor has the means).
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Tips

- **Not a Contract:** This is _not_ a contractual right. The supplier is not suing on the (void) contract. They are suing on the _statutory, quasi-contractual right_ given by Section 68.
    
- **"Necessaries" is a Question of Fact:** In an exam, you may need to argue whether an item is a "necessary" based on the (hypothetical) person's station in life.
    
- **Property is Key:** The most important point for any problem question is that recovery is _only_ possible if the incapable person has property.
