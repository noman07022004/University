## 📌 1.3 Principles of contract ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:** This is a 5-star topic. These principles are the fundamental theories and the "spirit" behind the specific rules of the Contract Act. Understanding them helps you interpret every other section and analyze _why_ the law is structured the way it is. They are the essential building blocks of all contract law.

The law of contract is not just a set of rules but is built upon several core philosophical and legal principles. These are the foundational ideas that give the law its purpose and direction.

### 1️⃣ Freedom of Contract:

- **Principle:** This is the classical, 19th-century cornerstone of contract law. It holds that individuals are **free (at liberty) to enter into any contract they choose, on whatever terms they deem fit.** The court's role, under this principle, is not to ask if a contract is "fair" or a "good deal." The court's only job is to **enforce the bargain the parties freely made**.
    
- **Limitation:** This freedom is not absolute. The law places major restrictions:
    
    - The parties must have **legal capacity** (not be minors, etc.) and must have given **free consent** (no coercion, fraud, etc.).
        
    - The contract's terms must be **legal**. The "freedom" does not extend to making contracts that are illegal, immoral, or against public policy (as detailed in **Section 23** of the Contract Act, 1872).
        
- **Modern View:** This principle has been significantly limited in modern times (as seen in Subtopic 1.2) by consumer protection laws and regulations on standard form contracts to protect weaker parties.
    

### 2️⃣ Sanctity of Contract:

- **Principle:** This principle is often expressed by the Latin maxim **_pacta sunt servanda_** ("agreements must be kept"). It means that once a valid contract is formed, it is **sacred, binding, and must be honored.**
    
- **Function:** This creates **certainty and predictability** in business and personal dealings. People can rely on promises, knowing the law will back them up. This principle is the entire reason for **Main Topic 6: Remedies**. If contracts weren't "sacred," there would be no legal basis to sue for damages.
    
- **Limitation:** The main exception is the **Doctrine of Frustration** (Subtopic 5.8), where the law _does_ excuse parties from performance if an unforeseen event makes the contract impossible.
    

### 3️⃣ Consensus Ad Idem ("Meeting of the Minds"):

- **Principle:** This is the technical legal term for a true "agreement." It means that for a contract to be valid, the parties must have **agreed to the _same thing_ in the _same sense_.** There must be a genuine meeting of the minds.
    
- **Function:** This is the foundation of **Main Topic 2: Formation**. An "offer" (2.1) and an "acceptance" (2.2) are the legal tools used to _prove_ that a _consensus ad idem_ was reached. If there is no _consensus ad idem_ due to a fundamental mistake, no contract is formed.
    

### 4️⃣ The Objective Principle (The Test for Agreement):

- **Principle:** This is the _practical test_ that courts use to determine if a _consensus ad idem_ (Principle 3) actually happened. The law is not concerned with what a party _secretly thought_ (their **subjective intention**). Instead, it looks at what a **"reasonable person"** would have concluded from the parties' external words and actions (their **objective intention**).
    
- **Function:** This principle prevents parties from escaping a contract by simply claiming, "I didn't really mean it." If your conduct _objectively_ looked like a valid offer or acceptance, you will be bound. This is the lens through which "Offer" (2.1) and "Acceptance" (2.2) are analyzed.
    

### 5️⃣ The Reliance Principle (Promissory Estoppel):

- **Principle:** This is a crucial _equitable_ principle that provides a basis for enforcing a promise _even if there is no consideration_. It states that if:
    
    1. A makes a clear and unambiguous promise to B;
        
    2. A intends for B to rely on that promise;
        
    3. B _does_ reasonably rely on the promise and, as a result, changes their position (often to their detriment); ...then the law will **"estop"** (prevent) A from going back on their promise, as it would be unjust to do so.
        
- **Function:** This principle is a major modern exception to the strict rule of **Consideration (Subtopic 2.4)**. It's based on fairness and preventing injustice, not on enforcing a "bargain."
    

### 6️⃣ Privity of Contract:

- **Principle:** This principle states that a contract creates rights and obligations **only for the parties who are "privy" (party to) the agreement.**
    
- **Function:** It means that a **third party** (a stranger to the contract) **cannot sue** on the contract to enforce a benefit, nor can they be **held liable** for an obligation under it.
    
- **Bangladeshi Context:** This is a classic English common law rule that is generally followed in Bangladesh. For example, if A promises B that A will pay 1,000 Taka to C, C (the third party) cannot sue A if A fails to pay. Only B (the party to the contract) can sue A.
    
- **Limitation:** This principle has several complex exceptions, such as in cases of trust, agency (Topic 9), or when a contract is made for the benefit of a family member.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Extra Information

- **Interrelation:** These principles are all connected. You need a **Consensus Ad Idem** (measured _objectively_) between parties with **Freedom of Contract** to create an agreement that has **Sanctity of Contract**, which is only enforceable by the parties who have **Privity of Contract**.
    
- **Bargain vs. Reliance:** This is a key theoretical split. Most contracts (like in the Contract Act, 1872) are based on the **Bargain Principle** (Offer + Acceptance + Consideration). The **Reliance Principle** (Promissory Estoppel) is a separate, equitable basis for enforcing a promise to prevent injustice.
    
- **Basis for the Act:** These principles, all derived from English Common Law, form the ideological basis for the specific sections of the Contract Act, 1872.
