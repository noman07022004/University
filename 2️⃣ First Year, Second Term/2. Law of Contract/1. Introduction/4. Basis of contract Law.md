## 📌 1.4 Basis of contract Law ⭐⭐⭐⭐

> **Rating Justification:**
> **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Very important. This topic explains the core _philosophical and economic reasons_ why contract law exists. Mastering this gives you a much deeper, academic understanding of _why_ the rules are the way they are.
> **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** Moderately important. This is a classic topic for a theoretical **essay question or a short note** (e.g., "Explain the theoretical basis of contract law"). It is unlikely to be the main focus of a problem-based question.


"Basis of Contract Law" refers to the core theories that explain _why_ society and the legal system choose to make certain promises legally binding. Different theories have emerged, each providing a different justification for legal intervention.

### 1️⃣ The "Will Theory" (Autonomy)

- **Basis:** This is the classical 19th-century view, closely linked to the principle of **Freedom of Contract**.
    
- **Argument:** The law enforces contracts because a contract is an expression of the human **free will** and **autonomy**. When two parties freely consent to an agreement, the law simply gives effect to their joined wills. The state's job is to act as an umpire to enforce what the parties have already willed into existence.
    
- **Limitation:** This theory struggles to explain standard form contracts (where there is no real negotiation or "meeting of wills") or why the law restricts freedom by, for example, making illegal agreements void.
    

### 2️⃣ The "Bargain Theory" (Consideration)

- **Basis:** This is the primary basis of the English Common Law and, consequently, the **Contract Act, 1872**.
    
- **Argument:** The law does _not_ enforce all promises. It only enforces **bargains**. A promise is only enforceable if it was "bought" by a "price." This price is what we call **Consideration** (Subtopic 2.4). This theory explains why a simple, one-sided promise of a gift (a "gratuitous promise") is generally _not_ a contract.
    
- **Bangladeshi Context:** The Contract Act, 1872, is built on this theory. **Section 25** explicitly states that "an agreement made without consideration is void," subject to a few exceptions. This shows that the "bargain" is the default basis for a contract in Bangladesh.
    

### 3️⃣ The "Reliance Theory" (Promissory Estoppel)

- **Basis:** This theory, as we discussed in 1.3, provides a separate basis for enforcement, rooted in justice and equity.
    
- **Argument:** The law should enforce a promise _not_ because it was a bargain, but because the **promisee relied on it** and would suffer an unjust detriment if the promisor were allowed to go back on their word. The basis is preventing the injustice caused by _reliance_.
    
- **Function:** This theory explains the doctrine of **Promissory Estoppel**, which is a vital equitable tool used by courts when the strict "bargain theory" (consideration) would lead to an unfair result.
    

### 4️⃣ The Economic Basis (Efficiency)

- **Basis:** This is the dominant modern justification for contract law.
    
- **Argument:** Contracts are the "engine" of a market economy. The law enforces contracts because it is **economically efficient** to do so. Legal enforcement:
    
    - **Creates Certainty:** Businesses can plan for the future, knowing their supply and sales agreements are binding.
        
    - **Allocates Risk:** Parties can negotiate to decide who bears the risk of future events (e.g., price changes, bad weather).
        
    - **Reduces Transaction Costs:** Parties don't have to waste resources investigating if the other side is "trustworthy"—they can trust the legal system to provide a remedy.
        
    - **Promotes Trade:** It allows strangers to do business with each other safely.
        

### 5️⃣ The Moral Basis (Sanctity)

- **Basis:** This is the simplest justification, linked to the principle of **Sanctity of Contract**.
    
- **Argument:** The law enforces contracts because it is **morally right** to keep one's promises. This theory holds that breaking a promise is an ethical wrong, and the law should reflect this moral position.
    

---

### 🔔 Important Notes & Extra Information

- **No Single Theory:** No single theory explains all of contract law. The **Bargain Theory** explains the core of the Contract Act. The **Reliance Theory** explains promissory estoppel. The **Economic Basis** explains _why_ we have the entire system in the first place.
    
- **Dominance of the Bargain:** For your course, the most important basis to associate with the Contract Act, 1872, is the **Bargain Theory**. The central role of Consideration (Section 2(d) and Section 25) is the best evidence for this.
    
- **Basis for Quasi-Contracts:** None of these theories fully explain "Quasi-Contracts" (Topic 3). Quasi-contracts have their _own_ basis: the principle of **preventing "Unjust Enrichment,"** which is another separate justification for legal intervention.
