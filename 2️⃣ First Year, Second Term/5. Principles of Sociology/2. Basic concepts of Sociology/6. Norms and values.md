

## Norms and Values ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** These are the "invisible threads" that hold society together. Without understanding norms and values, you cannot understand why people behave the way they do or how laws (which are just formalized norms) are created.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** A frequent topic for short notes. Examiners specifically look for the distinction between "Folkways" and "Mores" (Sumner’s classification).
    

---

### 1. The "Blueprint" of Behavior

Every society has a "blueprint" for how its members should behave. We don't just act randomly; our actions are guided by cultural standards. These standards are divided into two levels: the abstract (**Values**) and the concrete (**Norms**).

### 2. Social Values (The "Why")

Definition:

Values are broad, abstract, shared ideas about what is good, right, desirable, and worthwhile. They are the general standards by which we judge everything.

- **Nature:** They are general guidelines, not specific rules. They represent the _ideals_ of a society.
    
- **Example:**
    
    - **Value:** "Respect for Elders." (This is just an idea/sentiment).
        
    - **Value:** "Privacy." (Common in Western societies).
        
    - **Value:** "Patriotism."
        

**Function:** Values provide the motivation for our actions. They bind people together by giving them a shared worldview.

### 3. Social Norms (The "How")

Definition:

Norms are specific, concrete rules and expectations by which a society guides the behavior of its members in specific situations. They are the application of values.

- **Nature:** They tell you exactly what to do or not to do.
    
- **Relationship:** Values are the _goal_; Norms are the _path_ to get there.
    
    - _Because_ we value "Respect for Elders" (Value), the norm is "Touch their feet" or "Speak softly" (Norm).
        
    - _Because_ we value "Privacy" (Value), the norm is "Knock before entering" (Norm).
        


### 4. Classification of Norms (W.G. Sumner)

Not all norms are equally important. Walking on the wrong side of the hallway is a violation of a norm, but so is murder. To distinguish these, sociologist **William Graham Sumner** (in his book _Folkways_, 1906) created a famous hierarchy based on the severity of punishment (sanctions).

#### A. Folkways (The "Should" Do)

These are the ordinary conventions of everyday life. They are "customs" or habits of the group.

- **Severity:** Low.
    
- **Violation:** If you break a folkway, people might stare, laugh, or gossip, but you won't be arrested or shunned.
    
- **Examples:** Eating with your right hand (in Bangladesh), wearing a tie to court, shaking hands when introduced.
    
- _Sumner’s View:_ They are "the right way" to do things but not morally vital.
    

#### B. Mores (The "Must" Do)

(Pronounced _more-ays_). These are norms with great **moral significance**. They are viewed as essential to the welfare of the society.

- **Severity:** High.
    
- **Violation:** Breaking a more brings strong social condemnation, shock, or anger.
    
- **Examples:** Adultery, incest, desecrating a religious symbol, or (in traditional societies) a couple living together before marriage.
    
- _Difference:_ Folkways are about "politeness"; Mores are about "virtue and morality."
    

#### C. Laws (The "Must" Do or else...)

Laws are norms that have been formally defined and enforced by specific social institutions (the State).

- **Severity:** Highest (formal punishment).
    
- **Nature:** While Mores are enforced by public pressure, Laws are enforced by police and courts.
    
- **Relationship:** Most laws begin as Mores. (e.g., "Thou shalt not kill" was a More before it became Section 302 of the Penal Code).
    

### 5. Social Sanctions (Enforcement)

How does society ensure people follow these norms? Through **Sanctions** (rewards and punishments).

|**Type**|**Positive Sanction (Reward)**|**Negative Sanction (Punishment)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Formal**|Medal of Honor, Promotion, Degree.|Fine, Prison, Execution (Law).|
|**Informal**|A smile, a compliment, clapping.|A frown, gossip, social boycott (Folkways/Mores).|

### 6. Bangladeshi Context

In Bangladesh, the clash between traditional _Mores_ and modern _Laws_ is a major sociological issue for lawyers.

- **Example 1 (Dowry):** The _Law_ (Dowry Prohibition Act) strictly forbids it. However, the _Folkways/Mores_ of many communities still encourage it as a social custom. This explains why the law is often ineffective—it opposes the deep-rooted norms.
    
- **Example 2 (Dress Code):** Wearing a Sari or Salwar Kameez is a _Folkway_ for women. Wearing "western" clothes might violate the _Folkways_ of a rural village (leading to gossip) but not the _Law_.

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Sumner is Essential:** You _must_ mention **William Graham Sumner** and his book _Folkways_ (1906). This is the academic reference for this topic.
    
- **The Hierarchy:** Visualize a pyramid: Folkways at the bottom (most numerous, least strict), Mores in the middle, and Laws at the top (least numerous, most strict).
    
- **Mores vs. Morals:** "Mores" comes from the Latin word for "custom" (same root as _Morality_). Use this etymology to help remember that Mores = Moral norms.
    
- **Legal Insight:** Law is often described as "codified mores." However, mention that laws can sometimes _change_ mores (e.g., Laws against child marriage eventually changed the social acceptance of the practice).
