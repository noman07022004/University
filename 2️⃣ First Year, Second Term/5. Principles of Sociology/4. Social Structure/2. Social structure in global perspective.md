

## Social Structure in Global Perspective ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** In the 21st century, no society exists in isolation. Understanding that Bangladesh is part of a larger _global_ structure helps explain domestic economic issues, labor laws, and foreign policy.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While less frequent than "Definition of Structure," this topic often appears as a critical part of essay questions on **Globalization** or **Social Change** (Section B).
    

---

### 1. The Shift: From National to Global

Traditionally, sociologists studied "Social Structure" within a single country (e.g., the class structure of Bangladesh). However, in the era of **Globalization**, this is no longer enough.

The Global Perspective:

We must view the entire world not as a collection of independent nations, but as a single, unified social system. Just as a country has a class system (Rich vs. Poor), the world has a class system (Rich Nations vs. Poor Nations).

- **Key Concept:** Global Stratification.
    
- **The Structure:** It is a hierarchy of nations where some hold power and others provide resources.
    

### 2. Theoretical Framework: World Systems Theory

The most standard academic framework for analyzing global social structure is **Immanuel Wallerstein’s World Systems Theory**.

The Core Argument:

Wallerstein argued that since the 16th century, a Capitalist World Economy has emerged. It links all countries into a single economic unit, where there is a global division of labor.

The Three Structural Zones:

Wallerstein divided the global structure into three tiers, functioning exactly like social classes:

#### A. Core Nations (The Upper Class)

- **Characteristics:** Highly industrialized, technologically advanced, and economically diversified.
    
- **Role:** They control global finance and make high-profit consumption goods (computers, planes, pharmaceuticals).
    
- **Examples:** USA, Japan, Germany, UK, France.
    
- **Relation:** They exploit the other zones for cheap labor and raw materials.
    

#### B. Periphery Nations (The Lower Class)

- **Characteristics:** Weak industrial base, low literacy, and often politically unstable.
    
- **Role:** They export raw materials (oil, minerals, crops) and provide cheap, unskilled labor. They are dependent on the Core for capital.
    
- **Examples:** Many nations in Sub-Saharan Africa, Haiti, parts of Central Asia.
    
- **Relation:** They are exploited by the Core.
    

#### C. Semi-Periphery Nations (The Middle Class)

- **Characteristics:** Countries that are industrializing. They have some features of the Core and some of the Periphery.
    
- **Role:** They act as a buffer. They exploit the Periphery but are exploited by the Core. They are often the manufacturing hubs of the world.
    
- **Examples:** China, India, Brazil, Mexico, **Bangladesh** (transitioning).
    

### 3. Global Dependency and Neocolonialism

This global structure explains why "legal independence" did not lead to "economic independence" for many countries.

- Dependency Theory (Andre Gunder Frank):
    
    This theory argues that poor nations are not "undeveloped" because they are lazy; they are "underdeveloped" because the global structure forces them to be dependent.
    
    - _Mechanism:_ The structure ensures that high-value goods (sold by the Core) are expensive, while raw materials (sold by the Periphery) remain cheap.
        
- Neocolonialism:
    
    Even though the British Empire ended, the structure remains. Instead of soldiers, the Core now controls the Periphery through economic institutions (World Bank, IMF, Multinational Corporations).
    
    - _Legal Relevance:_ International Law is often criticized for protecting the property rights of Core corporations (IPR/Patents) over the human rights of Periphery citizens.
        

### 4. The Bangladeshi Context: Where do we fit?

This is crucial for your "Changing Society of Bangladesh" section later.

- 1971-1990 (Periphery):
    
    After independence, Bangladesh was a classic Periphery nation—exporting raw jute, dependent on foreign aid, with almost no industry.
    
- 1990-Present (Entering Semi-Periphery):
    
    With the rise of the Ready-Made Garment (RMG) sector, Bangladesh has moved into the Semi-Periphery.
    
    - _Role:_ We import raw cotton (from Periphery/Core), manufacture shirts (Manufacturing), and export to the Core (USA/EU).
        
    - _Structural Trap:_ While we are industrializing, we are stuck at the low end of the value chain (cheap labor). The Core (brands like H&M, Nike) keeps the majority of the profit.
        

### 5. Global Cultural Structure (Cultural Imperialism)

Social structure isn't just economic; it's also cultural.

- **Media Dominance:** The "Core" exports its culture (Hollywood, English language, Western laws) to the rest of the world.
    
- **Homogenization:** This creates a global culture that threatens local traditions (e.g., Fast food replacing local diet; English replacing local dialects).
