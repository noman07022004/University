
## Major Theoretical Perspectives of Sociology ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the "lens" through which you view the entire course. Every subsequent topic (Family, Crime, Stratification) will be analyzed using these three perspectives. If you don't understand these, you cannot analyze social issues critically.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is arguably the most tested area. You will almost certainly face a broad essay question comparing these theories, or specific problem questions asking you to "Analyze the situation using the Functionalist perspective."
    


Sociologists do not look at society in the same way. Just as a photographer can use different lenses (wide-angle, zoom, macro) to see the same landscape differently, sociologists use **Theoretical Perspectives** (or Paradigms).

A **paradigm** is a fundamental set of assumptions that guides thinking and research. While there are many theories, three dominate the discipline: two **macro-level** (focusing on large structures) and one **micro-level** (focusing on individual interaction).

### 1️⃣ Structural Functionalism (The Consensus Model)

Level of Analysis: Macro

Key Figures: Émile Durkheim, Herbert Spencer, Talcott Parsons, Robert Merton.

This perspective views society as a complex system whose parts work together to promote **solidarity and stability**.

- **Organic Analogy:** Herbert Spencer compared society to a biological organism. Just as the human body has organs (heart, lungs) that function to keep the body alive, society has **social institutions** (family, education, religion, law) that function to keep society surviving.
    
- **Social Structure:** Patterned social behavior (like a handshake or a classroom setup) exists because it serves a purpose.
    
- **Consensus:** It assumes that society is held together by a "collective conscience"—shared values and norms. We agree on what is right and wrong, which maintains order.
    

Robert Merton’s Contribution (Crucial for Exams):

Merton refined this theory by noting that consequences of social patterns aren't always obvious or good:

1. **Manifest Functions:** The recognized and intended consequences (e.g., The manifest function of a university is to educate).
    
2. **Latent Functions:** The unrecognized and unintended consequences (e.g., The latent function of a university is to serve as a "marriage market" for young adults).
    
3. **Dysfunctions:** Social patterns that have undesirable consequences for the operation of society (e.g., pollution is a dysfunction of industrialization).
    

> **Bangladeshi Context:** A functionalist would analyze the **Joint Family** system in rural Bangladesh not just as a tradition, but as an economic necessity that functioned to provide labor for agriculture and social security for the elderly before the state welfare system existed.

### 2️⃣ Social Conflict Theory (The Inequality Model)

Level of Analysis: Macro

Key Figures: Karl Marx, Max Weber, C. Wright Mills.

This perspective views society as an arena of inequality that generates **conflict and change**. It is the direct opposite of Functionalism.

- **Core Argument:** Society is not held together by consensus, but by power. Social order is maintained by domination, with power in the hands of those with the greatest political, economic, and social resources.
    
- **Class Struggle:** Karl Marx focused on the struggle between the owners of production (Bourgeoisie) and the workers (Proletariat). He argued that institutions like the **Law** and **Religion** are tools used by the rich to control the poor (e.g., "Religion is the opium of the masses").
    
- **Beyond Economics:** Later scholars like Max Weber expanded this to include conflict over status and political power, not just money.
    

> **Legal Relevance:** A conflict theorist would argue that laws against "vagrancy" (homelessness) are not about public safety, but about criminalizing the poor to protect the property of the elite.

> **Bangladeshi Context:** Analyzing the 1971 Liberation War is a classic Conflict Theory case—the economic and political exploitation of East Pakistan by West Pakistan created a class struggle that led to inevitable revolution and social change.

### 3️⃣ Symbolic Interactionism (The Meaning Model)

Level of Analysis: Micro

Key Figures: George Herbert Mead, Max Weber, Herbert Blumer, Erving Goffman.Shutterstock

While the first two theories look at society from the "top-down," Interactionism looks from the "bottom-up." It views society as the product of the everyday interactions of individuals.

- **Symbols:** Humans live in a world of meaningful objects—symbols. A "red light" is just a colored bulb physically, but socially it is a symbol for "STOP." Society relies on us agreeing on these meanings.
    
- **Subjective Reality:** There is no single objective "reality"; reality is socially constructed.
    
- **Dramaturgy (Erving Goffman):** This sub-theory compares everyday life to a theatrical performance. We are all actors on a stage, managing our "impression" to others (e.g., a lawyer wearing a wig and gown to convey authority).
    

> Key Concept - Definition of the Situation: "If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences." (W.I. Thomas).
> 
> Example: If a rumor spreads that a bank is bankrupt, people run to withdraw money, and the bank actually crashes. The definition created the reality.

### Summary Comparison Table

|**Feature**|**Structural Functionalism**|**Social Conflict Theory**|**Symbolic Interactionism**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Focus**|Stability, Order, Harmony|Inequality, Power, Struggle|Meaning, Interaction, Symbols|
|**Key Question**|How does this keep society working?|Who benefits from this?|How do people interpret this?|

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **The "Compare and Contrast" Essay:** A very common exam question is "Compare and Contrast Functionalism and Conflict Theory."
    
    - _Tip:_ Do not just list them separately. Use connecting sentences like, _"While Functionalism emphasizes the stability of social institutions, Conflict Theory critiques the inequality inherent within them."_
        
- **Don't Ignore Feminism:** While the syllabus lists the main three, modern "Conflict Theory" often includes **Feminist Theory**. You can briefly mention that Feminism is a specific type of conflict theory focusing on gender inequality.
    
- **Application is Key:** Examiners love to give a scenario (e.g., "Student Protests") and ask you to explain it.
    
    - _Functionalist:_ Protests are a "safety valve" letting off steam to restore order.
        
    - _Conflict:_ Protests are the powerless challenging the powerful.
        
    - _Interactionist:_ Focus on the slogans, signs, and chants (symbols) used by the students.
