### 21.4 Revolutions and Aftermath of Revolution ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Revolutions are the defining events of the modern world (1789, 1917, 1949, 1979). Understanding their lifecycle explains why hopeful uprisings often turn into brutal dictatorships.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is a classic essay topic. You will likely be asked to "Analyze the stages of a revolution" or discuss "Crane Brinton’s Anatomy of Revolution."
    

---

A **Revolution** is not just a change in leadership; it is a fundamental transformation of the social, political, and economic structures of a society.

Theda Skocpol, a leading scholar on the topic, defines it as: _"Rapid, basic transformations of a society's state and class structures... accompanied and in part carried through by class-based revolts from below."_

It is the political equivalent of an earthquake—rare, destructive, but capable of reshaping the landscape forever.

#### 1. The Stages of Revolution (Crane Brinton’s "Fever" Model)

In his classic book _The Anatomy of Revolution_ (1938), historian Crane Brinton compared revolution to a **fever** that attacks the body politic. He studied the English, American, French, and Russian revolutions and found they all followed a similar four-stage pattern:

- **Stage 1: Incubation (The Old Regime Decays)**
    
    - The economy is improving, but people feel restricted (The J-Curve).
        
    - **Intellectual Desertion:** The writers, teachers, and journalists stop supporting the government and start criticizing it.
        
    - **Class Friction:** The wealthy merchant class feels blocked by the old aristocracy.
        
    - **Government Inefficiency:** The government goes bankrupt or fails to win a war.
        
- **Stage 2: The Moderate Phase (Symptomatic)**
    
    - Protests break out. The old ruler falls or abdicates.
        
    - **Moderates take power:** Reasonable, middle-class reformers (like the Mensheviks in Russia or the National Assembly in France) try to set up a democracy.
        
    - _Problem:_ They are too slow. The radical masses want faster change.
        
- **Stage 3: The Radical Phase (Crisis / Delirium)**
    
    - The Moderates are overthrown by **Radicals** (Jacobins, Bolsheviks).
        
    - **The Terror:** The revolution fights for its life against internal and external enemies. The guillotine or firing squads are used to purge "traitors."
        
    - Power is centralized in a small, disciplined council (e.g., Committee of Public Safety).
        
- **Stage 4: Thermidor (Convalescence / Recovery)**
    
    - The society becomes exhausted by the constant violence and fear.
        
    - **The Reaction:** The radicals are executed or pushed aside. A strong leader or dictator (Napoleon, Stalin, Cromwell) emerges to restore order.
        
    - The fever breaks, and the patient (society) survives, often stronger but scarred.
        

#### 2. The Aftermath: What comes next?

Do revolutions result in freedom? Historically, the answer is complex.

- **Stronger States:** Paradoxically, revolutions often create a state that is _more_ powerful and intrusive than the one they destroyed. The French King had little power compared to the total control of Napoleon or the French Republic.
    
- **Social Change:** Even if political freedom fails, revolutions usually succeed in destroying the old class structure (e.g., ending feudalism in France, ending landlordism in China).
    
- **Disappointment:** The utopian promises (perfect equality, end of poverty) are rarely met, leading to cynicism among the population.
    

#### 3. Why do they often end in Tyranny?

Why does a fight for liberty end with a dictator?

- **The Vacuum:** The destruction of all old institutions (courts, police, army) leaves a power vacuum that only the most ruthless and organized group (usually the army) can fill.
    
- **Circle of Fear:** During the radical phase, the leaders are so afraid of counter-revolution that they create a police state to protect themselves, which never goes away.

### Important Notes 📝

- **"The Revolution Devours Its Children":** A famous quote from the French Revolution. It refers to the fact that the original leaders of the revolution (like Danton or Trotsky) are often executed by the very regime they helped create.
    
- **Thermidor:** This term comes from the French Revolutionary calendar month (July) when Robespierre was overthrown. In political science, "Thermidor" refers to the cooling-off period where the revolution steps back from extremism.
    
- **Velvet Revolution:** A term used for the non-violent revolutions in Eastern Europe (1989) that overthrew communism. These are exceptions to the bloody rule of Brinton’s model.
