## 21. Violence and Revolution ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Political science is often about the _avoidance_ of violence. Understanding why systems break down and turn violent is crucial for understanding the fragility of order.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A frequent topic. Exams often ask for the **causes of revolution** (specifically the "J-Curve" theory) or to define **Terrorism** and distinguish it from other forms of violence.


---

"War," said Clausewitz, "is the continuation of politics by other means." Political violence is the use of physical force to achieve political goals. In a stable state, the government holds a "monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force" (Max Weber). When this monopoly is challenged or lost, the system breaks down. This section explores the dark side of politics: why people turn to violence, the strategic logic of terrorism, and the rare but earth-shattering events known as revolutions.

---

### 21.1 System Breakdown

Why does political order collapse? Violence is usually a symptom of a deeper failure in the political system.

- **Loss of Legitimacy:** Violence often begins when the government loses its moral right to rule. If citizens believe the system is rigged or the ruler is a tyrant, they no longer feel a duty to obey.
    
- **Erosion of Effectiveness:** When the state can no longer provide basic security or economic stability, power vacuums emerge. Warlords, gangs, or rebels step in to fill the void.
    
- **Rising Expectations:** Violence rarely happens when people are consistently destitute (they are too busy surviving). It happens when conditions improve and then suddenly drop, or when expectations rise faster than the government can deliver.
    

### 21.2 Types of Violence

Political violence is not uniform. It takes many forms depending on the actors and their goals.

1. **Primordial/Communal Violence:** Violence between different groups (ethnic, religious, tribal) within the state. The goal is often not to overthrow the state, but to attack the "enemy" group (e.g., Hindu-Muslim riots in India, Hutu-Tutsi genocide in Rwanda).
    
2. **Separatist Violence:** A group seeks to break away from the existing state to form their own independent nation (e.g., The Tamils in Sri Lanka, Kurds in the Middle East).
    
3. **Coups d'état:** A "stroke of state" where the military or elites forcibly remove the existing government. This changes the _leadership_ but usually leaves the social structure intact (e.g., Myanmar).
    
4. **State-Sponsored Violence:** When the government itself uses violence (death squads, secret police) against its own citizens to maintain control.
    

### 21.3 Terrorism

Terrorism is a specific, strategic type of violence.

- **Definition:** The use or threat of violence against **non-combatants (civilians)** to induce **fear (terror)** in a wider audience, for a **political goal**.
    
- **The Logic of Terrorism:** It is the "weapon of the weak." Terrorists know they cannot defeat the army on the battlefield. Instead, they attack soft targets (cafes, buses) to create panic.
    
- **The Goal:** The physical damage is secondary. The primary goal is psychological—to make the government look weak and force it to overreact (e.g., by restricting civil liberties), which terrorists hope will radicalize more people to their cause.
    

### 21.4 Revolutions and Aftermath

A Revolution is distinct from a coup or a riot. It is a fundamental transformation of the political, social, and economic structure of a society.

- **Theories of Revolution (The J-Curve):**
    
    - Sociologist **James C. Davies** proposed that revolutions do not occur during prolonged poverty.
        
    - They occur when a period of economic growth is followed by a sharp decline.
        
    - **The J-Curve:** The gap between "what people expect" (which keeps rising) and "what they get" (which suddenly drops) creates an **intolerable gap** of frustration, leading to revolution.
        
- **The Stages of Revolution (Crane Brinton):**
    
    1. **Old Regime Decay:** The government becomes incompetent and the intellectuals abandon it.
        
    2. **Moderate Stage:** Moderates take power initially and try to compromise.
        
    3. **Radical Stage (The Terror):** Extremists push aside the moderates (e.g., Jacobins in France, Bolsheviks in Russia) and use violence to "purify" society.
        
    4. **Thermidor (The Reaction):** The society becomes exhausted by the violence. A dictator or strongman restores order, often resembling the old regime (e.g., Napoleon, Stalin).
        

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Key Distinction:**
    
    - **Coup:** Changing the _Captain_ of the ship. (Top-down, rapid, elite-driven).
        
    - **Revolution:** Building a _New Ship_. (Bottom-up, masses involved, transforms society).
        
- **"One man's terrorist is another man's freedom fighter":** This cliché highlights the difficulty of defining terrorism politically. However, academically, if the target is _civilians_, it is terrorism.
    
- **Exam Tip:** If asked about the causes of revolution, **The J-Curve** is the "gold standard" answer. Draw the graph: X-axis = Time, Y-axis = Needs/Satisfaction. A rising line (expectations) that continues up while the actual reality line dips down.
