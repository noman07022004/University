### Introduction: The Central Problem of IR

If International Relations has a "central question," it is this: **Why do states fight, and how can they stop?** In the syllabus, "War and Peace" represents the spectrum of conflict. Historically, Realists viewed war as inevitable and natural, while Liberals viewed war as an aberration that could be cured through institutions.

### A. War

War is defined as organized violence between political entities. It is not mere random violence; it is political in nature.

#### 1. The Clausewitzian Definition

The most famous definition comes from the Prussian general and theorist **Carl von Clausewitz** (1832). He argued:

> **"War is the continuation of politics by other means."**

- **Meaning:** War is not an emotional outburst; it is a rational instrument of national policy. States go to war to achieve specific political objectives (e.g., taking territory, changing a regime) that they could not achieve through diplomacy.
    

#### 2. The Causes of War (Waltz’s Three Images)

To write a university-standard answer, you must use **Kenneth Waltz’s** framework from his book _Man, the State, and War_. He categorizes the causes of war into three levels of analysis (or "Images"):

- **1st Image (Human Nature):** War is caused by individuals. Bad leaders (like Hitler or Napoleon) or inherent human aggression lead to conflict.
    
- **2nd Image (The State):** War is caused by the internal nature of states. For example, Lenin argued that _Capitalist_ states cause war (Imperialism), while Democratic Peace Theory argues that _Autocratic_ states are more warlike.
    
- **3rd Image (The System):** War is caused by the **Anarchic structure** of the international system. Because there is no world police to stop them, states are forced to arm themselves, leading to a "Security Dilemma" and eventual war. Waltz argued this is the most important cause.
    

#### 3. Changing Nature of War

- **Total War:** Mobilization of all resources and population (e.g., WWII).
    
- **Limited War:** Fought for specific, limited objectives (e.g., The Gulf War).
    
- **Asymmetric War:** Conflict between a state and a non-state actor (e.g., US vs. Taliban).
    
- **Proxy War:** Major powers fighting indirectly through third parties (e.g., Cold War conflicts).

### B. Peace

Peace is not just the absence of shooting. In academic IR, we distinguish between two types of peace, a concept developed by **Johan Galtung**.

#### 1. Negative Peace

- **Definition:** The simple **absence of direct violence** (war).
    
- **Characteristics:** There is no active shooting, but the underlying causes of conflict (oppression, poverty) remain. A ceasefire is "Negative Peace."
    
- **Example:** The Cold War (no direct fighting between superpowers, but high tension).
    

#### 2. Positive Peace

- **Definition:** The presence of **social justice** and the removal of "Structural Violence."
    
- **Characteristics:** Relations are cooperative, and the root causes of conflict have been resolved.
    
- **Example:** The relationship between France and Germany today (integration and friendship).
    

#### 3. Approaches to Maintaining Peace

How do states prevent war?

- **Balance of Power:** (Realist view) Peace is kept by ensuring no single state becomes strong enough to dominate all others. If one rises, others form an alliance to block it.
    
- **Collective Security:** (Liberal view) "One for all, and all for one." If any state attacks another, _all_ other states promise to attack the aggressor (e.g., The UN mechanism).
    
- **Democratic Peace:** The empirical observation that democracies almost never fight wars against other democracies.

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **The "Security Dilemma":** This is a crucial concept to mention. It explains how war can happen even if no one wants it. _State A buys a shield for defense -> State B sees it as a threat and buys a sword -> State A buys a bigger shield._ The cycle escalates to war.
    
- **Citation:** Always cite **Kenneth Waltz** when discussing "Causes of War" and **Johan Galtung** when distinguishing between "Positive and Negative Peace." These are the gold standards for this topic.
    
- **Legal Perspective:** Since this course is a foundation for legal studies (as per the Rationale), note that **International Law** (UN Charter Article 2(4)) generally prohibits the use of force, except in self-defense (Article 51) or UN-authorized enforcement. This connects the _political_ concept of war to the _legal_ framework.
