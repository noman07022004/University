## 22.2 Power and national interest ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the core of Realism, the dominant theory in International Relations. You cannot understand why states go to war, form alliances, or sign treaties without grasping these two concepts. They are the "currency" (Power) and the "objective" (National Interest) of international politics.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Highly frequent. Questions often ask to define National Power, explain its elements (tangible/intangible), or discuss the relationship between Power and National Interest (Morgenthau's theory).
    

---

### Introduction: The Twin Pillars of Realism

In the anarchic international system—where there is no central world government to enforce laws—states must rely on themselves for survival. This creates a dual dynamic:

1. **National Interest** is the _end_ (the goal the state wants to achieve).
    
2. **Power** is the _means_ (the tool used to achieve that goal).
    

Scholars like **Hans J. Morgenthau**, the father of Classical Realism, argue that international politics is essentially "a struggle for power" where statesmen think and act in terms of "interest defined as power."

---

### A. Power in International Relations

#### 1. Definition of Power

Power is a relational concept. It is not just possessing weapons; it is the capacity to influence outcomes.

- **Robert Dahl's Definition:** "A has power over B to the extent that he can get B to do something that B would not otherwise do."
    
- **Hans Morgenthau's Definition:** "Man's control over the minds and actions of other men."
    

#### 2. Forms of Power (Hard vs. Soft)

Joseph Nye introduced a critical distinction in modern IR:

- **Hard Power:** The use of coercion and payment. It relies on tangible resources like military force and economic sanctions. (e.g., "Do this or I will bomb/sanction you.")
    
- **Soft Power:** The ability to obtain what you want through attraction rather than coercion or payment. It arises from the attractiveness of a country's culture, political ideals, and policies. (e.g., American movies, K-Pop, or democratic values influencing other nations).
    
- **Smart Power:** The strategic combination of both hard and soft power.
    

#### 3. Elements of National Power

Morgenthau classified the elements that make a state powerful into two categories:

|**Tangible Elements (Measurable)**|**Intangible Elements (Hard to Measure)**|
|---|---|
|**Geography:** Size, location, climate (e.g., USA's isolation by oceans).|**National Character:** The psychological traits of the people (e.g., resilience).|
|**Natural Resources:** Oil, minerals, food self-sufficiency.|**National Morale:** The degree of determination to support the government (e.g., high morale during war).|
|**Population:** Size and skills (demographic dividend).|**Quality of Diplomacy:** The brains of national power; how well resources are used.|
|**Military:** Size, technology, and readiness of armed forces.|**Quality of Leadership:** Governance and strategic vision.|

### B. National Interest

#### 1. Definition

National Interest is the vague but all-important concept that guides the foreign policy of a state. It represents the claims, objectives, and demands a nation seeks to protect in relation to other nations. It is the "compass" for statesmen.

#### 2. The Realist View (Morgenthau)

Morgenthau famously stated: "The main signpost that helps political realism to find its way through the landscape of international politics is the concept of interest defined in terms of power."

He argued that the only permanent interest of a state is survival (protecting physical, political, and cultural identity). Morality is secondary to national interest.

#### 3. Classification of National Interests

Scholar **Thomas Robinson** classifies interests into priorities:

1. **Primary Interests (Vital):** Interests worth fighting a war for.
    
    - _Examples:_ Protection of territory, survival of the state, preservation of political independence.
        
2. **Secondary Interests:** Important but not worth a war; usually settled diplomatically.
    
    - _Examples:_ Protection of citizens abroad, trade advantages.
        
3. **Permanent Interests:** Long-term goals that don't change with parties.
    
    - _Examples:_ Britain maintaining freedom of navigation; Bangladesh maintaining access to river waters.
        
4. **Variable Interests:** Goals that change with circumstances or public opinion.
    
    - _Examples:_ Supporting a specific ideology or a temporary ally.
        

---

### C. The Relationship: Means and Ends

Power and National Interest are inseparable.

- **Power is the currency:** Just as you need money to buy goods, a state needs power to "purchase" its national security and interests.
    
- **Limit of Ambition:** A wise state defines its National Interest according to its available Power. (e.g., A small state should not have a national interest of "global domination" because it lacks the power to achieve it). This gap is often called the "Lippmann Gap" (named after Walter Lippmann)—the gap between commitments (interests) and power.

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Key Quote:** For any question on this topic, you _must_ quote **Hans J. Morgenthau**: "International politics, like all politics, is a struggle for power."
    
- **Diagrammatic thought:** In an exam, you can visualize "Elements of Power" as a pyramid. Geography and Resources are the base, while Leadership and Diplomacy are the apex that directs the base.
    
- **Critical Analysis:** When discussing power, do not just list tanks and guns. Mention **Joseph Nye's "Soft Power"**. This shows you are updated with modern political science, not just classical theories.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** You can use Bangladesh as an example.
    
    - _Tangible Power:_ Strategic location (bridge between South and SE Asia), large population (workforce).
        
    - _National Interest:_ Economic development, climate justice, and maintaining sovereignty amidst big neighbors (India/Myanmar).
