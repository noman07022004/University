### 20.1 Definition of Political Economy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** You cannot understand the behavior of modern states without understanding how they manage their economies. This concept connects every previous topic (State, Parties, Interest Groups) to the material reality of money.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While essential for understanding, exam questions typically focus more on specific theories (like Liberalism vs. Marxism) or issues (Poverty/Welfare) rather than just the definition itself.
    

**Political Economy** is the study of how political institutions, the political environment, and the economic system—capitalist, socialist, or mixed—influence each other.

It rejects the idea that "the economy" operates in a vacuum controlled only by supply and demand. Instead, it argues that **economic outcomes are shaped by political power**, and **political decisions are constrained by economic realities**.

#### 1. The Core Relationship: State vs. Market

The discipline fundamentally studies the tension between two mechanisms of resource allocation:

- **The Market (Economics):** Allocates resources based on **efficiency** and **voluntary exchange**. (e.g., "I will buy this bread because the price is low.")
    
- **The State (Politics):** Allocates resources based on **authority**, **justice**, or **power**. (e.g., "We will tax the rich to feed the poor because it is fair," or "We will subsidize farmers because they vote for us.")
    

#### 2. Evolution of the Term

- **Classical Roots:** Originally, the field was just called "Political Economy." Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and Karl Marx considered themselves "Political Economists." They understood that the "Wealth of Nations" (Smith's famous book) was a concern of the _state_.
    
- **The Split:** In the late 19th century, "Economics" tried to become a pure science (like physics), stripping away the messy politics to focus on mathematical models of "perfect markets."
    
- **The Return:** In the late 20th century, scholars realized you cannot explain things like trade wars, budget deficits, or inequality with math alone. You need to understand voters, lobbies, and ideologies. Thus, "Political Economy" returned as a major field.
    

#### 3. Harold Lasswell's Lens

A famous definition of politics is **"Who gets what, when, and how."** Political Economy applies this strictly to material resources:

- _Who_ gets the tax breaks?
    
- _Who_ pays the cost of pollution?
    
- _How_ does the government regulate the banks?
    

#### 4. The Three Grand Theories

Political Economy is usually analyzed through three competing lenses (which we will explore further in "Political Ideologies" and "International Relations"):

1. **Liberalism:** Favors free markets and minimal state interference. The state should only protect property rights.
    
2. **Mercantilism/Economic Nationalism:** The economy should serve the national interest. The state should protect domestic industries and hoard wealth for national power.
    
3. **Marxism/Structuralism:** The economy is a site of exploitation between classes. The state is just a tool of the capitalist class to protect their profits.

### Important Notes 📝

- **Oikos + Nomos:** The word "Economy" comes from the Greek _Oikos_ (household) and _Nomos_ (law/management). Political Economy is, therefore, the "management of the state's household."
    
- **Autonomy of the State:** A key concept. Does the state control the economy (State Autonomy), or do big corporations control the state (Corporate Capture)?
    
- **Exam Tip:** If asked to define Political Economy, do not just say "Politics plus Economics." Define it as _the study of the interaction between the state and the market._
