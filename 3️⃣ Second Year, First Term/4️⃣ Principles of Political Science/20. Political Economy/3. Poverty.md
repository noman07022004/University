### 20.3 Poverty ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Poverty is often the primary driver of political conflict, revolution, and policy-making. Understanding its definitions and causes is essential for analyzing why governments rise and fall.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Questions often focus on the distinction between "Absolute" and "Relative" poverty, or ask to discuss the "Vicious Cycle of Poverty."
    

---

In political economy, **Poverty** is not merely the lack of money; it is a lack of power, opportunity, and access to the basic necessities of life. It is the most persistent problem facing governments, and the failure to address it often leads to "System Breakdown" (discussed in Section 21).

Political scientists generally divide poverty into two distinct categories:

#### 1. Absolute Poverty (Destitution)

This is the condition where an individual does not have the minimum resources necessary to sustain physical life.

- **Definition:** The inability to afford basic needs: food, clean water, shelter, and clothing.
    
- **Measurement:** The World Bank often defines this as living on less than a specific dollar amount per day (e.g., **$2.15 per day** at purchasing power parity).
    
- **Focus:** This is the primary concern in developing nations (Global South). The goal here is simple survival.
    

#### 2. Relative Poverty (Inequality)

This is the condition of having significantly fewer resources than the average person in your specific society.

- **Definition:** You may have food and a house, but you cannot afford the "standard of living" considered normal in your culture (e.g., internet access, a car, or university tuition).
    
- **Focus:** This is the primary concern in developed nations (Global North). It measures **Inequality** rather than starvation. A person considered "poor" in the USA might be considered wealthy in a developing country, but they still feel marginalized in their own society.
    

#### 3. The Vicious Cycle of Poverty (The Poverty Trap)

Economist Ragnar Nurkse famously described poverty as a self-reinforcing cycle.

- **The Mechanism:**
    
    1. **Low Income:** A family has no money.
        
    2. **Low Savings/Investment:** They spend everything on food; they cannot save to start a business or pay for education.
        
    3. **Low Productivity:** Without education or machinery, they can only do low-wage manual labor.
        
    4. **Result:** They remain with Low Income.
        
- **Political Consequence:** The poor cannot escape this trap alone. The state must intervene (through subsidies, free education, or infrastructure) to break the cycle.
    

#### 4. Explanations for Poverty (Why are people poor?)

Political ideologies disagree sharply on the _cause_ of poverty, which leads to different solutions.

- **The Individual/Cultural View (Conservative):**
    
    - **Cause:** Poverty is often caused by individual choices (lack of work ethic, bad habits) or a "Culture of Poverty" that devalues education.
        
    - **Solution:** Promote hard work, family values, and free markets. Welfare might make people lazy.
        
- **The Structural View (Socialist/Liberal):**
    
    - **Cause:** Poverty is caused by the _system_. Low wages, lack of good schools in poor areas, racism, or unfair trade laws prevent people from rising no matter how hard they work.
        
    - **Solution:** Structural reform, wealth redistribution, and affirmative action.

### Important Notes 📝

- **The Gini Coefficient:** This is the standard mathematical measure of inequality in a country.
    
    - _0.0_ = Perfect Equality (Everyone has the same income).
        
    - _1.0_ = Perfect Inequality (One person has all the money).
        
    - High Gini coefficients often correlate with political instability and crime.
        
- **Human Development Index (HDI):** The UN argues that measuring poverty by _money_ is not enough. The HDI measures poverty by looking at **Life Expectancy**, **Education**, and **Income** together.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** In exams, mentioning Bangladesh's success in reducing "Absolute Poverty" (through microfinance and garments) while facing challenges with "Relative Poverty" (inequality) shows strong application of theory.
