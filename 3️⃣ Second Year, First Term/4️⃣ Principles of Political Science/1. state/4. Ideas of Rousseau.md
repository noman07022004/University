## 1.4 Ideas of Rousseau ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) offers the most radical and complex theory of the state. His concept of the "General Will" is the foundation of modern democratic theory (Popular Sovereignty) but also, paradoxically, has been used to justify totalitarianism.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A favorite topic for examiners because of its complexity. Questions often ask to distinguish the **"General Will"** from the **"Will of All,"** or to critique his statement that citizens can be **"forced to be free."**

---

If Hobbes prioritized **Order** and Locke prioritized **Property**, Rousseau prioritized **Equality** and **Authentic Freedom**. Writing in the 18th century, he was a counter-cultural figure who argued that "Civilization" had not improved humanity but corrupted it. In his seminal work, _The Social Contract_ (1762), he sought to find a way for humans to live together in a state without losing their natural freedom.

---

### 1. Human Nature and the State of Nature

Rousseau’s view is the polar opposite of Hobbes.

- **The Noble Savage:** Rousseau argued that in the State of Nature, man was a "Noble Savage"—naturally good, solitary, and peaceful. Humans were motivated by two simple principles: **Self-preservation** and **Pity** (compassion for the suffering of others).
    
- **Corruption by Society:** Evil is not natural (as Hobbes thought); it is social. As humans gathered in groups, they began to compare themselves to others (pride/vanity).
    
- **The Origin of Inequality:** The real fall of man happened with the invention of **Private Property**. Rousseau famously wrote:
    
    > _"The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying 'This is mine,' and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society."_ Property led to inequality, crime, and war. The early states were just "conspiracies of the rich" to protect their property from the poor.
    

### 2. The Social Contract: A New Solution 
Rousseau begins _The Social Contract_ with the most famous line in political philosophy:

> **"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."**

His goal was to legitimize these chains. Since we cannot return to the forest, how can we be free _within_ society?

- **Total Alienation:** Unlike Locke (who keeps some rights), Rousseau argues that individuals must surrender **all their rights** to the whole community.
    
- **The Logic:** If _everyone_ gives up everything to the collective, then everyone is equal. No one has a personal advantage. In obeying the community, you are actually obeying yourself (since you are a part of that community).
    
- **Transformation:** This contract transforms the "stupid, limited animal" into an intelligent "citizen." We lose _Natural Liberty_ (the right to do whatever we can physically grab) but gain _Civil Liberty_ (protection by law) and _Moral Liberty_ (mastery over one's own impulses).
    

### 3. The General Will (_Volonté Générale_)

This is Rousseau’s most critical and difficult concept. The State is directed by the General Will.

- **Definition:** The General Will is the collective desire of the citizenry that aims at the **Common Good**. It is what a rational citizen _should_ want for the welfare of the community.
    
- **General Will vs. Will of All:**
    
    - **Will of All (_volonté de tous_):** The sum of selfish, private interests (e.g., what you get if everyone votes for their own wallet).
        
    - **General Will:** The interest of the collective (e.g., what you vote for if you think about what is best for the nation).
        
- **Infallibility:** Rousseau argues the General Will is always right. If the people make a mistake, it is because they were deceived or blinded by private groups, not because the General Will itself is wrong.
    

### 4. The Paradox of Freedom ("Forced to be Free") 

This concept is what makes Liberals (like Locke/Mill) nervous about Rousseau.

- **The Argument:** If the General Will represents the true common good, and you disagree with it, you are essentially disagreeing with your own best interests (your "better self").
    
- **Coercion:** Therefore, when the State punishes you or forces you to obey the law, it is not oppressing you; it is liberating you from your own irrational impulses. It is **"forcing you to be free."**
    
    - _Critique:_ This logic was later used by dictators (like Robespierre or Soviet leaders) to claim they were oppressing people for their own good.
        

### 5. Concept of Sovereignty 

For Rousseau, the Sovereign is not a King (Hobbes) or a Parliament (Locke), but the **People** themselves.

- **Direct Democracy:** Rousseau hated representative democracy (like the UK or USA). He believed sovereignty cannot be represented. "The moment a people allows itself to be represented, it is no longer free." He envisioned small city-states (like ancient Sparta or Geneva) where everyone gathers to make laws directly.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Comparative Table (Mental Check):**
    
    - _Hobbes:_ State of Nature is War. Sovereign is Absolute Monarch.
        
    - _Locke:_ State of Nature is Inconvenient. Sovereign is Limited Trustee.
        
    - _Rousseau:_ State of Nature is Idyllic. Sovereign is the Whole Community (General Will).
        
- **Historical Impact:** Rousseau was the spiritual father of the **French Revolution**. Robespierre kept a copy of _The Social Contract_ under his pillow.
    
- **Exam Warning:** Do not confuse "General Will" with "Majority Vote." A majority can be corrupt (Will of All). The General Will is an ethical standard of the common good.
    
- **Key Term - Popular Sovereignty:** Rousseau is the primary theorist of Popular Sovereignty—the idea that the ultimate authority lies with the people.
