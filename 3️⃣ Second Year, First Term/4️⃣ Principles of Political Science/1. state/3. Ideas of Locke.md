## 1.3 Ideas of Locke ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** John Locke is the intellectual father of **Liberalism**. His ideas form the operating system of modern Western democracies. Concepts like "natural rights," "consent of the governed," and "separation of powers" are essential for understanding the American and French Revolutions and modern constitutionalism.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A very high-priority topic. Exam questions frequently ask students to **compare and contrast** Locke’s limited government with Hobbes’ absolute sovereign, or to explain his theory of **Property**.

---

While Hobbes wrote to justify absolute monarchy to prevent chaos, **John Locke** (1632–1704) wrote to justify the **Glorious Revolution of 1688**, which established the supremacy of Parliament over the King. In his seminal work, _Two Treatises of Government_ (1689), Locke argued that the state exists not to intimidate citizens, but to serve them. If Hobbes is the philosopher of "Security," Locke is the philosopher of **"Liberty."**

---

### 1. View of Human Nature 

Locke rejected Hobbes' view that humans are driven only by restless appetites.

- **Rationality:** He believed humans are naturally rational and social creatures capable of following moral rules.
    
- **Equality:** Like Hobbes, he believed all men are created equal, but for Locke, this equality implies that no one has a natural right to rule over another (rejecting the "Divine Right of Kings").
    
- **Tabula Rasa:** He famously argued the human mind is a "blank slate" at birth, shaped by experience, suggesting that people are not born evil but are shaped by their environment.
    

### 2. The State of Nature

For Locke, the State of Nature is fundamentally different from Hobbes' "War of All Against All."

- **A State of Peace:** It is a state of "peace, goodwill, mutual assistance, and preservation."
    
- **The Law of Nature:** Even without a government, humans are governed by the **Law of Nature** (Reason). Reason teaches that since we are all equal and independent, _"no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions."_
    
- **Natural Rights:** Individuals possess rights _before_ the state exists. These are **Life, Liberty, and Property**.
    
- **The Problem (The "Inconveniences"):** If the State of Nature is so good, why leave it?
    
    - **Lack of an Impartial Judge:** When disputes arise (e.g., someone steals your apple), every man is his own judge and executioner. People tend to be biased in their own favor and overly vindictive in punishment.
        
    - **Lack of Execution:** Individuals might lack the power to punish a strong offender.
        
    - Therefore, the State of Nature is **"inconvenient"** and unstable.
        

### 3. The Social Contract 
To remedy these inconveniences, individuals voluntarily agree to form a political society.

- **The Compact:** People agree to surrender _some_ of their natural rights (specifically, the right to interpret and enforce the Law of Nature) to the community.
    
- **Consent:** Legitimate government is based entirely on the **Consent of the Governed**.
    
- **Fiduciary Trust:** The government is not a "Leviathan"; it is a **Trustee**. It is hired by the people to perform a specific job: the protection of their natural rights.
    
- **Limited Government:** Because the government is a trustee, its power is **limited**. It cannot take property without consent (no taxation without representation) or rule arbitrarily.
    

### 4. Theory of Property 
Locke is unique in making "Property" a central reason for the state's existence.

- **Labor Theory of Value:** How does something become "yours" in the state of nature? Locke argues that you own your own body. Therefore, when you mix your **Labor** with nature (e.g., picking an apple, tilling a field), that object becomes your property.
    
- **Chief End of Government:** "The great and chief end, therefore, of men's uniting into commonwealths... is the preservation of their property."
    

### 5. The Right to Revolution 
This is Locke's most radical contribution.

- **Breach of Trust:** Since the government is a trustee, if it acts contrary to the trust reposed in it (by becoming tyrannical or violating rights), it forfeits its authority.
    
- **Appeal to Heaven:** In such cases, power reverts to the people, who have a **Right to Revolution**. They can overthrow the government and establish a new one. This argument directly justified the American Revolution against the British King.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Comparison Key:**
    
    - **Hobbes:** You surrender _ALL_ rights -> Absolute Government -> No Right to Revolution.
        
    - **Locke:** You surrender _ONLY the right to judge/punish_ -> Limited Government -> Right to Revolution if contract is broken.
        
- **The "Property" Nuance:** When Locke says the state exists to protect "Property," he defines property broadly to include **"Life, Liberty, and Estate."**
    
- **Influence:** Thomas Jefferson plagiarized Locke (with admiration) in the US Declaration of Independence, changing "Life, Liberty, and Property" to **"Life, Liberty, and the pursuit of Happiness."**
    
- **Exam Tip:** If asked about the "Separation of Powers," note that while Montesquieu is famous for it, Locke was one of the first to argue that the **Legislative** (law-making) and **Executive** (law-enforcing) powers should be in different hands to prevent tyranny.
