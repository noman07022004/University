## 1. State ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**

>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is a high-frequency exam topic. Questions often focus on defining the state, distinguishing it from government, and critically analyzing the social contract theories of Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau, as well as the socialist perspective.


---

The **"State"** is the primary unit of analysis in political science. It is distinct from the **"government"** *(which is merely the agency through which the state's will is formulated and executed)* and the **"nation"** *(which implies a cultural or psychological bond)*. In academic terms, a state is a political association that establishes sovereign jurisdiction within defined territorial borders and exercises authority through a set of permanent institutions. To understand the state, one must navigate the intellectual history of how political authority is justified. This narrative flow moves from the definitions of the state to the classical "*Social Contract*" theorists—who viewed the state as a necessary creation to escape a chaotic pre-political existence—and concludes with the Socialist critique, which views the state not as a neutral arbiter, but as an instrument of class oppression.

---

### 1.1 Introduction to the State

Before analyzing specific theorists, it is essential to establish what constitutes a state. In political science, the state is generally defined by the **Montevideo Convention (1933)** criteria, though these concepts predate the treaty.

A state must possess four essential elements:

1. **Population:** A body of people.
    
2. **Territory:** A defined geographical area.
    
3. **Government:** The machinery to maintain order and enforce laws.
    
4. **Sovereignty:** The supreme and absolute power within its own territory and independence from external control.
    

Scholars distinguish between different _conceptions_ of the state:

- **Idealist View (Hegel):** The state is an ethical community underpinning society.
    
- **Functionalist View:** The state is defined by its functions (maintaining order, social stability).
    
- **Organizational View:** The state is the apparatus of government (bureaucracy, military, police).

### 1.2 The Social Contract Tradition (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau)

The "Social Contract" is a theoretical framework used to explain the origins of the state. These thinkers asked: _What was life like before the state (the State of Nature)?_ and _Why did people agree to create a sovereign authority?_

#### A. Thomas Hobbes: The State as Leviathan

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679), writing during the English Civil War, offered a grim view of human nature.

- **State of Nature:** For Hobbes, humans are driven by a restless desire for power. Without a common power to keep them in awe, the state of nature is a *"war of every man against every man."* Life is *"solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."*
    
- ** The Covenant:** To escape this fear of violent death, individuals surrender _all_ their rights (except the right to self-preservation) to a supreme authority—the **Leviathan**.
    
- **Nature of the State:** The resulting state is absolute. The sovereign's power is indivisible and unlimited because anything less would lead back to the chaos of civil war. Order is prioritized over liberty.
    

#### B. John Locke: The State as a Trustee

John Locke (1632–1704) is the father of Liberalism. His view of the state contrasts sharply with Hobbes.

- **State of Nature:** Locke argued that the state of nature was not a state of war, but a state of *"peace, goodwill, mutual assistance, and preservation."* Humans are rational and governed by the Law of Nature. However, it was *"inconvenient"* because there was no impartial judge to settle disputes regarding property.
    
- **The Compact:** People enter a contract not to surrender all rights, but to protect their **natural rights**: Life, Liberty, and Property.
    
- **Nature of the State:** The state is a **limited government**. It is a "trustee" acting on behalf of the people. If the state violates the trust (by taking property without consent or acting tyrannically), the people have the **right to revolution**. This theory underpins modern constitutional democracy.
    

#### C. Jean-Jacques Rousseau: The General Will

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) offered a radical critique of both Hobbes and Locke.

- **State of Nature:** Rousseau believed man is a *"noble savage"*—naturally good, free, and happy, but corrupted by civilization and private property. *"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."*
    
- **The Contract:** To regain freedom within a society, individuals must surrender their natural liberty to the whole community, not to a king.
    
- **Nature of the State:** The state is directed by the **General Will** (_volonté générale_), which represents the collective interest of the community, distinct from the **"will of all"** *(which is just a sum of private desires)*. Obedience to the General Will is actually obedience to one's true self; thus, the state forces citizens to be free. This concept influenced both radical democracy and totalitarianism(সর্বগ্রাসীবাদ).

### 1.3 Ideas of Socialism: The State as an Instrument of Class Rule

Moving away from the Social Contract tradition, the Socialist (specifically Marxist) perspective offers a materialist critique of the state.

- **Rejection of Neutrality:** Unlike Liberals who see the state as a neutral referee, Socialists view the state as a superstructure arising from the economic base.
    
- **Instrument of Oppression:** Marx and Engels argued that *"the executive of the modern state is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie."* The state exists to protect the property rights of the ruling class (capitalists) and suppress the working class (proletariat).
    
- **The Withering Away:** In orthodox Marxist theory, after the proletariat revolution, a *"Dictatorship of the Proletariat"* will temporarily manage society. Eventually, as class distinctions disappear and communism is achieved, the state (as an instrument of force) will become unnecessary and *"wither away,"* replaced by the *"administration of things."*

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Comparison Tip:** For exams, create a table comparing Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau on three points: _Human Nature_, _State of Nature_, and _Nature of the Sovereign_.
    
    - _Hobbes_: Pessimistic / State of War / Absolute Monarchy.
        
    - _Locke_: Optimistic / Inconvenient / Limited Constitutionalism.
        
    - _Rousseau_: Romantic / Corrupted by society / Popular Sovereignty (General Will).
        
- **Term Clarification:** Do not confuse "State" with "Nation." A _Nation_ is a group sharing culture/language (e.g., the Kurds). A _State_ is a legal/political entity (e.g., Iraq). A _Nation-State_ is when the two coincide (e.g., Japan).
    
- **Key Quote for Exams:** For Locke, always mention "Life, Liberty, and Property." For Hobbes, "Nasty, brutish, and short."
    
- **Modern Relevance:** Locke's ideas directly influenced the **American Declaration of Independence** (Thomas Jefferson changed "Property" to "Pursuit of Happiness").
