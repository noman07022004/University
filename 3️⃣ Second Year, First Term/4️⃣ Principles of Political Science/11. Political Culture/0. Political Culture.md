## 11. Political Culture ⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** While institutions (parliaments, courts) are the "hardware" of politics, political culture is the "software." Understanding why the _same_ constitution works in the USA but fails in Liberia requires understanding the underlying beliefs and values of the people.  
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐):** A moderately frequent topic. Exams often ask for a definition of "Political Socialization" or an explanation of the difference between "Elite" and "Mass" culture. The concept of "Social Capital" (Decay) is also a popular essay subject.

---

Why do some nations accept corruption as normal while others riot over it? Why do some citizens trust their government while others fear it? This is the domain of **Political Culture**. It refers to the set of attitudes, beliefs, and sentiments that give order and meaning to a political process. It is the psychological dimension of the political system. While "Public Opinion" (Topic 12) is about what people think about specific issues _today_, Political Culture is about the deep-seated values they hold _permanently_.

---

### 11.1 Definition and Political Socialization

How is a political culture formed? It is not genetic; it is learned.

- **Definition:** Political Culture is the collective political memory and values of a nation. It includes beliefs about legitimate authority, the rights of citizens, and the proper role of the state.
    
- **Political Socialization:** This is the _process_ by which these values are transmitted from one generation to the next. It is how "subjects" are turned into "citizens."
    
    - **Agents of Socialization:**
        
        1. **Family:** The primary agent. Most people acquire their initial party identification and respect for authority from their parents.
            
        2. **School:** Schools teach patriotism (pledge of allegiance, national anthem) and history (shared myths and heroes).
            
        3. **Peers:** Friends and coworkers shape attitudes, often reinforcing existing beliefs.
            
        4. **Media:** As discussed in later topics, media frames how we see the world.
            

### 11.2 Subcultures: Elite, Mass, and Minority

Political culture is rarely uniform across an entire nation. It is often stratified.

- **Elite vs. Mass Subcultures:**
    
    - **The Elite:** Those with power, high education, and wealth often have a distinct culture. They tend to be more "participant" (active), more ideological, and more committed to democratic _norms_ (like free speech) than the average person.
        
    - **The Mass:** The general public may be less interested in politics, more focused on economic survival, and sometimes less tolerant of dissenting views.
        
    - **The Gap:** If the gap between the values of the Elites (e.g., globalism, secularism) and the Masses (e.g., nationalism, tradition) becomes too wide, it causes instability (populism).
        
- **Minority Subcultures:**
    
    - Distinct ethnic, religious, or linguistic groups may have their own political culture that challenges the dominant national culture.
        
    - _Example:_ The Quebecois in Canada or the Kurds in Iraq may feel loyalty to their sub-national group rather than the central state. Integrating these subcultures is a major challenge for nation-building.
        

### 11.3 Decay of Political Culture (Social Capital)

Can a political culture "die" or rot?

- **Social Capital:** Political scientist **Robert Putnam** argued that democracy depends on "Social Capital"—the networks of trust and cooperation between ordinary citizens.
    
- **The Decay:** In his famous book _Bowling Alone_, Putnam observed a "decay" in American political culture.
    
    - People are joining fewer clubs, unions, and civic groups.
        
    - **Trust** in government and in fellow citizens is declining.
        
    - **Result:** As people become isolated ("atomized"), they become cynical. Without a culture of trust and participation, democratic institutions cannot function effectively, leading to polarization and apathy.

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Key Theory - The Civic Culture:** Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba identified three types of political culture:
    
    1. **Parochial:** Citizens don't care/know about government (e.g., remote tribes).
        
    2. **Subject:** Citizens obey the government but don't participate (e.g., dictatorships).
        
    3. **Participant:** Citizens are active and challenge the government (e.g., democracies).
        
    
    - _Tip:_ They argued a stable democracy needs a **"Civic Culture"**—a mix of mostly Participant but with enough Subject loyalty to maintain stability.
        
- **Exam Terminology:** **"Social Capital"** is a buzzword. Use it to describe the "glue" that holds society together (trust/networks).
    
- **Context:** "Political Socialization" explains why you are likely to vote for the same party as your parents.
