### 16.2 Voting Systems ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** The "rules of the game" determine the winner. You cannot understand why the US has two parties while Israel has ten without understanding the difference between Plurality and Proportional systems.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is one of the most technical and frequently tested areas in political science. Questions often ask to compare FPTP and PR, or to explain the mechanics of a specific system.
    

---

A **Voting System** (or Electoral System) is the set of rules that determines how elections are conducted and how the results are determined. Crucially, it is the formula that translates **Votes** (from the people) into **Seats** (in the legislature).

Different formulas produce drastically different outcomes, even if the voters cast the exact same ballots. Political scientists broadly categorize these systems into two families: **Majoritarian/Plurality Systems** and **Proportional Representation (PR) Systems**.

#### 1. Plurality / Majoritarian Systems

These systems emphasize governability and stability. They typically use single-member districts (one MP per geographic area).

- **First-Past-The-Post (FPTP) / Single Member Plurality:**
    
    - **How it works:** The country is divided into districts. In each district, the candidate with the **most** votes wins. They do _not_ need a majority (over 50%), just more than anyone else (a plurality).
        
    - **Where:** USA, UK, Canada, India, Bangladesh.
        
    - **Pros:** Simple to understand; usually creates a strong single-party government; close link between MP and constituents.
        
    - **Cons:** High "disproportionality." A party can get 40% of the national vote but 60% of the seats. Small parties (like Greens) get almost no seats even with millions of votes because their support is spread thin.
        
- **Two-Round System (Run-off):**
    
    - **How it works:** To win, a candidate needs a true majority (50% + 1). If no one gets 50% in the first round, the top two candidates face off in a second round.
        
    - **Where:** France (Presidential and National Assembly).
        
    - **Pros:** Ensures the winner has broad support from the majority of people.
        
- **Alternative Vote (AV) / Ranked Choice Voting:**
    
    - **How it works:** Voters rank candidates (1, 2, 3...). If no one wins 50%, the last-place candidate is eliminated, and their votes are redistributed to the voters' second choices.
        
    - **Where:** Australia.
        
    - **Pros:** Eliminates the "spoiler effect" (voting for a small party doesn't help the candidate you hate win).
        

#### 2. Proportional Representation (PR) Systems

These systems emphasize fairness and representation. They use multi-member districts (many MPs representing one large area).

- **List-PR (Party List):**
    
    - **How it works:** Voters vote for a **Party**, not an individual. If a party gets 30% of the vote, they get roughly 30% of the seats in parliament. The party publishes a list of candidates; if they win 10 seats, the top 10 people on the list get in.
        
    - **Where:** Most of Europe (Netherlands, Spain), Israel, Brazil.
        
    - **Pros:** Highly fair; minorities and small parties get represented; few "wasted votes."
        
    - **Cons:** Often leads to unstable coalition governments; weak link between voters and specific representatives (you can't "fire" your local MP).
        
- **Single Transferable Vote (STV):**
    
    - **How it works:** A complex ranking system in multi-member districts. Voters rank candidates. A "quota" is set to win a seat. Surplus votes from winners are transferred to other candidates.
        
    - **Where:** Ireland, Malta.
        
    - **Pros:** The most sophisticated system; allows choice between candidates of the _same_ party.
        

#### 3. Mixed Systems

Attempts to get the "best of both worlds."

- **Mixed Member Proportional (MMP):**
    
    - **How it works:** Voters get **two votes**: one for a local candidate (FPTP) and one for a party (PR). The parliament is filled half with local winners, and then "top-up" seats are added from the party lists to ensure the final total matches the % of the party vote.
        
    - **Where:** Germany, New Zealand.
        
    - **Pros:** Keeps local MPs but ensures the overall result is fair (proportional).
        

#### 4. Comparison Table

|**Feature**|**First-Past-The-Post (FPTP)**|**Proportional Representation (PR)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Principle**|Winner-takes-all|Proportionality (Fair shares)|
|**Districts**|Single-Member (Small)|Multi-Member (Large)|
|**Government**|Usually Single-Party Majority|Usually Coalition|
|**Party System**|Two-Party System|Multi-Party System|
|**Priority**|Stability & Effectiveness|Fairness & Representation|

### Important Notes 📝

- **Duverger's Law:** I cannot stress this enough—it connects this topic to the "Parties" topic. _The electoral system shapes the party system._ FPTP creates two parties; PR creates many parties.
    
- **Gerrymandering:** In FPTP systems, politicians can redraw district boundaries to favor their own party. This manipulates the result without changing a single vote. This is largely impossible in PR systems.
    
- **Wasted Votes:** In FPTP, if you vote for a losing candidate (or for a winner who already has enough votes), your vote technically "does nothing" to affect the result. In PR, almost every vote helps add to the party's total count.
