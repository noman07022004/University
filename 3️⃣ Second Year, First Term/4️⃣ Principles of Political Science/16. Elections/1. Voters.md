### 16.1 Voters ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** The voter is the atomic unit of democracy. Understanding _who_ votes, _why_ they vote, and _how_ they decide is foundational to political sociology and behavioralism.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Questions often focus on "Voting Behavior" (determinants like class, age, party ID) or the decline in "Voter Turnout."
    

To understand **Voters**, we must look at three distinct aspects: **The Franchise** (who is allowed to vote), **Turnout** (who actually shows up), and **Voting Behavior** (how they make their choice).

#### 1. The Franchise (Suffrage)

The history of democracy is largely the history of expanding the franchise (the right to vote).

- **Evolution:** Originally, voting was restricted to property-owning males (based on the idea that only those with a "stake" in society should govern). Over the 19th and 20th centuries, this expanded to include non-property owners, racial minorities, and finally women (Universal Adult Suffrage).
    
- **Current Standard:** Today, the only common restrictions are age (usually 18+), citizenship, and sometimes criminal record (felony disenfranchisement).
    

#### 2. Voter Turnout

Having the _right_ to vote does not mean one _will_ vote. **Voter Turnout** is the percentage of eligible voters who cast a ballot.

- **The Puzzle:** Why is turnout declining in many established democracies?
    
    - **Voter Fatigue:** Too many elections (local, state, national) lead to exhaustion (common in the USA/Switzerland).
        
    - **Registration Barriers:** In some countries (like the US), the voter must actively register. In others (like Sweden), the state automatically registers everyone, leading to higher turnout.
        
    - **Political Efficacy:** If voters feel "my vote doesn't count" or "all politicians are the same," they stay home.
        

#### 3. Theories of Voting Behavior

The most academically rigorous part of this topic is explaining _why_ a voter chooses Candidate A over Candidate B. Political scientists use three main models:

- **A. The Sociological Model (Columbia School):**
    
    - **Premise:** "A person thinks, politically, as he is, socially."
        
    - **Factors:** Voting is determined by group membership—social class, religion, ethnicity, and region.
        
    - **Example:** A factory worker votes for the Labor/Socialist party because his coworkers and family do. It is a badge of social identity.
        
- **B. The Psychological Model (Michigan School):**
    
    - **Premise:** The key factor is **Party Identification** (Party ID).
        
    - **Factors:** Voters develop a deep psychological attachment to a party early in life (often from parents). This acts as a filter; they interpret news through this partisan lens.
        
    - **Example:** A "strong Democrat" will vote for the Democratic candidate even if the candidate is weak, because loyalty overrides policy details.
        
- **C. The Rational Choice Model (Economic Model):**
    
    - **Premise:** Voters are rational actors who act in their own self-interest.
        
    - **Factors:** They perform a cost-benefit analysis. They vote for the party that will provide the most personal benefit (lower taxes, better jobs).
        
    - **Issue Voting:** This model suggests voters look at specific policies ("I am voting for the Green party because I want to stop climate change").
        

#### 4. Retrospective vs. Prospective Voting

- **Retrospective Voting:** Looking backward. "Am I better off now than I was 4 years ago?" If yes, re-elect the incumbent. If no, vote for the challenger. This is very common.
    
- **Prospective Voting:** Looking forward. "Which candidate has the best plan for the future?" This requires more effort and research from the voter.

### Important Notes 📝

- **The Paradox of Voting (Downs):** Anthony Downs pointed out that strictly speaking, it is "irrational" to vote. The chance of _your single vote_ deciding an election is near zero, yet the "cost" (time, effort) is high. Therefore, people vote not for rational gain, but out of a sense of **Civic Duty**.
    
- **Class Dealignment:** In modern times, the link between social class and voting is weakening (the Sociological Model is fading). Working-class voters now often vote for conservative parties, and wealthy educated voters often support left-liberal parties.
    
- **Swing Voters:** These are voters with no strong party attachment. They decide elections because they are the only ones who can be persuaded to change their minds.
