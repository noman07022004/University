## 16. Elections ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Elections are the defining feature of representative democracy. They are the primary mechanism by which "the people" exercise their sovereignty. Understanding the mechanics of voting is crucial for understanding why parliaments look the way they do.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is a highly technical and frequent exam topic. Questions often ask to compare **First-Past-The-Post** and **Proportional Representation** systems, or to explain factors influencing **Voter Behavior**.


---

An election is a formal decision-making process by which a population chooses an individual to hold public office. It is the link between the rulers and the ruled. In modern mass societies, we cannot all gather in a town square to decide policy (Direct Democracy), so we elect representatives to do it for us. This section examines the two key components of this process: the **Voters** (who gets to participate and why they make their choices) and the **Voting Systems** (the mathematical rules that translate votes into seats).

---

### 16.1 Voters (Suffrage and Behavior)

The history of democracy is largely the history of the expansion of the franchise (the right to vote).

- **Evolution of Suffrage:**
    
    - **Restriction:** Initially, voting was restricted to white, property-owning men. It was believed that only those with "a stake in society" (property) were rational enough to vote.
        
    - **Expansion:** Over the 19th and 20th centuries, this expanded to include non-property owners, racial minorities, and finally women (Universal Adult Suffrage).
        
- **Voter Turnout:**
    
    - Not everyone who _can_ vote _does_ vote. Turnout is lower in the US (~50-60%) compared to Europe (~70-80%).
        
    - **Rational Choice Theory (The Paradox of Voting):** Economists argue voting is "irrational." The "cost" (time, effort) is high, and the chance of one vote deciding the election is near zero. Yet, millions vote. Why? Because of a sense of **civic duty** or social pressure.
        
- **Voter Behavior (How do they decide?):**
    
    - **Sociological Model (The Columbia School):** "A person thinks, politically, as he is, socially." People vote based on group identity (Class, Religion, Ethnicity). e.g., "I am a worker, so I vote Labour."
        
    - **Psychological Model (The Michigan School):** Focuses on **Party Identification**. Voters have a deep emotional attachment to a party (like a sports team) inherited from parents.
        
    - **Rational Choice (Issue Voting):** Voters act as consumers, evaluating which candidate offers the best policy "product" for their personal interest.
        

### 16.2 Voting Systems (Translating Votes into Seats)

The rules of the game determine the winner. Two main families of systems exist:

**A. Plurality/Majoritarian Systems (The "Winner-Take-All" approach)**

- **First-Past-The-Post (FPTP) / Single Member District (SMD):**
    
    - **How it works:** The country is divided into districts. The candidate with the _most_ votes in a district wins. You don't need 50% + 1 (a majority), just more than anyone else (a plurality).
        
    - **Effect:** It exaggerates the victory of the largest party. A party with 40% of the vote can win 60% of the seats.
        
    - **Pros:** Simplicity; usually produces a strong, stable, single-party government.
        
    - **Cons:** Wasted votes (if you vote for a loser, your vote counts for nothing); unfair to small parties.
        
    - **Used in:** USA, UK, Canada, Bangladesh.
        

**B. Proportional Representation (PR) Systems (The "Fair Share" approach)**

- **Party List PR:**
    
    - **How it works:** Voters vote for a _party_, not a person. If Party A gets 30% of the national vote, they get 30% of the seats in parliament.
        
    - **Effect:** Parliament is a "mirror" of the nation. Even small minority views are represented.
        
    - **Pros:** Highly fair; fewer wasted votes; encourages diversity.
        
    - **Cons:** Often leads to unstable coalition governments; gives power to extremist parties; breaks the link between a specific representative and a local district.
        
    - **Used in:** Most of Europe (e.g., Netherlands, Sweden), Israel, Brazil.
        

**C. Mixed Systems**

- **Mixed Member Proportional (MMP):** Combines both. You get two votes: one for a local candidate (FPTP) and one for a national party (PR). Used in **Germany** and New Zealand.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Gerrymandering:** A critical concept for Plurality systems. It involves redrawing district boundaries to favor a specific party (e.g., packing all opposition voters into one district so they win that one easily, but lose everywhere else).
    
- **Tactical Voting:** In FPTP systems, voters often don't vote for their favorite candidate but for the "least bad" one who has a chance of winning, to prevent "wasting" their vote.
    
- **Duverger's Law (Recap):** Always remember: FPTP = Two Parties; PR = Multi-Party.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh uses the **First-Past-The-Post** system for its 300 parliamentary seats. This is why election results often show huge seat majorities (e.g., 250+ seats) even if the popular vote was closer.
