### 6.1 Problem of Distributive Justice ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the central economic question of political science. Every society produces a certain amount of wealth ("the pie"). The problem of distributive justice is: _How do we cut the pie?_ Understanding this requires grasping the conflict between competing values like Equality, Merit, and Need.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Exams often ask students to define **Distributive Justice** or to compare different principles of distribution (e.g., "To each according to his need" vs. "To each according to his ability").
    

---

**Introduction: Who Gets What and Why?** The **Problem of Distributive Justice** concerns the moral principles guiding the allocation of benefits and burdens in a society. In any society, resources (money, jobs, healthcare, honor) are **scarce**. Everyone wants them, but there isn't enough for everyone to have everything. Therefore, we need a set of rules to decide who gets what. The "problem" arises because different people believe in different rules.

**1. The Three Components of the Problem** To analyze distributive justice, we must answer three questions:

1. **What is being distributed?** (Income, wealth, opportunities, status, healthcare?)
    
2. **To whom is it distributed?** (All citizens? Only workers? Only the needy?)
    
3. **On what basis?** (This is the core debate—what serves as the justification?)
    

**2. Competing Principles of Distribution** There is no single "right" way to distribute goods. Political philosophers identify four main competing principles. The "problem" is that these principles often contradict each other.

- **A. Equality (Strict Egalitarianism):**
    
    - _Principle:_ Everyone should get exactly the same share.
        
    - _Logic:_ Since all humans are morally equal, inequality is arbitrary and wrong.
        
    - _Problem:_ It ignores effort. If the lazy person gets the same as the hardworking person, why work hard?
        
- **B. Need (Socialism/Marxism):**
    
    - _Principle:_ Resources should be distributed to those who need them most.
        
    - _Famous Quote:_ **"From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs"** (Karl Marx).
        
    - _Logic:_ Suffering (hunger, sickness) should be eliminated first. A rich man doesn't "need" a second yacht; a poor man "needs" bread.
        
    - _Problem:_ Who defines "need"? Is a TV a need?
        
- **C. Desert/Merit (Aristotle/Meritocracy):**
    
    - _Principle:_ Resources should go to those who _deserve_ them based on their contribution, talent, or hard work.
        
    - _Logic:_ It is fair to reward those who produce more value for society.
        
    - _Problem:_ People are born with different talents. Is it fair that a genius gets rich while a slow learner stays poor, even if both try their hardest?
        
- **D. Entitlement/Choice (Libertarianism/Nozick):**
    
    - _Principle:_ Resources should go to whoever acquired them freely through trade or luck, without fraud.
        
    - _Logic:_ If I make $1 million by selling a book people want to buy, that money is mine. Taking it away (taxation) is theft.
        
    - _Focus:_ Focuses on the _Process_ (was the transaction free?), not the _Outcome_.
        

**3. Procedural vs. End-State Justice**

This is a critical distinction for understanding the debate (e.g., between Rawls and Nozick).

- **End-State Justice (Patterned):**
    
    - Looks at the **Result**.
        
    - _Example:_ "A society is just only if the gap between rich and poor is small."
        
    - If the result looks "wrong" (e.g., 1% own 90% of wealth), the state must intervene to redistribute.
        
- **Procedural Justice (Historical):**
    
    - Looks at the **Process**.
        
    - _Example:_ "A society is just if everyone played by the rules."
        
    - If the game was fair (no cheating/stealing), then _whatever_ result occurs is just, even if one person wins everything and everyone else goes broke.
        

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Aristotle’s Formula:** Aristotle defined justice as **"Treating equals equally and unequals unequally."** The problem is deciding _what_ makes us unequal (Merit? Need? Birth?).
    
- **Exam Strategy:** If asked "What is the problem of distributive justice?", do not just list solutions. Explain the **Tension**. For example: "The tension is that rewarding _Merit_ often leads to _Inequality_, while ensuring _Equality_ often punishes _Merit_."
    
- **Real World Application:** Progressive Taxation (Taxing the rich more) is a debate about distributive justice. Is it a penalty on success (Merit) or a necessary adjustment for fairness (Equality/Need)?
