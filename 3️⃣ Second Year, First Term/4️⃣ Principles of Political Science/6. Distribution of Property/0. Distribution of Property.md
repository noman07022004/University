## 6. Distribution of Property ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This topic addresses "Distributive Justice"—the central ethical question of modern politics. It asks how wealth, rights, and opportunities should be divided. Understanding Rawls and Marx is non-negotiable for political science students.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** **John Rawls** is the most important political philosopher of the 20th century. Exams almost always include a question on his "Veil of Ignorance" or "Difference Principle." Additionally, Marx's concept of "Alienation" is a frequent essay topic.

---

Politics is often described as "who gets what, when, and how." This is the problem of Distributive Justice. It concerns the moral framework guiding the allocation of economic goods (income, property) and social goods (rights, status) among members of a society. This section navigates the debate between those who see the free market as the ultimate distributor of property (Classical Liberals), those who argue for state intervention to ensure fairness (Rawlsian Liberals), and those who view private property itself as a source of human misery (Marxists).

---

### 6.1 The Problem of Distributive Justice & Markets

- **The Core Problem:** Resources are scarce, but human wants are unlimited. Society must decide on a mechanism to distribute these scarce resources. Is it "to each according to his need," "to each according to his merit," or "to each according to his luck"?
    
- **Property and Markets:**
    
    - **Lockean View:** Private property is a natural right. Markets are the most efficient way to exchange property. If exchanges are voluntary, the outcome is just.
        
    - **Market Limitation:** While markets are efficient, they are amoral. They can lead to vast inequality where some starve while others hoard wealth, regardless of "merit."
        

### 6.2 Rawls' Theory of Justice (Justice as Fairness)

In his 1971 book _A Theory of Justice_, **John Rawls** revolutionized political philosophy by reviving the Social Contract tradition to answer how property should be distributed.

- **The Original Position:** Rawls asks us to imagine a hypothetical scenario before society is formed.
    
- **The Veil of Ignorance:** In this position, no one knows their place in society—their class, race, gender, intelligence, or strength. You don't know if you will be born a billionaire or a beggar.
    
- **The Decision:** From behind this veil, rational people would choose a system that protects them even if they end up in the worst position (Maximin Strategy).
    
- **The Two Principles of Justice:**
    
    1. **The Liberty Principle:** Everyone has the maximum amount of basic liberty compatible with a similar liberty for others.
        
    2. **The Difference Principle:** Social and economic inequalities are allowed _only if_ they benefit the **least advantaged** members of society.
        
    
    - _Meaning:_ You can have rich doctors and poor janitors, but only if the high pay for doctors incentivizes them to provide better care that ultimately helps the janitor more than strict equality would.
        

### 6.3 Criticism of Rawls

Rawls' theory faces strong opposition from both the Right (Libertarians) and the Left (Socialists/Communitarians).

- **Libertarian Criticism (Robert Nozick):**
    
    - In _Anarchy, State, and Utopia_, Nozick argues that Rawls violates property rights.
        
    - **Entitlement Theory:** If I acquire wealth fairly (without stealing), the state has no right to take it (tax it) to give to the poor, even if it helps them. Redistribution is "forced labor."
        
- **Communitarian Criticism (Michael Sandel):**
    
    - Rawls treats the "self" as unencumbered by social ties. Communitarians argue justice isn't just about abstract rules but about the values and character of the specific community.
        

### 6.4 Marx's Idea of Alienation and Property

While Rawls tries to _fix_ inequality within a capitalist property system, **Karl Marx** argues that the system of private property itself dehumanizes people.

- **Property as Theft:** Marxists argue that capital is merely accumulated labor. The capitalist's profit comes from exploiting the surplus value created by the worker.
    
- **Alienation (_Entfremdung_):** Under capitalism and private property, workers lose their humanity. They are alienated in four ways:
    
    1. **From the Product:** The worker creates a car, but the capitalist owns it. The object of his labor becomes an alien power over him.
        
    2. **From the Process:** Work is not creative expression; it is forced drudgery for a wage.
        
    3. **From Species-Being:** Humans are naturally creative and social. Capitalism turns them into mechanical cogs, stripping them of their human essence.
        
    4. **From Other Workers:** Capitalism forces workers to compete against each other for jobs, destroying social solidarity.

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Exam Strategy (Rawls):** If you write about Rawls, _always_ define the **"Veil of Ignorance"** clearly. It is the most famous thought experiment in modern political philosophy.
    
- **The "Maximin" Rule:** This is the logic behind Rawls' choice. Rational people will try to **Max**imize the **Min**imum position (make the worst-case scenario as good as possible).
    
- **Contrast:**
    
    - **Nozick:** Justice is about the _process_ (fair acquisition).
        
    - **Rawls:** Justice is about the _outcome_ (fair distribution/fairness to the poor).
        
    - **Marx:** Justice requires _abolishing_ the source of injustice (private property).
