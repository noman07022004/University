### 8.4 Electoral Systems ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** The electoral system is the "game engine" of democracy. It determines who wins power. A slight change in these rules can completely change the election result, even if the people vote exactly the same way.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Highly frequent. Exams almost always ask to **compare "First Past the Post" with "Proportional Representation"** or to explain **Duverger’s Law**.
    

---

**Introduction: Translating Votes into Seats** An electoral system is the set of rules that decides how votes cast by citizens are translated into seats in the legislature. It is not just about counting; it is about _math_. Different systems prioritize different values: some prioritize **Stability** (strong government), while others prioritize **Fairness** (representation).

**1. Plurality/Majoritarian Systems** The most common form is the **"First Past the Post" (FPTP)** system (technically called Single Member Plurality).

- **How it Works:**
    
    - The country is divided into geographical constituencies (e.g., 300 in Bangladesh).
        
    - In each constituency, candidates compete.
        
    - The candidate with the **most votes** wins the seat.
        
    - _Crucial:_ You do not need 50% + 1 (a majority); you just need one more vote than the second-place person (a plurality).
        
- **Examples:** **Bangladesh**, USA, UK, India.
    
- **Pros:**
    
    - **Simplicity:** Easy to understand.
        
    - **Stability:** It tends to produce strong, single-party governments (because it boosts the winner's seat count).
        
    - **Link:** Creates a direct link between the MP and the local voters.
        
- **Cons:**
    
    - **Disproportionality:** A party can get 40% of the national vote but 60% of the seats.
        
    - **Wasted Votes:** If you vote for a losing candidate, your vote literally counts for nothing in the final parliament.
        
    - **Exclusion:** Small parties (like Greens or religious minorities) rarely win seats unless their support is concentrated in one area.
        

**2. Proportional Representation (PR)**

This system is designed to ensure that the parliament mirrors the national vote share.

- **How it Works:**
    
    - People usually vote for a **Party**, not an individual candidate.
        
    - The country is often one big constituency (or huge multi-member districts).
        
    - **The Formula:** If Party A gets 20% of the votes, they get roughly 20% of the seats.
        
- **Examples:** Israel, Netherlands, Brazil.
    
- **Pros:**
    
    - **Fairness:** Every vote counts. The parliament accurately reflects the diversity of public opinion.
        
    - **Inclusion:** Small parties and minorities can easily get representation.
        
- **Cons:**
    
    - **Instability:** It rarely produces a single-party majority. This leads to **Coalition Governments**, which can be weak and slow to act.
        
    - **Extremism:** It allows radical/extremist parties to enter parliament.
        
    - **No Local Link:** MPs are often chosen from a central "Party List," so they answer to party bosses, not local voters.
        

**3. Mixed Systems** Some countries try to get the "best of both worlds" (e.g., **Mixed Member Proportional - MMP**).

- **Mechanism:** Voters get two votes: one for a local candidate (FPTP) and one for a party (PR).
    
- **Example:** Germany, New Zealand.
    
- **Result:** You get a local MP _and_ a proportional parliament.
    

**4. Duverger’s Law** This is a famous theory in political science proposed by Maurice Duverger.

- **The Law:** The electoral system determines the party system.
    
    - **FPTP** tends to create a **Two-Party System** (e.g., USA: Democrats vs. Republicans; Bangladesh: AL vs. BNP historically).
        
    - **PR** tends to create a **Multi-Party System** (e.g., Italy, Israel).
        
- **Why?** In FPTP, voters know small parties can't win, so they vote strategically for the "lesser of two evils," squeezing out the third parties.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh uses **First Past the Post (FPTP)** for its 300 parliamentary seats. This explains why politics in Bangladesh has historically been dominated by two major alliances (AL and BNP), fitting Duverger's Law.
    
- **Gerrymandering:** In FPTP systems, the government can manipulate the boundaries of constituencies to favor their own party. This is called Gerrymandering.
    
- **Exam Strategy:** If asked "Which system is better?", the answer depends on the goal. For a developing nation needing decisive leadership, FPTP might be preferred. For a divided nation needing consensus, PR might be safer.
