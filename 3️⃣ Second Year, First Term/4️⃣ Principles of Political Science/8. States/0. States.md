## 8. States ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This topic shifts from abstract theory to practical comparative politics. Understanding how states are structured (Federal vs. Unitary) and how they function (Electoral Systems) is essential for analyzing real-world political systems.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Extremely high exam frequency. Questions asking for the differences between **Unitary and Federal systems**, or asking to distinguish between **Effective and Failed states**, are standard in political science examinations.
    

---

While "The State" (Topic 1) was a philosophical concept, "States" (Topic 8) refers to the actual machinery of government as it exists in the real world. A functioning state is not just a ruler; it is **Institutionalized Power**. This means power is attached to an office (like the Presidency), not a person. When the President leaves, the power stays with the office. This section examines how this power is organized geographically (Federalism), how it is populated (Electoral Systems), how successful it is (Effective vs. Failed), and its relationship with the economic market.

---

### 8.1 Institutionalized Power & State Capacity

A state is more than just a government; it is a permanent set of institutions (courts, bureaucracy, police, military).

- **Institutionalization:** In modern states, power is "depersonalized." Citizens obey the _law_, not the _individual_.
    
- **State Capacity:** This refers to the ability of the state to actually carry out its basic functions: controlling territory, taxing the population, and enforcing laws. Based on this capacity, states are classified as:
    
    - **Effective States:** Control their territory, have low corruption, and provide public goods (e.g., Japan, Germany).
        
    - **Weak States:** Have trouble enforcing laws, high crime/corruption, and "penetration" of the state by criminal gangs or warlords (e.g., parts of Latin America or Africa).
        
    - **Failed States:** Have completely lost "the monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force." There is no national government authority; the country is ruled by warlords or is in anarchy (e.g., Somalia, Haiti).
        

### 8.2 Unitary and Federal Systems (Territorial Organization)

How is power distributed across the territory?

1. **Unitary Systems:**
    
    - **Definition:** All power is concentrated in the central (national) government. Local governments exist only because the center _allows_ them to. The center can abolish them at will.
        
    - **Examples:** UK, France, Japan, Bangladesh.
        
    - **Pros:** Uniform laws, efficiency, national unity.
        
    - **Cons:** Ignores local differences, can be too distant from people.
        
2. **Federal Systems:**
    
    - **Definition:** Power is _constitutionally divided_ between the central government and regional governments (states/provinces). Both levels are sovereign in their own spheres; the center cannot simply abolish the states.
        
    - **Examples:** USA, India, Germany, Brazil.
        
    - **Pros:** Protects minorities, allows local experimentation ("laboratories of democracy"), checks central power.
        
    - **Cons:** Duplication of services, slower decision-making, conflicts between center and state.
        

### 8.3 Electoral Systems

How do votes translate into seats in the legislature? This determines the political landscape.

1. **Single-Member District (SMD) / First-Past-The-Post:**
    
    - **Mechanism:** The country is divided into districts. One person wins per district. The candidate with the _most_ votes wins (even if it's less than 50%).
        
    - **Result:** Usually leads to a **Two-Party System** (Duverger's Law). Small parties get no seats even if they have national support.
        
    - **Example:** USA, UK.
        
2. **Proportional Representation (PR):**
    
    - **Mechanism:** Citizens vote for a party, not a person. If a party gets 20% of the national vote, they get roughly 20% of the seats in parliament.
        
    - **Result:** Leads to a **Multi-Party System**. Small parties are represented, necessitating coalition governments.
        
    - **Example:** Most of Europe, Israel.
        

### 8.4 States and the Economy

The state's relationship with the economy is a defining characteristic of its regime.

- **Laissez-Faire (Minimal State):** The state generally stays out of the economy, providing only defense and property rights (Classical Liberal model).
    
- **Welfare State:** The state redistributes wealth to provide a safety net (health, education, unemployment benefits) while maintaining a capitalist market (e.g., Sweden).
    
- **Statism:** The state is the primary investor and controller of the economy, often owning key industries (State-Owned Enterprises), common in developing nations or socialist regimes.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Duverger's Law:** A crucial concept for exams. It states: _"Single-Member District systems tend to produce Two-Party systems."_ (Because people don't want to "waste" their vote on a third party that can't win the district).
    
- **Federalism Distinction:** A "Confederation" is different from a "Federation." In a Confederation (like the EU or the old US South), the _regions_ are sovereign and the center is weak. In a Federation, the center is strong but shares power.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh is a **Unitary State**. All power flows from Dhaka. This is often a relevant example if the exam allows local context.
    
- **Failed State vs. Weak State:** The line is blurry, but the key is _monopoly of force_. If the government cannot safely walk into a neighborhood because a gang controls it, the state is failing in that area.
