### 8.3 Unitary and Federal Systems ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is the fundamental structural choice for any state. It determines "who does what" geographically. You cannot understand the politics of countries like the USA (Federal) versus Bangladesh (Unitary) without grasping this distinction.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A standard exam question. You will often be asked to **compare and contrast Unitary and Federal systems** or to explain the reasons why a country might choose Federalism.
    

---

Introduction: The Geography of Power

Every state must decide how to distribute power across its territory. Does all the power reside in the capital city, or is it shared with the provinces?

This structure defines the relationship between the Center (National Government) and the Periphery (Local/Regional Governments).

1. Unitary Systems (Centralized Power)

In a unitary system, sovereignty rests exclusively with the Central National Government.

- **The Logic:** The local governments (districts, counties, municipalities) exist only because the central government allows them to. The central government can create, abolish, or redraw them at will.
    
- **Power Flow:** Top-down. Power flows from the Capital to the regions.
    
- **Examples:** **Bangladesh**, United Kingdom, France, Japan.
    
    - _Note:_ In Bangladesh, the Parliament in Dhaka makes laws for the entire country. A local council in Khulna cannot make a law that contradicts Dhaka, nor can it claim "sovereignty."
        

**2. Federal Systems (Shared Power)**

In a federal system, sovereignty is constitutionally **divided** between a Central Government and constituent units (States/Provinces).

- **The Logic:** Both the Central government and the State governments derive their power directly from the **Constitution**, not from each other. The Center cannot abolish a State (e.g., Washington D.C. cannot abolish Texas).
    
- **Dual Sovereignty:**
    
    - **Federal List:** Things the Center handles (Defense, Currency, Foreign Affairs).
        
    - **State List:** Things the States handle (Police, Education, Local Roads).
        
- **Examples:** **USA**, India, Canada, Germany, Australia.
    

**3. Comparison: Pros and Cons**

|**Feature**|**Unitary System**|**Federal System**|
|---|---|---|
|**Efficiency**|**High.** Uniform laws make it easy to govern. Decisions are made quickly.|**Low.** Duplication of services (two sets of courts, police) and endless debates.|
|**Unity**|**High.** Promotes a single national identity.|**Moderate.** Risk of secession or regionalism (e.g., Quebec in Canada).|
|**Local Needs**|**Low.** The capital may ignore remote rural problems.|**High.** "Laboratories of Democracy." Local states can experiment with laws that fit their culture.|
|**Accountability**|Clear. You know the central government is to blame.|Confusing. "Blame Game" between State and Center (e.g., during disasters).|

4. Why Choose Federalism?

Federalism is usually chosen by countries that are:

- **Large:** (Russia, USA, Brazil) – Too big to run from one city.
    
- **Diverse:** (India, Nigeria, Switzerland) – Different languages or religions live in different regions. Federalism gives them autonomy so they don't feel oppressed by the majority, preventing civil war.
    

5. Confederation (The Third Type)

Though rarer, it is important to know the third option.

- **Confederation:** A loose alliance of independent states. The "Center" is very weak and only does what the states allow it to do.
    
- **Example:** The European Union (EU) is close to a confederation. Historically, the USA (1781–1789) was a confederation before it became a federation.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh is a **Unitary** state. Article 1 of the Constitution declares it "a unitary, independent, sovereign Republic." This was chosen to ensure national unity after the liberation war and because the country is ethnically and linguistically homogeneous (mostly Bengali).
    
- **Exam Strategy:** When writing about Federalism, always mention the **"Supremacy of the Constitution."** In a unitary state, the Parliament is often supreme. In a federal state, the Constitution is supreme because it acts as the referee between the Center and the States.
    
- **Key Term - "Devolution":** This is when a Unitary state (like the UK) gives _some_ power to regions (like Scotland) but keeps the legal right to take it back. It is "Federalism-lite."
