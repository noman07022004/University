### 13.3 Television ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** For the last 60 years, television has been the dominant medium of politics. Even in the internet age, most voters still get their primary news from TV (or clips of TV shared on social media). It changed politics from a contest of _ideas_ to a contest of _images_.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A frequent topic. Exams often ask about the **"Kennedy-Nixon Debate"** as a turning point or the concept of the **"CNN Effect."**
    

---

**Introduction: The Visual Turn** Before television, politics was about words (newspapers, radio speeches). You judged a leader by what they _said_. With the arrival of **Television**, politics became visual. You judge a leader by how they _look_. Political communication shifted from being substantive (detailed arguments) to performative. TV demands drama, emotion, and good looks. If you have a "face for radio," you cannot win an election in the TV era.

**1. The Turning Point: Kennedy vs. Nixon (1960)**

This is the most famous case study in political science regarding TV.

- **The Event:** The first televised presidential debate between John F. Kennedy (JFK) and Richard Nixon.
    
- **The Contrast:**
    
    - **Nixon:** Refused makeup, had a 5 o'clock shadow, looked pale/sweaty, and wore a grey suit that blended into the background. He looked sick and shifty.
        
    - **Kennedy:** Was tanned, handsome, confident, and looked directly into the camera.
        
- **The Result:**
    
    - People who listened on the **Radio** thought **Nixon won** (because his arguments were better).
        
    - People who watched on **TV** thought **Kennedy won** (because he looked like a leader).
        
- **Lesson:** On TV, _style_ matters more than _substance_.
    

**2. The Personalization of Politics** Television focuses on the individual, not the party.

- **Close-ups:** The camera zooms in on the leader's face. We look for emotional cues—is he sweating? Is he smiling? Does he look sincere?
    
- **Impact:** This weakened political parties. Voters stopped voting for "The Platform" and started voting for "The Personality." It turned elections into a celebrity contest.
    

**3. The 24-Hour News Cycle (The CNN Effect)** Before cable TV (CNN, BBC, Al Jazeera), news was broadcast once a day (at 6:00 PM). Leaders had 24 hours to think and make decisions.

- **The Cycle:** Now, news is live 24/7.
    
- **Pressure:** If a bomb goes off, the President is expected to react _immediately_ on live TV. There is no time for deep reflection.
    
- **The CNN Effect:** The theory that 24-hour news coverage of a crisis (e.g., starving children in Somalia) forces the government to intervene militarily because the images are so emotionally powerful that the public demands action instantly.
    

**4. Infotainment and Punditry** To keep viewers watching for 24 hours, TV cannot just read boring facts. It must be entertaining.

- **Infotainment:** Blending "Information" and "Entertainment." News is presented with dramatic music, flashy graphics, and sensational headlines ("Breaking News!").
    
- **Punditry:** Instead of reporting news, TV fills time with "Talking Heads" (Pundits) arguing with each other. It creates conflict and drama, which gets higher ratings than boring agreement.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Key Term - "Telegenic":** A quality of looking good on TV. Being telegenic is now a mandatory requirement for political leadership.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** Television Talk Shows (e.g., _Tritiyo Matra_) are a massive part of political culture in Bangladesh. They serve as the primary arena where government and opposition clash publicly, often acting as a substitute for parliamentary debate.
    
- **Exam Strategy:** If asked "Has TV damaged democracy?", argue **Yes** (it reduced attention spans to 8-second soundbites) and **No** (it brought politics into the living room, making it accessible to illiterate people who couldn't read newspapers).
