### 9.1 Theories of Constitution ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** The Constitution is the "Supreme Law of the Land." It is the instruction manual for the state. You cannot understand how a government legally functions—or how citizens are protected—without understanding constitutional theory.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A fundamental topic. Exams frequently ask for the **classification of constitutions** (Written vs. Unwritten, Rigid vs. Flexible) or to explain the concept of **"Constitutionalism."**
    

---

**Introduction: The Rule Book of the State** A constitution is the fundamental body of principles or established precedents according to which a state is acknowledged to be governed. If the state is a game, the Constitution is the rule book. It defines the players (Legislature, Executive, Judiciary), their powers, and the fouls (Rights violations).

- **Definition:** K.C. Wheare defines a constitution as "that body of rules which regulates the ends of government, the organs of government, and their relation to one another."
    

**1. Classification of Constitutions** Constitutional theory classifies constitutions based on their form and how difficult they are to change.

- **A. Written vs. Unwritten (Codified vs. Uncodified):**
    
    - **Written (Codified):** The rules are written down in a single document.
        
        - _Examples:_ **USA, Bangladesh, India.**
            
        - _Feature:_ It is created at a specific time by a specific body (Constituent Assembly). It is usually superior to ordinary laws.
            
    - **Unwritten (Uncodified):** The rules are not found in one single book but are scattered across centuries of customs, court decisions, and ordinary laws.
        
        - _Examples:_ **United Kingdom (UK), New Zealand.**
            
        - _Feature:_ It evolves slowly over time. In the UK, the "Constitution" is just a collection of Acts of Parliament and traditions (Conventions).
            
- **B. Rigid vs. Flexible:**
    
    - **Rigid:** The constitution is very hard to change. It cannot be amended by a simple majority vote; it requires a special procedure (e.g., 2/3rds majority or a referendum).
        
        - _Example:_ **USA** (requires 3/4 of states to agree), **Bangladesh** (requires 2/3rds of Parliament).
            
        - _Theory:_ This ensures stability and protects minority rights from the whim of a temporary majority.
            
    - **Flexible:** The constitution can be changed just as easily as an ordinary law (50% + 1 vote).
        
        - _Example:_ **UK** (Parliament can change the constitution by passing a regular Act).
            
        - _Theory:_ This allows the state to adapt quickly to emergencies.
            

**2. The Theory of Constitutionalism** There is a difference between having a _Constitution_ and having _Constitutionalism_.

- **Concept:** Constitutionalism is the political philosophy that government authority is derived from and **limited by** a body of fundamental law.
    
- **Limited Government:** The core theory is that the government is _not_ all-powerful. Even the King or the President is under the law.
    
    - _Dictatorships_ often have Constitutions (usually very long and detailed ones), but they lack _Constitutionalism_ because the dictator ignores the limits.
        
- **Rule of Law:** Constitutionalism ensures "Government of laws, not of men."
    

**3. Functions of a Constitution** Theoretically, a constitution serves three main purposes:

1. **Constitution as Power Map:** It defines who has power. It creates the organs of the state (Parliament, Courts) and assigns their duties.
    
2. **Constitution as Shield:** It protects the rights of citizens (Fundamental Rights) from the state.
    
3. **Constitution as Symbol:** It represents the values and identity of the nation (e.g., "We the People...").
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh has a **Written and Rigid** Constitution. It was adopted on **November 4, 1972**. To amend it, Article 142 requires a **two-thirds majority** in Parliament.
    
- **The "Living Document":** A theory that a constitution is not dead paper; its meaning changes over time through judicial interpretation. For example, "Freedom of Speech" in 1787 meant writing pamphlets; today, the courts say it includes the Internet.
    
- **Exam Strategy:** If asked "Is an unwritten constitution bad?", argue that it depends on **Political Culture**. The UK has a strong culture of democracy, so they don't _need_ a written book to respect rights. In newer democracies, a written/rigid constitution is safer to prevent dictatorship.
