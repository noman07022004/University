## 9. Constitutions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A constitution is the "rulebook" of politics. Without understanding it, you cannot understand how a government is limited or how rights are protected. It forms the legal skeleton of the state.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** While sometimes grouped with "Judiciaries," specific questions on **Constitutional Supremacy** vs. **Parliamentary Supremacy** and the concept of **Judicial Review** are very common.

---

A Constitution is the fundamental law of a state. It determines the structure of the government, the distribution of powers between different organs (Legislature, Executive, Judiciary), and the rights of the citizens. It is often described as the **"supreme law of the land"** because all other laws must conform to it. Constitutions are not just legal documents; they are political manifestos that reflect the values and history of a nation. This section explores the theoretical basis of constitutions and the critical concept of their supremacy over ordinary legislation.

---

### 9.1 Theories of Constitutions

Constitutionalism is the political theory that government power should be limited by law. It is the opposite of Arbitrary Rule.

**A. Purpose of a Constitution**

1. **Empowerment:** It creates the institutions of government (e.g., establishing a Parliament or Presidency) and grants them authority.
    
2. **Limitation:** It restricts what the government can do (e.g., through a Bill of Rights) to prevent tyranny.
    
3. **Stability:** It provides a stable framework for political competition so that losing an election doesn't mean losing one's life.
    

**B. Types of Constitutions**

- **Written (Codified) vs. Unwritten (Uncodified):**
    
    - **Written:** The constitution is contained in a single document (e.g., USA, Bangladesh, India). It is created at a specific time.
        
    - **Unwritten:** There is no single document. The "constitution" is a collection of ancient customs, legal precedents, and ordinary laws (e.g., **United Kingdom**, New Zealand, Israel).
        
- **Rigid vs. Flexible:**
    
    - **Rigid:** Very difficult to amend. It requires a special procedure (e.g., 2/3rds majority or a referendum). Most written constitutions are rigid to protect them from temporary political whims (e.g., USA).
        
    - **Flexible:** Can be amended by the legislature just like any ordinary law (50% + 1 vote). The UK constitution is flexible.
        

### 9.2 Constitutional Supremacy

This concept answers the question: _Who is the ultimate master? The Parliament or the Constitution?_

- **Definition:** Constitutional Supremacy means the Constitution is the highest law. Any law passed by the legislature that conflicts with the Constitution is **null and void**.
    
- **Judicial Review:** To enforce supremacy, the Judiciary (Courts) is given the power of **Judicial Review**. This allows courts to strike down laws that violate the constitution.
    
    - _Origin:_ This principle was famously established in the US Supreme Court case **Marbury v. Madison (1803)**.
        
- **Contrast - Parliamentary Supremacy:**
    
    - In the **UK system**, Parliament is supreme. No court can strike down an Act of Parliament. The Constitution is whatever Parliament says it is at any given moment.
        
    - In **Constitutional Supremacy systems (USA, Bangladesh)**, the Parliament is _not_ supreme; it is limited by the written constitution.
        

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Key Case:** **Marbury v. Madison (1803)** is the most important legal case in political science history. It established **Judicial Review**. You _must_ cite this if writing about Constitutional Supremacy.
    
- **The "Living Document" Debate:** A major theoretical debate exists between "Originalists" (interpret the text as written in the past) and "Living Constitutionalists" (interpret the text to fit modern times).
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh has a **Written, Rigid, and Supreme** Constitution. The Supreme Court of Bangladesh can (and has) declared amendments illegal (e.g., the 8th Amendment case regarding the establishment of High Court benches outside Dhaka).
