## 4. Idea of Liberty ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Liberty is perhaps the most cherished value in modern liberal democracies. Grasping the distinction between "doing what you want" and "being free" is essential for understanding political ideology, rights, and justice.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** John Stuart Mill’s _On Liberty_ is a cornerstone text. Exam questions frequently require applying Mill’s "Harm Principle" to contemporary issues (like censorship or drug laws) or critiquing the limits of classical liberalism.

---

Liberty (or freedom) is the ability to act according to one's own will. However, in a political context, absolute liberty is impossible, as it would lead to the "State of War" described by Hobbes. Therefore, political science concerns itself with civil liberty: the nature and limits of the power which can be legitimately exercised by society over the individual. This section focuses heavily on John Stuart Mill, the most articulate defender of free speech and individuality in the English language. It moves from his central doctrine—the **"Harm Principle"**—to the utilitarian justifications for freedom, and concludes with the major critiques of the liberal view of liberty.

---

### 4.1 John S. Mill's Idea on Liberty

In his 1859 masterpiece _On Liberty_, J.S. Mill addressed a new threat. He was not afraid of a tyrant king, but of the **"Tyranny of the Majority"**—the social pressure and democratic laws that impose conformity on individuals.

- **The Harm Principle:** To protect the individual, Mill proposed one simple principle:
    
    > "The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant."
    
- **Two Spheres of Action:**
    
    - **Self-Regarding Actions:** Actions that affect _only_ the actor (e.g., what you read, what you wear, religious worship). In this sphere, the individual is sovereign. Society has **no right** to interfere, even if the person is harming themselves.
        
    - **Other-Regarding Actions:** Actions that affect _others_ (e.g., theft, assault, breach of contract). Society **can** intervene here to prevent harm.
        

### 4.2 Justification for Liberty

Why should we allow people to make mistakes or hold offensive views? Mill does not argue from "natural rights" (like Locke). As a Utilitarian, he argues that liberty is **useful**; it produces the best consequences for society in the long run.

1. **The Marketplace of Ideas (Freedom of Speech):** Mill argues we should never silence an opinion, even if we are sure it is false.
    
    - **If the opinion is true:** We lose the opportunity to exchange error for truth.
        
    - **If the opinion is false:** We lose the clearer perception of truth produced by its collision with error. (i.e., We need false arguments to keep our true beliefs "alive" and not just dead dogmas).
        
2. **Experiments in Living (Individuality):**
    
    - Progress requires genius, and geniuses are often eccentric(অদ্ভুত).
        
    - By allowing people to live differently ("experiments in living"), society discovers better ways of living. Conformity kills social progress; liberty fuels innovation.
        

### 4.3 Problems with Liberalism

While Mill's arguments are powerful, the classical liberal view of liberty faces significant criticism from other perspectives.

- **The Definition of "Harm":**
    
    - Critics argue that almost _no_ action is purely "self-regarding." If a person drinks heavily (a "victimless" crime), they may neglect their family or cost the healthcare system money. If "harm" is defined too broadly, liberty vanishes; if defined too narrowly, the state cannot protect the vulnerable.
        
- **Marxist/Socialist Critique:**
    
    - Liberals focus on **Negative Liberty** (freedom from interference). Marxists argue this is insufficient. A poor laborer is "free" to starve or take an exploitative job.
        
    - True liberty requires **Positive Liberty** (the actual capacity and resources to act). Without economic equality, legal liberty is a "formal" illusion for the working class.
        
- **Communitarian Critique:**
    
    - Thinkers like Michael Sandel argue that liberalism creates "atomized" individuals who care only about themselves. It ignores the fact that we are social beings shaped by our community obligations, not just unencumbered individuals making choices in a vacuum.

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Key Concept - Isaiah Berlin:** Although not in the subtopic list, exams often ask you to differentiate between **Negative Liberty** (absence of obstacles/interference—*"I am slave to no man"*) and **Positive Liberty** (presence of control/mastery—*"I am my own master"*). Mill advocates for _Negative Liberty_.

- **The Bridge Analogy:** Mill gives an example: If you see a man crossing an unsafe bridge, you can grab him to warn him (because he doesn't want to fall). But once he knows the risk, if he still wants to cross, you must let him. This perfectly illustrates the limit of state paternalism.

- **Exam Tip:** If asked about *"Hate Speech,"* apply Mill's reasoning. Mill would likely protect hate speech unless it incites immediate violence (a direct "harm"). He would argue that suppressing it drives it underground, whereas debating it exposes its stupidity.
