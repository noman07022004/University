### 2.3 Principle of Fairness (The Argument from Fair Play) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This theory attempts to fix the holes in the Social Contract. It explains why you have to obey the law even if you never explicitly "signed" anything and even if the law is annoying (Utilitarianism). It appeals to our basic moral intuition about "cheating."
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A sophisticated topic often used to separate top students. Exams may ask you to explain the **"Free Rider Problem"** or to discuss **H.L.A. Hart** or **John Rawls'** contribution to this theory.
    

---

**Beyond Consent and Utility** 

We have seen that **Consent** is hard to prove (most people never signed a contract), and **Utilitarianism** can be dangerous (sacrificing minorities). The **Principle of Fairness** (often called the Principle of Fair Play) offers a third way. It argues that political obligation arises not from a promise, but from the **receipt of benefits** from a cooperative scheme. If you benefit from the State, you have a moral duty not to be a "free rider" on the efforts of others.

---

### 1. The Core Argument (H.L.A. Hart and Rawls) 

The legal philosopher **H.L.A. Hart** (1955) and later **John Rawls** (1964) formalized this argument.

- **The Logic:**
    
    1. **Cooperative Scheme:** The State is a joint enterprise where citizens restrict their own liberty (pay taxes, obey laws) to produce a public good (security, rule of law).
        
    2. **Benefit:** You accept the benefits of this scheme(পরিকল্পনা/ ফন্দি/ ষড়্যন্ত্র) (e.g., your house isn't burned down, you use paved roads).
        
    3. **Cost:** Producing these benefits requires everyone to chip in.
        
    4. **Unfairness:** If you accept the benefits but refuse to pay the "cost" (obeying the law), you are taking advantage of the sacrifices of others.
        
    5. **Conclusion:** Therefore, fairness demands that you do your fair share. You owe your obedience not to the government directly, but to your **fellow citizens**.
        

### 2. The Free Rider Problem

This theory relies heavily on the economic concept of the **Free Rider**.

- **Definition:** A free rider is someone who consumes a resource without paying for it or contributing to its maintenance.
    
- **Example:** Imagine a group of neighbors agrees to clean a local park every Sunday. Everyone does it for a month, and the park looks great.
    
    - One neighbor, Bob, enjoys the clean park but stays home on Sundays to watch TV.
        
    - Bob is a **Free Rider**. He gets the benefit (clean park) without the burden (work).
        
    - According to the Principle of Fairness, Bob is acting immorally. He is treating his neighbors as "suckers."
        
- **Political Application:** The Tax Evader is a free rider. He enjoys the army protecting him and the police watching his street, but he lets _you_ pay for it.
    

### 3. Critique: Robert Nozick and the "Unsolicited Benefit" 

The libertarian philosopher **Robert Nozick** (in _Anarchy, State, and Utopia_) launched a famous attack on this theory. He argued that receiving a benefit doesn't create an obligation if you didn't _ask_ for it.

- **The Public Address System Example:**
    
    - Imagine your neighborhood sets up a PA system to play music and read news. Everyone takes turns running it for a day.
        
    - You never agreed to this. You never voted for it. But you open your window and _hear_ the music (you benefit).
        
    - One day, the neighbors knock on your door: "It's your turn to run the station today."
        
    - **The Question:** Do you have a duty to do it?
        
- **Nozick's Answer:** **No.** You cannot force a benefit on someone and then demand payment. If you buy me a pizza I didn't order, I might eat it, but I don't owe you money.
    
- **Implication for the State:** Since we cannot "opt out" of state benefits (we can't choose _not_ to be protected by the army), the benefits are "foisted" upon us. Therefore, simply receiving them does not create a duty to obey.
    

### 4. Acceptance vs. Receipt 

To save the theory from Nozick's critique, modern theorists distinguish between:

- **Mere Receipt:** Benefits fall into your lap unavoidably (e.g., National Defense). This might _not_ create an obligation.
    
- **Voluntary Acceptance:** You actively seek out the benefit (e.g., calling the fire department, sending kids to public school). This _does_ create an obligation.
    
- _Problem:_ This brings us back to the problem of "Tacit Consent"—what about the people who just stay quiet and survive? Do they owe nothing?

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Summary of Justifications:**
    
    - **Social Contract:** "I obey because I **promised**." (Focus: Will/Consent).
        
    - **Utilitarianism:** "I obey because it works/is **useful**." (Focus: Consequences).
        
    - **Fairness:** "I obey because it is **unfair** to cheat my neighbors." (Focus: Reciprocity/Moral Duty).
        
- **Exam Terminology:** Use the phrase **"Mutuality of Restrictions."** This means "I restrict my liberty (by obeying the law) only because you restrict yours." If you break the law, you break the mutuality.
    
- **Key Distinction:** Fairness is not about the relationship between _Subject and King_; it is about the relationship between _Citizen and Citizen_.
