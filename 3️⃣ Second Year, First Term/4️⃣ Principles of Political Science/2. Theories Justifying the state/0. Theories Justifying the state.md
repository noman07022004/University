## 2. Theories Justifying the State ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This topic tackles the core question of political philosophy: _Political Obligation_. Why do citizens have a moral duty to obey the law? Understanding these theories is crucial for analyzing the legitimacy of any government.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is a frequent exam area. Questions often ask students to compare "Consent" theories (Social Contract) with "Consequentialist" theories (Utilitarianism) or to discuss the "Fair Play" argument.

---
*Why should an individual obey the state?* This is the problem of Political Obligation. A state uses coercion (police, prisons, taxes) to enforce its will. To distinguish this from a gunman demanding your wallet, the state must offer a moral justification for its authority. Theoretical justifications generally fall into three categories: Consent (we agreed to it), Consequences (it brings the best results), or Fairness (we owe it to our fellow citizens). This section explores these three dominant frameworks used to legitimize state power.

---

### 2.1 Social Contract (The Argument from Consent)

The Social Contract theory, discussed in the previous section regarding the _origin_ of the state, is also the primary argument for its _justification_.

- **Voluntarism:** The core premise is that individuals are naturally free and equal. Therefore, no one has a natural right to rule over another. Legitimate authority can _only_ arise from the voluntary **consent** of the governed.
    
- **Explicit vs. Tacit Consent:**
    
    - **Explicit Consent:** A formal pledge (e.g., an oath of allegiance). However, most people never sign a formal contract with their state.
        
    - **Tacit Consent:** John Locke argued that by simply residing in a territory, walking on its roads, or owning property protected by its laws, a person gives "tacit" (silent) consent to the government and agrees to obey its laws.
        
- **Justification:** The state is justified because "we agreed to it" to escape the insecurity of the State of Nature. If the state breaks the contract (becomes tyrannical), the justification vanishes, and citizens are released from their obligation.
    

### 2.2 Utilitarianism (The Argument from Consequences)

Utilitarianism, associated with thinkers like **Jeremy Bentham** and **John Stuart Mill**, rejects the idea of "natural rights" or "social contracts" as fiction. Instead, it justifies the state based on its results.

- **Greatest Happiness Principle:** The moral worth of an action (or institution) is determined by its contribution to overall utility—defined as "the greatest happiness of the greatest number."
    
- **State as a Tool:** The state is not a sacred entity formed by a mythical contract. It is a tool. It is justified _only_ if the consequences of having a state (order, security, public goods) produce more happiness/utility than the consequences of anarchy (chaos, fear).
    
- **Obedience:** We obey the state not because we "promised" to, but because disobedience would lead to instability, which causes pain. However, if a revolution would produce _more_ total happiness than maintaining the status quo, Utilitarianism would justify the revolution.
    

### 2.3 Principle of Fairness (The Argument from Fair Play)

This theory, often associated with legal philosopher **H.L.A. Hart** and later **John Rawls**, focuses on the unfairness of "free-riding."

- **Cooperative Scheme:** The state is viewed as a large cooperative enterprise where citizens follow rules (pay taxes, obey traffic laws) to generate benefits for everyone (security, roads, rule of law).
    
- **The "Free Rider" Problem:** If you accept these benefits but refuse to follow the rules (e.g., you use the roads but don't pay taxes), you are a "free rider." You are taking advantage of the sacrifices of others.
    
- **Justification:** The state is justified in coercing you because it ensures fairness. You have a moral obligation to bear your share of the burden (obeying laws) because you have voluntarily accepted the benefits of the cooperation. It is a matter of **reciprocity**—fair play requires that we do not exploit the compliance of our fellow citizens.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Distinction Key:**
    
    - _Social Contract_ looks at the **Past** (Did I agree to this?).
        
    - _Utilitarianism_ looks at the **Future** (Will this produce good results?).
        
    - _Fairness_ looks at the **Present** relationship with others (Am I cheating my neighbors?).
        
- **Exam Tip:** A common critique of **Tacit Consent** (Locke) to use in essays is David Hume's objection: You cannot say someone "consented" to stay on a ship if the only alternative is jumping into the ocean and drowning. Similarly, poor citizens cannot simply "leave" the state; thus, their residence isn't truly voluntary.
    
- **Key Concept - "Legitimacy":** In political science, _legitimacy_ is the "right to rule." Power is the ability to force obedience; Legitimacy is the recognized right to request it. These theories explain how Power becomes Legitimacy.
