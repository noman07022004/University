## 2.1 Social Contract (The Argument from Consent) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** The Social Contract is the most dominant theory of political legitimacy in the modern world. It replaces the "Divine Right of Kings" with the idea that government is a human creation based on agreement. Understanding this is essential for understanding the concept of "citizenship" versus "subjecthood."
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is a core exam topic. Questions frequently ask for a critique of **Tacit Consent** or ask how the Social Contract creates **Political Obligation** (the moral duty to obey the law).

---

In Topic 1, we examined the Social Contract as a historical (or hypothetical) story about how the state _began_ (Origin). In Topic 2, we analyze it as a moral argument for why the state is _right_ (Justification). The central problem of political philosophy is **Political Obligation**: _Why do I have a moral duty to obey the law?_ The Social Contract theory answers this with **Voluntarism**: Since all humans are born free and equal, no one has a natural right to rule another. Therefore, legitimate authority can _only_ arise from the voluntary **consent** of the governed. We obey the state because we _promised_ to.

---

### 1. The Logic of Voluntarism

- **The Premise:** In the pre-political world, everyone is free.
    
- **The Act:** Individuals voluntarily transfer their right of self-government to a sovereign authority.
    
- **The Result:** The State has legitimacy, and the citizen has an obligation. This obligation is based on the moral principle _pacta sunt servanda_ ("agreements must be kept"). If you freely agree to a contract, you are bound by its terms.
    

### 2. The Problem of Consent 
The obvious problem is that very few people have ever actually signed a contract with their government. Most of us are just born into a country. If we never "signed," why are we bound? Theorists offer two solutions:

#### A. Explicit Consent (The Ideal)

- This is a clear, spoken, or written promise (e.g., an immigrant taking an Oath of Citizenship, or soldiers swearing allegiance).
    
- **Limitation:** This applies to only a tiny fraction of the population. If obligation depended _only_ on explicit consent, the state would have no authority over most native-born citizens.
    

#### B. Tacit Consent (The Practical Solution)

**John Locke** proposed the idea of "Tacit" (silent) Consent to solve the problem.

- **The Argument:** He argued that you do not need to sign a paper to consent. You consent by your **behavior**.
    
- **Locke's Criterion:**
    
    > _"Every man, that hath any possessions, or enjoyment, of any part of the dominions of any government, doth thereby give his tacit consent."_
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - Walking on the state's roads.
        
    - Owning property protected by the state's laws.
        
    - Simply residing within the territory.
        
- **Implication(অন্তর্নিহিত অর্থ):** By enjoying the benefits of the state, you implicitly agree to obey its laws. If you don't like it, your "exit option" is to leave.
    

### 3. Hypothetical Consent (Kant and Rawls) 
Later theorists, realizing that "Tacit Consent" is weak (see critique below), shifted the argument.

- **Immanuel Kant:** Argued the contract doesn't need to be a historical fact. It is an **idea of reason**. The state is justified if the laws are such that free and rational people _would have_ agreed to them.
    
- **John Rawls:** As we will see in Topic 6, Rawls uses the "Veil of Ignorance" to argue that justice is what rational people _would_ choose in a hypothetical situation.
    

### 4. Critique: The "Ship at Sea" Objection 
The most devastating critique of Tacit Consent comes from the philosopher **David Hume**.

- **The Analogy:** Hume argued that "residence" is not voluntary if you cannot leave. He compared a citizen to a man who is carried asleep onto a ship.
    
    - When he wakes up, the ship is in the middle of the ocean.
        
    - The captain says, "Obey my commands or jump into the sea and drown."
        
    - Can we say this man "consents" to the captain's rule just because he stays on the ship? No, because he has no viable(কার্যকর) alternative.
        
- **Application:** Poor citizens cannot simply "leave" their country (costs, visas, language barriers). Therefore, their staying is not a choice, and it cannot be counted as consent.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Key Distinction:**
    
    - **Divine Right:** God gave the King power; you must obey or go to Hell. (Pre-Modern).
        
    - **Social Contract:** _You_ gave the King power; you must obey because you promised. (Modern).
        
- **Exam Terminology:** **"Political Obligation"** is the key phrase here. Use it instead of just "obeying the law" to sound more academic.
    
- **Connections:** This topic connects directly to **Topic 9 (Constitutions)**—a Constitution is essentially the written record of the Social Contract.
    
- **Essay Tip:** When discussing Tacit Consent, always mention **Hume's Ship**. It shows critical thinking and deep reading.
