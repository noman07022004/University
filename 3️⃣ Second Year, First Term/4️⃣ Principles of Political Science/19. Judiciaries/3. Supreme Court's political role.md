### 19.3 Supreme Court's Political Role ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** It is a common misconception that courts are purely "legal" and "neutral." Understanding that the Supreme Court is a **political institution** that shapes public policy is arguably the most sophisticated concept in this entire section.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Questions on **"Judicial Review"** and **"Judicial Activism"** are extremely common in political science exams. You will often be asked: _“Is the Judiciary becoming a super-legislature?”_
    

---

While the Supreme Court is designed to be an impartial arbiter of the law, distinct from the rough-and-tumble of party politics, it inevitably plays a massive **political role**. This occurs because constitutional disputes are rarely just about technical legalities; they are usually about the fundamental values and power structures of the society.

When a court decides a case on abortion, election results, or the powers of the Prime Minister, it is making a political decision that affects millions of lives.

#### 1. Judicial Review

The primary engine of the Court's political power is **Judicial Review**.

- **Definition:** The power of the Supreme Court to declare a law passed by the Legislature or an action taken by the Executive as **null and void** (illegal) if it violates the Constitution.
    
- **Political Impact:** This allows a group of unelected judges to strike down the will of the democratically elected majority. It acts as a "check" on democracy to protect constitutional principles.
    
- **Origin:** This concept was most famously established in the US case _Marbury v. Madison_ (1803), and it is a core feature of the Bangladeshi and Indian legal systems.
    

#### 2. Judicial Activism vs. Judicial Restraint

Political scientists analyze _how_ judges use their power through two opposing philosophies. This is the central debate in modern jurisprudence.

- **Judicial Restraint:**
    
    - **Philosophy:** Judges should be cautious. They should strictly interpret the Constitution as it was written and defer to the elected branches (Parliament/Congress) whenever possible.
        
    - **Argument:** "We are not elected. If the people want a bad law, they have the right to have a bad law, as long as it is constitutional. It is not our job to fix society."
        
- **Judicial Activism:**
    
    - **Philosophy:** Judges should use their power to correct social injustices, especially when the other branches fail to act. They interpret the Constitution broadly as a "living document."
        
    - **Argument:** "The Parliament is paralyzed by politics. We must step in to protect the weak, minorities, and the environment."
        
    - **Example:** The US Supreme Court ending segregation (_Brown v. Board of Education_) or the Indian Supreme Court expanding the "Right to Life" to include the right to a clean environment.
        

#### 3. Policy Making

While legislatures _make_ law, Supreme Courts often effectively _rewrite_ it.

- **Filing the Gap:** When a law is vague, the Court’s interpretation sets the policy for the entire nation.
    
- **Public Interest Litigation (PIL):** In South Asia (India/Bangladesh), courts have become very active through PILs. Citizens can file a simple petition to the court about pollution or corruption, and the Court can issue direct orders to the government to fix the roads or clean the rivers. This effectively makes the Court an administrator.
    

#### 4. The Politicization of the Judiciary

Because the Court has so much political power, the process of selecting judges has become political.

- **Appointments:** In the USA, the nomination of a Supreme Court Justice is a fierce political battle between Democrats and Republicans. Each side wants a judge who shares their _ideology_.
    
- **Court Packing:** A political strategy where a government tries to expand the number of judges or appoint loyalists to ensure the Court rules in their favor.
    

---

### Important Notes 📝

- **Marbury v. Madison (1803):** You **must** memorize this case name. It is the genesis of Judicial Review in the American tradition. Chief Justice John Marshall famously wrote: _"It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."_
    
- **PIL (Public Interest Litigation):** This is highly relevant for students in Bangladesh or India. It is a unique innovation where the rules of "standing" (who can sue) are relaxed so that any citizen can sue on behalf of the poor or oppressed.
    
- **Counter-Majoritarian Difficulty:** This is an academic term for the inherent tension in democracy: _Why should 9 unelected lawyers be allowed to overrule 300 million people?_ The answer is usually: _To protect the minority from the tyranny of the majority._
