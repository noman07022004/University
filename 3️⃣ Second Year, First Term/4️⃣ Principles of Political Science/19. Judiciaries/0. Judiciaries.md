## 19. Judiciaries ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** The Judiciary is the guardian of the constitution. Understanding how judges interpret the law is essential for understanding how rights are protected and how the "Rule of Law" is maintained against the arbitrary power of the state.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is a significant exam topic. Questions frequently ask about the **Independence of the Judiciary**, the concept of **Judicial Review**, and the debate between **Judicial Activism** and **Judicial Restraint**.

---

The Judiciary is the branch of government empowered to decide legal disputes and interpret the law. While the Legislature makes the law and the Executive enforces it, the Judiciary applies it to specific cases. In a modern democracy, the judiciary must be **independent**—meaning judges must be free from political pressure to make fair decisions. This section explores the structure of the legal system (the Bench and Bar), the fundamental roles courts play in society, and the increasingly political nature of Supreme Courts in shaping national policy.

---

### 19.1 Courts, Bench, and Bar (Structure and Actors)

The legal system is composed of specific institutions and actors.

- **The Courts (Hierarchy):**
    
    - Most systems are hierarchical.
        
    - **Trial Courts:** The lowest level where cases are first heard (facts are established).
        
    - **Appellate Courts:** Higher courts that review the decisions of lower courts (focus on errors of law, not facts).
        
    - **Supreme Court:** The final court of appeal. Its decisions are binding on all lower courts.
        
- **The Bench and The Bar:**
    
    - **The Bench:** Refers collectively to the **Judges**. They are the impartial arbiters. In Common Law systems (like UK/Bangladesh), judges are often drawn from senior lawyers.
        
    - **The Bar:** Refers collectively to the **Lawyers** (Advocates/Attorneys). Their job is to represent clients and present arguments.
        
    - **The Relationship:** In an **Adversarial System** (used in former British colonies), the Judge acts as a neutral referee while opposing lawyers (The Bar) fight to prove their case.
        

### 19.2 Role of Courts

What purpose do courts serve in the political system?

1. **Adjudication (Dispute Resolution):** The primary function. Courts peacefully settle disputes between private citizens (Civil Law) or between the state and a citizen (Criminal Law), preventing vigilante justice.
    
2. **Interpretation of Law:** Laws written by parliament are often vague or general. Courts must interpret what the law actually _means_ in specific situations.
    
3. **Guardian of the Constitution:** Courts protect the "Rule of Law." They ensure that the government acts within legal limits and protects the fundamental rights of citizens (e.g., Habeas Corpus—preventing illegal detention).
    
4. **Legitimation:** By processing disputes through fair procedures, courts give the regime legitimacy. Even dictatorships use courts to make their actions look legal ("Rule _by_ Law" vs "Rule _of_ Law").
    

### 19.3 Supreme Court's Political Role

While judges are supposed to be "neutral," supreme courts inevitably play a major political role because their decisions affect public policy.

- **Judicial Review:** As discussed in Topic 9, this is the power to declare laws unconstitutional. When a court strikes down a law passed by a democratically elected parliament, it is performing a political act.
    
- **Judicial Activism vs. Judicial Restraint:**
    
    - **Judicial Restraint:** The belief that judges should be cautious. They should defer to the elected branches (Legislature/Executive) and only strike down laws if they are obviously unconstitutional. They stick to the "original intent" of the law.
        
    - **Judicial Activism:** The belief that judges should use their power to correct social injustices when the other branches fail to act. Activist judges interpret the constitution broadly to create new rights (e.g., the US Supreme Court legalizing gay marriage, or Indian/Bangladeshi courts issuing orders on environmental pollution).
        
- **Politicization of the Judiciary:** Because the Supreme Court has so much power, the **appointment** of judges becomes a political battle. Political parties try to appoint judges who share their ideology, threatening the perception of judicial neutrality.

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Common Law vs. Civil Law:**
    
    - **Common Law (UK/USA/Bangladesh):** Based on _Precedent_ (Stare Decisis). Judges follow the decisions of previous judges. Judges are powerful creators of law.
        
    - **Civil Law (France/Germany):** Based on comprehensive _Codes_. Judges just apply the written code; they have less power to interpret.
        
- **PIL (Public Interest Litigation):** A vital concept in South Asia. Unlike traditional law (where _you_ must be the victim to sue), PIL allows any citizen to file a case on behalf of the poor or the environment. Courts in Bangladesh and India have used this to become very activist.
    
- **Independence of Judiciary:** To be independent, judges usually have **Security of Tenure** (cannot be fired easily) and fixed salaries, so politicians cannot threaten their livelihood.
