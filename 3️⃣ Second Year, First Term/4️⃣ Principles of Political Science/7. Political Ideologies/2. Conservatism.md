### 7.2 Conservatism ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Conservatism is often misunderstood as simply "opposing change." In reality, it is a sophisticated philosophy about human imperfection and the value of tradition. Understanding it is crucial for analyzing Right-wing politics.
    
- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Questions often focus on **Edmund Burke’s** critique of the French Revolution, the concept of the **"Organic Society,"** or the tension between **Paternalistic Conservatism** and the **New Right**.
    

---

**Introduction: The Philosophy of Imperfection** Conservatism arose in the late 18th century as a reaction against the radical changes of the Enlightenment and the French Revolution. While Liberals shouted "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity," Conservatives warned that tearing down ancient institutions (like the Church or Monarchy) to build a "perfect" society would lead to chaos and tyranny. Conservatism is not an ideology with a fixed "blueprint" (like Marxism); rather, it is a "disposition" or state of mind. It prefers the known to the unknown, the tried to the untried, and the actual to the possible.

**1. Core Themes of Conservatism**

- **A. Tradition (The Wisdom of the Past):**
    
    - Conservatives value customs, rituals, and institutions that have stood the test of time.
        
    - **G.K. Chesterton** famously called tradition the **"Democracy of the Dead."** It means giving a vote to our ancestors. If a law has worked for 500 years, we shouldn't scrap it just because we think we are smarter today.
        
- **B. Human Imperfection:**
    
    - Unlike Liberals (who think humans are rational) or Socialists (who think humans are cooperative), Conservatives believe humans are **flawed**.
        
    - We are psychologically dependent (we need safety/belonging), morally corruptible (we are greedy/selfish), and intellectually limited (the world is too complex for us to understand fully).
        
    - _Conclusion:_ We need a strong state and strict laws to keep order.
        
- **C. Organic Society:** *
    
    - Liberals view society as a "Machine" (made of individual parts/atoms).
        
    - Conservatives view society as a **"Living Organism"** (like a body). The parts (family, church, government) are interconnected. If you remove one organ (e.g., destroy the traditional family), the whole body might die.
        
- **D. Hierarchy and Authority:**
    
    - Equality is a myth. People have different talents and roles. Just as parents have authority over children, the state must have authority over citizens. Hierarchy provides structure and discipline, which prevents anarchy.
        

**2. Key Thinker: Edmund Burke (1729–1797)**

The "Father of Conservatism." In his book _Reflections on the Revolution in France_ (1790), he predicted that the French Revolution would end in terror/dictatorship (he was right).

- **Change to Conserve:** Burke was not against _all_ change. He argued: **"A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation."**
    
- **The Method:** Change should be slow, gradual, and evolutionary (pruning the tree), not revolutionary (chopping down the tree).
    
- **The Social Contract:** Unlike Locke (who said the contract is between living people), Burke argued society is a partnership between **"those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born."** We are merely custodians of the nation for future generations.
    

**3. Types of Conservatism**

- **One-Nation Conservatism (Paternalism):**
    
    - Associated with **Benjamin Disraeli**.
        
    - _Belief:_ The rich have a duty (**Noblesse Oblige**) to look after the poor. The state should provide social welfare to prevent the poor from revolting. This is a "soft" conservatism that values social cohesion.
        
- **The New Right (Thatcherism/Reaganism):**
    
    - Emerged in the 1970s. It blends **Neoliberal Economics** (free markets/low tax) with **Social Conservatism** (strong police/traditional morality).
        
    - _Contradiction:_ It wants the state to be small in the economy but strong in law and order.

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Property:** Conservatives love private property. It gives people a "stake" in society. If you own a house, you are less likely to be a revolutionary because you have something to lose.
    
- **Pragmatism:** Conservatives hate abstract theories (like "Human Rights"). They prefer **Pragmatism**—doing "what works" in the real world, based on experience and history.
    
- **Exam Strategy:** If asked "Why do Conservatives oppose revolution?", explain the **Unintended Consequences**. Because society is an "organism," radical changes often have side effects that are worse than the original problem.
