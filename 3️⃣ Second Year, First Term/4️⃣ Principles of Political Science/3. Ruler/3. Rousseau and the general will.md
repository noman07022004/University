## 3.3 Rousseau and the General Will (The Democratic Ruler) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** Rousseau's "General Will" is the theoretical counter-punch to Plato's "Philosopher King." Instead of expertise, Rousseau champions _legitimacy_ through popular sovereignty. It is the bridge between ancient philosophy and modern democracy.
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** This is one of the most intellectually demanding concepts in the syllabus. Exams often ask you to differentiate between the **General Will** and the **Will of All**, or to explain why Rousseau believes the General Will is always right.

---

**The Collective Sovereign**

While Plato believed the "Ruler" should be a wise expert standing above the people, Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778) argued that the "Ruler" is the people. In his view, legitimate political authority cannot be based on force, tradition, or superior intelligence; it can only be based on the collective will of the citizens themselves. For Rousseau, the only legitimate ruler is the General Will.

---

### 1. The Concept of the General Will (_Volonté Générale_)

The General Will is the heart of Rousseau's political philosophy. It is an abstract concept representing the collective interest of the community.

- **Definition:** The General Will is the will of the sovereign body (the people) that aims at the **Common Good**. It is what every citizen _would_ want if they were thinking rationally about the welfare of the state rather than their own selfish wallet.
    
- **Characteristics:**
    
    - **Inalienable:** Power can be transferred (to a king/representative), but the _Will_ cannot. The people cannot give away their right to decide the laws.
        
    - **Indivisible:** It cannot be split into parties or factions. A divided will is not a general will.
        
    - **Infallible:** "The general will is always right and tends to the public advantage." (Note: This means the _standard_ is always right, even if the people are momentarily tricked).
        

### 2. The Critical Distinction: General Will vs. Will of All

This is the most common exam question on Rousseau. You must distinguish between these two:

|**Feature**|**General Will**|**Will of All (Volonté de Tous)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Focus**|The **Common Good** (Public Interest).|The **Sum of Private Interests**.|
|**Nature**|Moral and Qualitative.|Statistical and Quantitative.|
|**Example**|Voting for a tax to build a hospital because the community needs it.|Voting against the tax because _I_ don't want to pay it.|
|**Math**|The intersection of interests (what remains after "pluses and minuses" cancel out).|A simple headcount/majority vote of selfish desires.|

### 3. The Ruler as an Agent (Rejection of Representation)

Rousseau has a unique view on government structure.

- **The Sovereign:** The _People_ acting together (Legislative Power). They make the laws via the General Will.
    
- **The Government:** The _Magistrates/Kings_ (Executive Power).
    
- **The Relationship:** The Government is **not the master** (as Hobbes thought) nor even a Representative (as Locke thought). The Government is merely an **agent** or clerk.
    
- **Direct Democracy:** Rousseau famously argued that **"Sovereignty cannot be represented."** Therefore, the British people are "free only during the election of Members of Parliament. As soon as they are elected, slavery overtakes them." True freedom requires citizens to vote on laws directly (like in ancient Athens or modern Swiss cantons).
    

### 4. The Paradox: "Forced to be Free"

Rousseau argued that true freedom is not "doing whatever you want" (which is slavery to impulse), but "living under a law you prescribed for yourself."

- **The Scenario:** If a citizen votes against the General Will (e.g., he wants to steal or evade taxes), he is acting against the common good, and therefore against his own _true_ freedom as a citizen.
    
- **The Justification:** When the State punishes him or forces him to obey the law, it is not oppressing him. It is bringing him back into alignment with the General Will. It is **"forcing him to be free."**
    
    - _Critique:_ This idea terrified later liberals (like Isaiah Berlin), who saw it as a recipe for totalitarianism (e.g., "The Dictator knows what you _really_ want better than you do").
        

---

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Key Analogy:** Think of a sports team.
    
    - _Private Will:_ "I want to score the goal myself to be the star."
        
    - _General Will:_ "I want the _team_ to win, so I will pass the ball."
        
    - Rousseau says the State only functions when citizens think like the Team, not the Star.
        
- **Exam Strategy:** When discussing the "General Will," emphasize that it is an **ethical** concept, not just a mathematical one. It requires civic virtue—citizens must care about the whole.
    
- **Contrast:**
    
    - **Plato:** The wise Few rule the ignorant Many.
        
    - **Rousseau:** The Whole rules itself (but must be virtuous).
