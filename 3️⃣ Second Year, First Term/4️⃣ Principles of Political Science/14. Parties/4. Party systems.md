### 14.4 Party Systems ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** A "party" is an organization; a "party system" is the environment in which they compete. Understanding this distinction is crucial for comparative politics (e.g., comparing the US to the UK or China).
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Questions frequently ask students to compare "Two-Party vs. Multi-Party systems" or discuss the stability of coalition governments.
    

---

Moving from the internal classification of individual parties, we now look at the **Party System**. This term refers not just to the parties themselves, but to the **interactions** between them—how many exist, how they compete, and how they share power.

Political scientists classify party systems primarily by the **number of relevant parties** competing for power. A "relevant" party is one that has a realistic chance of forming a government or influencing policy (not just any tiny group that declares itself a party).

#### 1. One-Party System (Single-Party System)

In this system, only one political party is legally allowed to hold power.

- **Nature:** This is characteristic of authoritarian or totalitarian regimes. The party controls the state, the military, and the economy. Dissent is suppressed.
    
- **Function:** The party acts as a tool of mobilization and control rather than competition.
    
- **Examples:** The Communist Party of China (CPC), the former Soviet Union, or North Korea.
    
- **Pros/Cons:** It offers extreme stability and quick decision-making but lacks accountability, freedom, and checks on power.
    

#### 2. Dominant-Party System

Here, multiple parties are legal and elections are held, but **one party consistently wins** over a long period.

- **Nature:** The playing field may be tilted (through media control or funding), or the opposition may simply be too fragmented to win. The dominant party becomes synonymous with the state itself.
    
- **Examples:** The LDP in Japan (ruled for most of the post-WWII era), the ANC in South Africa, or the Awami League in Bangladesh (in recent contexts).
    
- **Analysis:** While technically democratic, these systems often suffer from corruption and a lack of responsiveness because the ruling party does not fear losing.
    

#### 3. Two-Party System

Two major parties dominate the political landscape. Other small parties may exist, but they have virtually no chance of winning the executive branch.

- **Nature:** Power alternates between these two giants. One forms the government, and the other forms the opposition.
    
- **Examples:** The United States (Democrats vs. Republicans) is the purest example. The UK is also largely a two-party system (Conservative vs. Labour), though third parties have some influence.
    
- **Pros:**
    
    - **Stability:** Governments are usually strong single-party majorities.
        
    - **Moderation:** Both parties fight for the "center," avoiding extremes.
        
- **Cons:**
    
    - **Limited Choice:** Voters who feel represented by neither party have nowhere to go.
        
    - **Polarization:** It can create an "us vs. them" mentality.
        

#### 4. Multi-Party System

Three or more parties have the capacity to gain control of government, separately or in coalition.

- **Nature:** No single party usually wins a majority (50% + 1) of the seats. Therefore, parties must form **Coalitions** (partnerships) to govern.
    
- **Examples:** Most of Europe (Germany, France, Italy, Sweden), India, and Israel.
    
- **Pros:**
    
    - **Representation:** It reflects the diversity of society much better. Minorities and niche interests (greens, libertarians) get seats.
        
- **Cons:**
    
    - **Instability:** Coalitions can fall apart if partners disagree, leading to frequent elections (e.g., Italy or Israel).
        
    - **Gridlock:** Decision-making can be slow due to constant negotiation.
        

#### 5. Sartori’s Classification (Advanced Academic View)

While the "number" of parties is important, political scientist **Giovanni Sartori** argued that the _ideological distance_ matters more.

- **Moderate Pluralism:** Many parties exist, but they are ideologically close (center-left and center-right). This is stable (e.g., Germany).
    
- **Polarized Pluralism:** Parties are far apart (extreme left vs. extreme right) with a weak center. This is unstable and dangerous for democracy (e.g., Weimar Germany before Hitler).

### Important Notes 📝

- **Duverger's Law:** I mentioned this in the previous section, but it is **essential** here.
    
    - _First-Past-The-Post (Winner-Take-All) systems_ $\rightarrow$ **Two-Party Systems**.
        
    - _Proportional Representation systems_ $\rightarrow$ **Multi-Party Systems**.
        
    - _Reasoning:_ In a winner-take-all system, voting for a small party is a "wasted vote," so voters flock to the big two.
        
- **Coalition Government:** A government formed by two or more parties that agree to share executive power. This is the norm in multi-party systems.
    
- **Hung Parliament:** A situation in a parliamentary system where no single party has a majority of seats.
