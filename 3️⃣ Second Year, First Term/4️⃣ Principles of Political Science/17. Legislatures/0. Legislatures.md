## 17. Legislatures ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐⭐⭐⭐):** The legislature is the "voice" of the people. Understanding how it functions—and how it relates to the Executive—is the single most important structural distinction in Comparative Politics (Presidential vs. Parliamentary systems).
>- **Exam (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):** A critical exam topic. Questions almost always ask for the **differences between Presidential and Parliamentary systems**, or the arguments for and against **Bicameralism** (Two Houses).

---

The Legislature (or Parliament/Congress) is the branch of government empowered to make laws. The word comes from the Latin _lex_ (law) and the French _parler_ (to speak). It is the supreme forum for national debate. In democratic theory, the legislature holds the most legitimacy because it is directly elected by the people to represent their diverse interests. However, in the modern era, many scholars argue that legislatures are in decline, losing power to the increasingly dominant Executive branch.

---

### 17.1 Origins of Parliaments

Modern legislatures did not appear overnight; they evolved over centuries.

- **Medieval Roots:** They began as royal councils in medieval Europe. Kings needed money for wars, but feudal lords would only pay taxes if they were consulted. The principle "What touches all should be approved by all" led to the first parliaments.
    
- **The "Power of the Purse":** The legislature's power originated from its control over the budget. By refusing to grant the King money until he addressed their grievances, parliaments gradually wrestled power away from monarchs.
    
- **The Mother of Parliaments:** The British Parliament is often cited as the model. It evolved from a purely advisory body to the supreme sovereign power (Parliamentary Supremacy) after the Glorious Revolution of 1688.
    

### 17.2 Presidential and Parliamentary Systems

This is the most fundamental classification of democratic regimes. It describes the relationship between the Legislature (Law-makers) and the Executive (Law-enforcers).

**A. Parliamentary System (Fusion of Powers)**

- **Selection:** The Head of Government (Prime Minister) is selected _by_ the Legislature, not the voters directly. The PM is usually the leader of the majority party in Parliament.
    
- **Fusion:** There is no strict separation. The Executive sits _inside_ the Legislature. The PM and Ministers must be Members of Parliament (MPs).
    
- **Accountability:** The Executive is directly responsible to the Legislature. Parliament can remove the PM at any time via a **Vote of No Confidence**.
    
- **Dissolution:** The PM can typically dissolve the Parliament and call early elections.
    
- **Examples:** UK, Bangladesh, India, Canada.
    

**B. Presidential System (Separation of Powers)**

- **Selection:** The Head of Government (President) is elected _independently_ of the Legislature (by the people).
    
- **Separation:** The Executive and Legislature are separate institutions. The President cannot be a member of Congress.
    
- **Accountability:** The Legislature cannot easily remove the President (except through difficult Impeachment for crimes). The President serves a fixed term regardless of popularity.
    
- **No Dissolution:** The President cannot dissolve the Legislature.
    
- **Examples:** USA, Brazil, Indonesia.
    

### 17.3 Bicameral and Unicameral Parliament

Should the legislature have one house (chamber) or two?

1. **Bicameralism (Two Houses):**
    
    - **Structure:** Consists of a **Lower House** (representing the people directly, e.g., House of Commons, US House) and an **Upper House** (representing regions or elites, e.g., US Senate, House of Lords).
        
    - **Rationale:**
        
        - **Checks and Balances:** The Upper House acts as a "cooling saucer," reviewing and delaying hasty legislation passed by the Lower House.
            
        - **Federalism:** In federal states (like the USA or India), the Upper House represents the _States_, protecting them from the national majority.
            
2. **Unicameralism (One House):**
    
    - **Structure:** A single chamber does all the work.
        
    - **Rationale:**
        
        - **Efficiency:** Laws are passed faster without a second house to block them.
            
        - **Democracy:** If the people are one, why should their will be split? A second house is seen as undemocratic or redundant.
            
    - **Example:** Bangladesh, New Zealand, Sweden.
        

### 17.4 Functions of the Legislatures

What do they actually do?

1. **Law-Making:** The primary technical function. They debate, amend, and vote on bills to turn them into statutes.
    
2. **Representation:** They act as the "voice" of the citizens. MPs represent the grievances of their local constituents to the center.
    
3. **Scrutiny and Oversight (The Watchdog Function):** This is crucial. The legislature monitors the Executive to prevent abuse of power. This is done through "Question Hour" (asking Ministers tough questions) and committee hearings.
    
4. **Legitimacy:** By debating policies in public, the legislature gives the government the "stamp of approval" (legitimacy) it needs to govern peacefully.

### Important Notes, Tips, and Facts

- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh has a **Unicameral** legislature called the _Jatiya Sangsad_ (National Parliament). It has 350 seats (300 direct election + 50 reserved for women).
    
- **Gridlock:** A major problem in Presidential systems is "Gridlock"—when the President and Congress are from different parties, they block each other, and nothing gets done. Parliamentary systems avoid this because the PM _always_ has a majority.
    
- **Exam Terminology - "Rubber Stamp":** This refers to a legislature that has no real power and simply approves whatever the dictator/leader wants (common in authoritarian regimes like North Korea).
    
- **Impeachment vs. No Confidence:**
    
    - _Vote of No Confidence:_ "We don't like your policies, so you are fired." (Parliamentary - Easy/Political).
        
    - _Impeachment:_ "You committed a crime." (Presidential - Hard/Legal).
