### 17.2 Presidential and Parliamentary Systems ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the fundamental structural choice for any democracy. It dictates how the government is formed, how stable it is, and how accountable it remains to the people.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** A classic comparative politics question. You will almost certainly face a question asking to "Compare and Contrast" these two systems or to discuss the "Fusion of Powers" vs. "Separation of Powers."
    

---

The most significant distinction in modern democratic constitutions is between **Parliamentary** and **Presidential** systems. The core difference lies in the relationship between the **Legislative branch** (law-making) and the **Executive branch** (law-enforcing).

#### 1. Parliamentary System (The Westminster Model)

- **Core Principle:** **Fusion of Powers**. The executive and legislative branches are fused together.
    
- **Executive Selection:** The Head of Government (Prime Minister) is selected **by the legislature**, not directly by the people. They are usually the leader of the majority party in parliament.
    
- **Accountability:** The Prime Minister (PM) and Cabinet are directly accountable to Parliament. They must answer questions (Question Time) and defend their policies.
    
- **Removal:** The legislature can remove the executive at any time through a **Vote of No Confidence**. If the government loses this vote, they must resign or call an election.
    
- **Dual Executive:** There is a separation between the Head of State (Ceremonial role, e.g., Queen or President) and the Head of Government (Political role, e.g., Prime Minister).
    
- **Examples:** UK, India, Canada, Japan, Bangladesh.
    

#### 2. Presidential System

- **Core Principle:** **Separation of Powers**. The executive and legislative branches are completely separate and independent bodies.
    
- **Executive Selection:** The Head of Government (President) is elected **by the people** (directly or via an electoral college). Their mandate comes from the voters, not the legislature.
    
- **Accountability:** The President is not responsible to the legislature in day-to-day matters. Cabinet members are appointed by the President and do not sit in Congress.
    
- **Removal:** It is extremely difficult to remove the President before their fixed term ends. It requires **Impeachment**, which is a legal trial for crimes, not just a political disagreement.
    
- **Single Executive:** The President is both the Head of State (ceremonial) and the Head of Government (political).
    
- **Examples:** USA, Brazil, Mexico, Philippines.
    

#### 3. Semi-Presidential System (Hybrid)

- **Nature:** A mix of both. There is both a directly elected President (with real power) and a Prime Minister (responsible to parliament).
    
- **Cohabitation:** Problems arise when the President and PM are from opposing parties.
    
- **Examples:** France, Russia.
    

#### 4. Comparison Table (Critical for Exams)

|**Feature**|**Parliamentary System**|**Presidential System**|
|---|---|---|
|**Relationship**|**Fusion** of powers (Legislature + Executive)|**Separation** of powers|
|**Head of Govt**|Prime Minister (part of Parliament)|President (separate from Congress)|
|**Term Limit**|Flexible (can be removed by No Confidence vote)|Fixed Term (e.g., 4 years)|
|**Cabinet**|Must be Members of Parliament (MPs)|Cannot be members of the legislature|
|**Focus**|Party discipline & Collective Responsibility|Individual leadership & Checks/Balances|
|**Stability**|Lower (govts can fall)|Higher (fixed terms)|
|**Gridlock**|Rare (PM controls the majority)|Common (President vs. Congress)|

---

### Important Notes 📝

- **Gridlock:** In Presidential systems, if the President is from one party and the Congress is controlled by another, they can block each other, leading to "gridlock" (nothing gets done). This is rare in Parliamentary systems because the PM _is_ the leader of the legislative majority.
    
- **"Presidentialization" of Politics:** Even in parliamentary systems (like the UK or India), there is a trend where PMs act more like Presidents—focusing on their personal brand and centralizing power, rather than acting as "first among equals" in the Cabinet.
    
- **Zero-Sum Game:** Juan Linz argued that Presidentialism is dangerous for new democracies because it is a "winner-take-all" system (zero-sum), whereas Parliamentarism encourages coalition and power-sharing.
