### 17.3 Bicameral and Unicameral Parliament ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** The structure of the legislature (one house or two) profoundly affects the speed of law-making and the quality of representation. It is essential for understanding federal systems.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Questions often ask for the "Arguments for and against Bicameralism" or "Why is a second chamber necessary in a federation?"
    

---

The structure of a legislature is defined by the number of chambers (or "houses") it contains. This is not just an architectural detail; it reflects the political philosophy of the state.

#### 1. Bicameralism (Two Chambers)

A bicameral legislature consists of two separate assemblies that must both agree for a law to pass.

- **The Lower House:** Usually represents the **people** directly (based on population). It is typically more powerful in parliamentary systems. (e.g., House of Commons in UK, Lok Sabha in India, House of Representatives in US).
    
- **The Upper House:** Represents a different principle, such as **regions/states** (in federations) or **tradition/wisdom**. (e.g., House of Lords in UK, Rajya Sabha in India, Senate in US).
    

**Why choose Bicameralism?**

1. **Federalism:** This is the strongest argument. In a federation (like the USA or India), the Lower House represents the _population_ (giving power to big states), while the Upper House represents the _states_ equally (protecting small states). It balances the "majority of people" with the "majority of regions."
    
2. **Checks and Balances:** A second chamber acts as a "revising chamber." It creates a pause button, preventing the Lower House from passing hasty, emotional, or populist legislation. As George Washington famously said, the Senate is the "saucer" that cools the hot tea of the House.
    
3. **Expertise:** Upper houses often contain elder statesmen, experts, or appointed officials who can debate technical details without the pressure of constant re-election campaigning.
    

#### 2. Unicameralism (One Chamber)

A unicameral legislature consists of a single assembly.

- **Where:** Common in unitary states, small nations, or countries that value decisive action over debate. (e.g., New Zealand, China, Sweden, Bangladesh).
    

**Why choose Unicameralism?**

1. **Efficiency:** Laws are passed quickly. There is no "gridlock" where one house blocks the other.
    
2. **Accountability:** It is clear who is responsible. Voters know exactly who to blame if a law is bad. In bicameral systems, houses can blame each other.
    
3. **Cost:** It is significantly cheaper to maintain one set of legislators and staff than two.
    
4. **Democratic Theory:** Radical democrats (like French revolutionaries) argue that the "Will of the People" is one and indivisible. Therefore, having two houses implies the people have two wills, which is absurd.
    

#### 3. Comparison Summary

|**Feature**|**Bicameralism**|**Unicameralism**|
|---|---|---|
|**Structure**|Two Houses (Upper & Lower)|One House|
|**Primary Goal**|Checks & Balances, Federalism|Efficiency, Unity|
|**Speed**|Slower (Deliberate)|Faster (Decisive)|
|**Cost**|Expensive|Economical|
|**Typical User**|Large Federal States (USA, India)|Small Unitary States (New Zealand, Sweden)|

---

### Important Notes 📝

- **Federalism Rule:** A key axiom in political science is: _Federalism requires Bicameralism._ You cannot have a true federation without a chamber to represent the states.
    
- **The "Weak" Upper House:** In most parliamentary systems (like the UK or India), the Upper House is intentionally weaker. It can _delay_ a Money Bill but usually cannot _veto_ it. The Lower House (representing the people) has the final say on the budget.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh has a **Unicameral** parliament (Jatiya Sangsad). This was chosen to ensure unity and avoid the delays associated with upper houses, reflecting the unitary nature of the state.
