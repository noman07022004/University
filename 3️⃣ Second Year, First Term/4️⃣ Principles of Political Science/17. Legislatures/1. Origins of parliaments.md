### 17.1 Origins of Parliaments ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Knowing where parliaments came from helps explain their two core historical powers: controlling money (taxation) and checking the executive (the King).
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While essential for context, exams rarely ask for a detailed history of the Middle Ages. Questions are more likely to focus on _current_ functions. However, knowing the history of the "Power of the Purse" is useful for essays on legislative control.
    

The term "Parliament" comes from the French word _parler_ (to speak). Originally, it was simply a place where the King and his barons "spoke" or negotiated. The evolution of this body from a servant of the King to the master of the state is one of the most important stories in political science.

#### 1. Feudal Roots: The King's Council

In the Middle Ages (approx. 11th–13th century), Kings were powerful but not absolute. To fight wars or run the state, they needed money and soldiers. They could not simply take these from powerful lords without risking rebellion.

- **The _Curia Regis_:** The King would summon his "Great Council" (bishops, barons, and earls) to ask for advice and, more importantly, for consent to collect taxes.
    
- **Quid Pro Quo:** This established a fundamental transaction: **Money for Grievances**. The King got tax revenue, and in return, the lords got the right to present complaints (grievances) which the King had to address.
    

#### 2. The Magna Carta (1215)

This is the foundational document of parliamentary sovereignty.

- **The Conflict:** King John of England abused his power. The barons rebelled and forced him to sign the "Great Charter" (Magna Carta).
    
- **The Principle:** It established that the King is not above the law and cannot levy taxes without the "common counsel of the realm." This is the seed of **"No Taxation Without Representation."**
    

#### 3. The Model Parliament (1295)

King Edward I of England needed broad support for war. He summoned not just the high nobles (who would become the House of Lords) but also "commoners"—two knights from every county and two burgesses from every town.

- **Significance:** This created the structure of the **Bicameral** (two-house) system:
    
    - **House of Lords:** Representing the aristocracy.
        
    - **House of Commons:** Representing the communities (towns and counties).
        

#### 4. The Rise of Sovereignty (The Glorious Revolution 1688)

For centuries, Parliament and King fought for supremacy. This climaxed in the English Civil War and finally the **Glorious Revolution of 1688**.

- **Bill of Rights (1689):** This document legally established that Parliament was supreme. The King could not suspend laws or keep a standing army without Parliament's permission.
    
- **Shift:** Parliament transformed from a temporary body summoned by the King into a permanent institution that _controlled_ the King (or at least his ministers).
    

#### 5. Expansion of Representation

Originally, "the people" meant only rich male landowners.

- **19th Century:** The Industrial Revolution created a new class of workers who demanded a voice. Through a series of Reform Acts (in the UK) and revolutions (in Europe), the franchise was expanded to the working class.
    
- **Modernity:** By the 20th century, parliaments became truly democratic bodies representing the entire adult population.

### Important Notes 📝

- **"Mother of Parliaments":** This phrase refers to the **UK Parliament** (Westminster). Because the British Empire spread across the globe, many countries (Canada, India, Australia, Bangladesh) modeled their legislatures on the British system.
    
- **Power of the Purse:** Always remember this phrase. It is the oldest and most powerful weapon of any legislature. If the executive (President/Prime Minister) ignores the law, the legislature can simply cut off their funding.
    
- **Legislature vs. Parliament vs. Congress:**
    
    - **Legislature:** The general academic term.
        
    - **Parliament:** A system where the executive is _inside_ the legislature (UK, India).
        
    - **Congress:** A system where the executive is _separate_ from the legislature (USA).
