### 1. The Axiomatic Approach (Kolmogorov’s Axioms)
In 1933, Russian mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov established the rigorous foundation of probability theory. Before this, probability was largely intuitive. Kolmogorov reduced probability to three simple axioms (rules) from which every other theorem is derived.

To define a valid probability model, the following three axioms must hold true for a Sample Space ($S$) and any Event ($E$):

#### Axiom 1: Non-Negativity

The probability of any event is a non-negative real number.

$$P(E) \ge 0$$

- _Implication:_ You can never have a negative probability. "Minus 5% chance" is mathematically undefined.
    

#### Axiom 2: Unitarity (Normalization)

The probability of the entire sample space (the "Certain Event") is 1.

$$P(S) = 1$$

- _Implication:_ Something must happen. The sum of probabilities of all possible outcomes must equal exactly 1 (or 100%).
    

#### Axiom 3: Countable Additivity

If two events $A$ and $B$ are Mutually Exclusive (they cannot happen together, $A \cap B = \emptyset$), then the probability of their union is the sum of their individual probabilities.

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

- Extension: This applies to any countable sequence of disjoint events ($E_1, E_2, ...$).
    
    $$P(\cup E_i) = \sum P(E_i)$$
    

---

### 2. Theorems Derived from the Foundations

Using only the three axioms above, we can mathematically **prove** all other rules used in this course.

- **The Complement Rule:**
    
    - _Logic:_ Since $A$ and $A^c$ are mutually exclusive and together make up the sample space $S$...
        
    - _Proof:_ $P(A \cup A^c) = P(S)$. By Axiom 3, $P(A) + P(A^c) = 1$.
        
    - _Result:_ $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$.
        
- **The Impossible Event:**
    
    - _Result:_ $P(\emptyset) = 0$.
        
    - _Logic:_ The empty set is the complement of the sample space.
        
- **The General Addition Rule:**
    
    - For events that are _not_ mutually exclusive, Axiom 3 fails because we double-count the overlap.
        
    - _Correction:_ $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.
        
- **Monotonicity:**
    
    - If event $A$ is a subset of event $B$ (i.e., every time A happens, B also happens), then:
        
        $$P(A) \le P(B)$$
        

---

### 3. Probability as Set Theory

The foundation of probability is essentially **Set Theory** applied to random outcomes.

- **Sample Space ($S$)** = The Universal Set.
    
- **Event ($E$)** = A Subset.
    
- **AND** = Intersection ($\cap$).
    
- **OR** = Union ($\cup$).
    
- **NOT** = Complement ($A'$ or $A^c$).
    

---

### 4. Objective vs. Subjective Foundations

Academic statistics often distinguishes between two philosophical foundations for _interpreting_ these axioms:

- **Frequentist (Objective):** Probability is the limit of relative frequency over the long run.
    
    - _Definition:_ $P(E) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n(E)}{n}$.
        
    - _Constraint:_ You cannot assign a probability to a single, non-repeatable event (e.g., "Probability that candidate X wins the 2024 election").
        
- **Bayesian (Subjective):** Probability is a measure of "Degree of Belief."
    
    - _Definition:_ Based on prior knowledge and updated with evidence.
        
    - _Freedom:_ You _can_ assign probabilities to non-repeatable events.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

- **The "Sum > 1" Error:** If you are given a list of probabilities for mutually exclusive outcomes (e.g., P(Win)=0.4, P(Lose)=0.5, P(Draw)=0.2), sum them up. $0.4+0.5+0.2 = 1.1$. This violates **Axiom 2**. The model is invalid.
    
- **Venn Diagrams:** These are your best friend in this topic. If you can draw the circles, you can visually verify the axioms.
    
- **Independence is NOT an Axiom:** Independence is a definition derived _from_ the multiplication rule, not a foundational axiom itself.
