### 1. Core Definitions

To speak the language of probability, we must define the basic elements of random processes.

#### The Process: Random Experiment

A **Random Experiment** is any process that leads to one of several possible observations, where the outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance.

- **Key characteristic:** If you repeat the experiment under the exact same conditions, you might get different results.
    
- **Contrast:** It differs from a _Deterministic Experiment_ (like heating water to 100°C), where the result is always the same.
    
- **Examples:** Tossing a coin, rolling a die, measuring the daily rainfall.
    

#### The Action: Trial

A **Trial** is a single performance of a random experiment.

- **Example:** If you flip a coin 5 times, you have performed 5 trials of the experiment.
    

#### The Result: Outcome (or Sample Point)

An **Outcome** is the specific result of a single trial. It is the most basic building block.

- **Notation:** Often denoted by lowercase letters (e.g., $e_1, e_2$) or specific values ($H, T$).
    
- **Example:** In rolling a die, rolling a "4" is an outcome.
    

#### The Collection: Sample Space ($S$ or $\Omega$)

The **Sample Space** is the set of _all possible_ outcomes of a random experiment. No outcome is left out.

- **Definition:** $S = \{ \text{all possible outcomes} \}$
    
- **Example (2 Coins):** $S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$
    

#### The Interest: Event ($E$ or $A$)

An **Event** is any subset of the sample space. It is what we are typically calculating the probability _of_.

- **Simple Event:** An event containing exactly one outcome.
    
    - _Example:_ Getting a sum of 2 when rolling two dice (only $\{1,1\}$ works).
        
- **Compound Event:** An event containing more than one outcome.
    
    - _Example:_ Rolling an "Even Number" on a die ($E = \{2, 4, 6\}$).
        
- **Impossible Event:** An event that contains no outcomes (Empty set $\emptyset$).
    
    - _Probability:_ $P(\emptyset) = 0$.
        
    - _Example:_ Rolling a "7" on a standard 6-sided die.
        
- **Certain Event:** An event that contains _every_ outcome in the sample space ($S$).
    
    - _Probability:_ $P(S) = 1$.
        
    - _Example:_ Rolling a number less than 10 on a standard die.
        
#### Summary Hierarchy (The "Die Roll" Example)

To visualize how these fit together, imagine the experiment of **rolling one standard die**:

| **Term**             | **Definition in this Context**            | **Set Notation**               |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| **Experiment**       | Rolling the die once.                     | N/A                            |
| **Outcome**          | The number that faces up.                 | $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \text{ or } 6$ |
| **Sample Space**     | The list of everything that could happen. | $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$     |
| **Event (Simple)**   | Rolling a "5".                            | $A = \{5\}$                    |
| **Event (Compound)** | Rolling a number greater than 4.          | $B = \{5, 6\}$                 |

### 2. Three Approaches to Probability
How do we actually assign a number to the "chance" of something happening? There are three standard academic frameworks:
#### 1. Classical (Theoretical) Approach

This is the oldest form of probability, often associated with games of chance (gambling). It relies on logic rather than experiments.

- **The Core Idea:** If an experiment has a finite number of outcomes and **every outcome is equally likely**, you can calculate probability by counting.
    
- **The Assumption:** The system is "fair" (e.g., a balanced coin, a perfect die).
    
- The Formula:
    
    $$P(A) = \frac{\text{Number of Favorable Outcomes}}{\text{Total Number of Possible Outcomes}}$$
    

> **Example:** Rolling a fair 6-sided die.7
> 
> - Total outcomes (8$S$): 9$\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$
>     
> - Event ($A$): Rolling a number greater than 4 ($\{5, 6\}$).
>     
> - Calculation: $P(A) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$.
>     

- **Limitation:** It fails in real-world situations where outcomes are **not** equally likely (e.g., predicting if it will rain tomorrow is not 10$50/50$ just because there are two outcomes: rain or no rain).11
    

#### 2. Relative Frequency (Empirical) Approach12

This approach is based on **observation** and **data**. It is used when you cannot assume outcomes are equally likely, so you must experiment to find the answer.

- **The Core Idea:** Probability is the "long-run" average frequency of an event occurring over many repeated trials.
    
- **The Law of Large Numbers:** As you increase the number of trials (16$n$), the observed frequency gets closer to the "true" probability.
    
- The Formula:
    
    $$P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\text{Number of times event A occurred}}{\text{Total number of trials } (n)}$$
    

> Example: Quality Control.
> 
> You cannot calculate the "theoretical" probability that a specific machine produces a defective part. Instead, you inspect 1,000 parts.
> 
> - Defects found: 15
>     
> - Total parts: 1,000
>     
> - Probability: $P(\text{Defect}) = \frac{15}{1000} = 0.015$ (or $1.5\%$).
>     

- **Limitation:** It requires a large amount of data. If you only flip a coin twice and get Heads both times, the empirical probability of Heads is $100\%$, which we know is wrong.
    

#### 3. Subjective Approach

This approach is based on **personal belief**, intuition, or expert opinion. It is used when an event is unique and cannot be repeated (so you can't use Frequency) and outcomes aren't equally likely (so you can't use Classical).

- **The Core Idea:** Probability is a measure of the "degree of belief" an individual holds about an event.
    
- **Context:** It is widely used in business decisions, economics, and sports betting where historical data might not apply directly to a new unique situation.
    

> Example: A Business Launch.
> 
> A CEO asks, "What is the probability our new product will succeed?"
> 
> You cannot repeat the launch 1,000 times (Frequency), and it's not a dice roll (Classical). The CEO estimates a $70\%$ chance based on market analysis and experience.

- **Limitation:** It is not objective. Two different experts might give two different probabilities for the same event.

#### Summary Comparison

| **Approach**   | **Basis**           | **Formula**                              | **Best Used For**                                    |
| -------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Classical**  | Logic & Symmetry    | $\frac{\text{Favorable}}{\text{Total}}$  | Coins, dice, cards, lotteries.                       |
| **Empirical**  | Data & Experiments  | $\frac{\text{Successes}}{\text{Trials}}$ | Insurance, manufacturing, baseball batting averages. |
| **Subjective** | Belief & Experience | None (Estimation)                        | Elections, stock market, unique business decisions.  |

#### Note on the "Axiomatic Approach"

You will see "Foundation of probability" in **Section 3.6** of your syllabus. This refers to the **Axiomatic Approach** (developed by Kolmogorov).

While the three approaches above tell us _how to calculate_ probability, the Axiomatic approach gives us the _mathematical rules_ that all three must follow (like 21$0 \le P(A) \le 1$).22 It unifies the three methods into one consistent mathematical system.

### 3. Relationships Between Events

Understanding how events interact is the key to solving probability problems.
#### i. The "Overlap" Relationship (Mutually Exclusive)

This relationship asks: **"Can these two events happen at the exact same time?"**

##### A. Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint) Events

Two events are mutually exclusive if they share **no common outcomes**. The occurrence of one makes the other impossible.

- **Visual:** Imagine two circles in a Venn diagram that are completely separate; they do not touch.
    
- Mathematical Rule:
    
    $$P(A \text{ and } B) = 0$$
    
    $$P(A \cap B) = 0$$
    
- **Example:** A single coin toss.
    
    - Event A: Heads
        
    - Event B: Tails
        
    - You cannot get both; if Heads happens, Tails is impossible.
        

##### B. Non-Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events can happen simultaneously. They share an overlap.

- **Visual:** Two circles that intersect (overlap).
    
- Mathematical Rule:
    
    $$P(A \cap B) \neq 0$$
    
- **Example:** Drawing a card from a deck.
    
    - Event A: Drawing a King
        
    - Event B: Drawing a Red card
        
    - **Overlap:** The "King of Hearts" and "King of Diamonds" are both King _and_ Red.

#### ii. The "Influence" Relationship (Independence)

This relationship asks: **"Does knowing the result of event A change the probability of event B?"**

##### A. Independent Events

Events are independent if the occurrence of one **does not affect** the probability of the other. The "memory" of the first event does not carry over to the second.

- Mathematical Test:
    
    $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$
    
    Alternatively: $P(A|B) = P(A)$ (The probability of A remains the same, given B happened).
    
- **Example:** Rolling a die and flipping a coin.
    
    - Getting a "6" on the die does not change the probability of getting "Heads" on the coin. They are physically unconnected systems.
        

##### B. Dependent Events

Events are dependent if the occurrence of one **changes** the probability of the other. This usually happens when you sample **"without replacement."**

- Mathematical Rule:
    
    $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B|A)$$
    
- **Example:** Drawing two Aces from a deck _without_ putting the first one back.
    
    - Prob of 1st Ace: $4/52$
        
    - Prob of 2nd Ace: Now only $3/51$ (because one Ace is gone). The second probability _depended_ on the first outcome.

#### iii. The "Coverage" Relationship (Exhaustive)

This relationship asks: **"Do these events cover every single possibility?"**

##### A. Exhaustive Events

A set of events is exhaustive if at least one of them **must** occur. If you combine them all, you get the entire Sample Space ($S$).

- Mathematical Rule:
    
    $$\sum P(E_i) = 1$$
    
    $$E_1 \cup E_2 \cup ... \cup E_n = S$$
    
- **Example:** Rolling a die.
    
    - Events: $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ are exhaustive. You cannot roll a "7".
        

##### B. Complementary Events

This is a special pair of events that are both **Mutually Exclusive** AND **Exhaustive**. It is "Event A" vs "Everything else that is not A" ($A'$ or $A^c$).

- Mathematical Rule:
    
    $$P(A) + P(A') = 1$$
    
    $$P(A') = 1 - P(A)$$


#### Deep Insight: The Common Trap

Students often confuse **Mutually Exclusive** with **Independent**. They are effectively opposites.

- **Scenario:** Suppose $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$.
    
- If A and B are **Mutually Exclusive**, they represent the strongest form of **Dependence**.
    
    - _Why?_ If I tell you Event A happened, the probability of Event B instantly drops to $0$ The knowledge of A _drastically changed_ the chances of B.
        
- Therefore: **Two events cannot be both Mutually Exclusive and Independent** (unless one of them has a probability of zero).

### 4. Basic Laws of Probability

These rules govern the calculation of combined probabilities.

#### 1. The Addition Law (The "OR" Rule)

This law is used when you want to find the probability that **Event A** OR **Event B** (or both) will occur.

Formula:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

- **Logic:** You add the probability of A and the probability of B, but because you counted the overlap ($A \cap B$) twice, you must subtract it once to correct the double-counting.
    
- Special Case (Mutually Exclusive): If A and B cannot happen together, the overlap is zero ($P(A \cap B) = 0$). The formula simplifies to:
    
    $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$
    

> **Example:** From a deck of 52 cards, what is the probability of drawing a **King** OR a **Heart**?
> 
> - $P(\text{King}) = 4/52$
>     
> - $P(\text{Heart}) = 13/52$
>     
> - $P(\text{King AND Heart}) = 1/52$ (The King of Hearts)
>     
> - **Result:** $4/52 + 13/52 - 1/52 = 16/52 = \mathbf{4/13}$
>     


#### 2. The Multiplication Law (The "AND" Rule)

This law is used when you want to find the probability that **Event A** AND **Event B** will both occur.

Formula:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B|A)$$

- **Logic:** To have both happen, A must happen first ($P(A)$). Then, _given that A has already happened_, B must happen ($P(B|A)$).
    
- Special Case (Independent Events): If A occurring does not change the probability of B, then $P(B|A) = P(B)$. The formula simplifies to:
    
    $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$
    

> **Example:** You have a bag with 3 Red balls and 2 Blue balls. You draw two balls **without replacement** (Dependent). What is the probability both are Red?
> 
> - $P(\text{1st Red}) = 3/5$
>     
> - $P(\text{2nd Red } | \text{ 1st Red}) = 2/4$ (One red is gone, total is now 4)
>     
> - **Result:** $(3/5) \times (2/4) = 6/20 = \mathbf{3/10}$
>     


#### 3. The Complement Law (The "NOT" Rule)

This law calculates the probability that an event **does not** happen. It is often the fastest way to solve "at least one" problems.

Formula:

$$P(A') = 1 - P(A)$$

(Where $A'$ or $A^c$ is "Not A")

- **Logic:** The sum of probabilities of all possible outcomes is always 1 ($100\%$). So, "Not A" is simply Total minus "A".
    

> **Example:** If the probability of rain today is $0.2$ (20%), what is the probability it will **not** rain?
> 
> - $P(\text{No Rain}) = 1 - 0.2 = \mathbf{0.8}$ (80%)
>

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **The "Mutually Exclusive vs. Independent" Trap:** This is the #1 concept students confuse.
    
    - **Mutually Exclusive:** They **cannot** happen together. (Dependent: If A happens, B is impossible).
        
    - **Independent:** They **can** happen together, and they don't care about each other.
        
    - _Fact:_ Two events cannot be both mutually exclusive and independent (unless the probability of one is zero).
        
2. **Probability Bounds:** Probabilities are strictly between 0 and 1. If you calculate $P = 1.2$ or $P = -0.5$, stop. You made a math error.
    
3. **Union vs. Intersection:**
    
    - $\cup$ (Cup) = **Union** = "OR" (Add).
        
    - $\cap$ (Cap) = **Intersection** = "AND" (Multiply).
