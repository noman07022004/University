## 1.3 Measures of Dispersion ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Averages (Mean/Median) tell you where the data _is_, but Dispersion tells you _what the data looks like_. Without dispersion, you cannot assess risk, reliability, or consistency. It is the basis for the Standard Deviation, which is used in almost every statistical formula (Z-scores, T-tests, ANOVA).
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** You will absolutely be asked to calculate the Standard Deviation or Variance. Furthermore, conceptual questions about "spread" and "variability" are frequent.

---

### 1. Why Measure Dispersion?

**Dispersion** (also called Variability, Scatter, or Spread) measures the extent to which data points diverge from the central value (usually the mean) and from each other.

- **Example:** Consider two students.
    
    - Student A Scores: 48, 50, 52 (Mean = 50).
        
    - Student B Scores: 0, 50, 100 (Mean = 50).
        
    - Both have the same average, but Student B's performance is wildly inconsistent. Measures of dispersion quantify this "inconsistency."
        

---

### 2. The Range

The simplest measure of spread.

- Definition: The difference between the largest and smallest values in the dataset.
    
    $$Range = X_{max} - X_{min}$$
    
- **Limitations:** It relies on only two data points. It ignores the distribution of the data in the middle and is extremely sensitive to outliers.
    

---

### 3. Variance (বৈচিত্র্য) ($\sigma^2$ or $s^2$)

This is the average of the _squared_ deviations from the mean. It quantifies "spread" mathematically but results in "squared units" (e.g., "dollars squared"), which are hard to interpret.

- Population Variance ($\sigma^2$):
    
    $$\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \mu)^2}{N}$$
    
- Sample Variance ($s^2$):
    
    $$s^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n - 1}$$
    
    - **Why $n-1$? (Bessel's Correction):** When using a sample to estimate the population, the raw calculation tends to underestimate the true spread (bias). Dividing by $n-1$ corrects this bias, making $s^2$ an **unbiased estimator**.
        

---

### 4. Standard Deviation ($\sigma$ or $s$)

The most important measure of dispersion in all of statistics.

- Definition: The square root of the variance. It returns the measure to the original units of the data (e.g., dollars), making it intuitive.
    
    $$s = \sqrt{s^2}$$
    
- **Interpretation:** It represents the "typical" distance of a data point from the mean. A low SD means data is clustered tightly; a high SD means data is spread out.

### 5. Knowledge Gap Fill: The Coefficient of Variation (CV)

What if you want to compare the spread of two different variables?

- _Scenario:_ Is a stock priced at $100 with a variance of $10 "riskier" than a stock priced at $1 with a variance of $0.50? You cannot compare the standard deviations directly because the scales are different.
    
- The Solution: The Coefficient of Variation. It measures relative dispersion.
    
    $$CV = \frac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 100\%$$
    
    - It expresses standard deviation as a percentage of the mean.
        
    - **Usage:** Used in finance to measure risk per unit of return.
        

---

### 6. Measures of Position (Quartiles & IQR)

While Variance uses the Mean, Quartiles use the **Median** to describe spread.

- **Quartiles (চতুর্থাংশ):** Divide the sorted data into four equal parts.
    
    - $Q_1$ (25th Percentile): 25% of data is below this.
        
    - $Q_2$ (Median): 50% of data is below this.
        
    - $Q_3$ (75th Percentile): 75% of data is below this.
        
- **Interquartile Range (আন্তঃচতুর্থাংশ পরিসর) (IQR):**
    
    $$IQR = Q_3 - Q_1$$
    
    - **Significance:** It measures the spread of the **middle 50%** of the data. Unlike the Range or Standard Deviation, the IQR is **resistant to outliers**. It is the standard measure of spread for skewed distributions.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

- **Chebyshev’s Theorem:** Applies to _any_ distribution (even non-normal). It states that at least $(1 - 1/k^2)$ of data lies within $k$ standard deviations of the mean.
    
    - _Example:_ At least 75% of data is always within 2 SDs of the mean.
        
- **The Empirical Rule (Normal Rule):** Only for bell-shaped curves.
    
    - 68% of data within $\pm 1\sigma$.
        
    - 95% of data within $\pm 2\sigma$.
        
    - 99.7% of data within $\pm 3\sigma$.
        
- **Calculator Tip:** In exams, use the "STAT" mode on your calculator. You usually input the list of numbers, and it gives you $\bar{x}$, $\sigma x$ (population SD), and $sx$ (sample SD) automatically. Do not waste time calculating sums of squares by hand unless asked to show steps.
