## 1.2 Measures of Central Location

>**Context:** Once data is collected and classified (as discussed in 1.1), the immediate next step is finding a single representative value that describes the "center" or "typical" behavior of the distribution. These are often called **Measures of Central Tendency**.

---

### 1. The Arithmetic Mean ($\bar{x}$ or $\mu$)**

The mean is the most widely used measure of location in statistics. It serves as the "balance point" or center of gravity for the data.

- **Definition:** The sum of all observations divided by the number of observations.
    
- **Formula:**
    
    - Sample Mean ($\bar{x}$): Used when analyzing a subset of data.
        
        $$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}$$
        
    - Population Mean ($\mu$): Used when analyzing the entire group.
        
        $$\mu = \frac{\sum x_i}{N}$$
        
- **Properties:**
    
    1. **Uniqueness:** A dataset has one and only one mean.
        
    2. **Sensitivity:** The mean uses _every_ data point in the calculation. Consequently, it is **highly sensitive to outliers** (extreme values).
        
        - _Example:_ In a salary dataset of {30k, 40k, 50k, 5M}, the mean is massively skewed by the 5M, making it a poor representation of the "typical" salary.
            
    3. **Algebraic Utility:** The sum of deviations from the mean is always zero ($\sum(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$).
        

---

### 2. The Median

The median is the positional center of the dataset.

- **Definition:** The value that divides an ordered dataset into two equal halves. 50% of the data lies below the median, and 50% lies above it.
    
- **Calculation:**
    
    1. **Order** the data from smallest to largest ($x_1, x_2, ..., x_n$).
        
    2. Locate the position using the formula $\frac{n+1}{2}$.
        
        - If $n$ is **odd**, the median is the exact middle number.
            
        - If $n$ is **even**, the median is the average of the two middle numbers.
            
- **Properties:**
    
    1. **Robustness:** The median is **resistant to outliers**. Changing the largest value in a dataset to infinity does not change the median.
        
    2. **Usage:** It is the preferred measure for **skewed distributions** (e.g., household income, house prices).
        

---

### 3. The Mode

The mode represents the most frequent observation.

- **Definition:** The value that appears with the highest frequency in the dataset.
    
- **Properties:**
    
    1. **Not Unique:** A dataset can be **Unimodal** (one peak), **Bimodal** (two peaks), or **Multimodal**. It is also possible to have _no_ mode if all values are unique.
        
    2. **Categorical Data:** The mode is the **only** measure of central tendency that can be used for **Nominal Data** (e.g., "The modal blood type is O+").
        

---

### 4. Relationship between Mean, Median, and Mode

The relative position of these three measures tells us about the **Shape (Skewness)** of the distribution:

- Symmetrical (Normal) Distribution:
    
    $$Mean = Median = Mode$$
    
    The curve is a perfect mirror image.
    
- Positively Skewed (Right-Skewed):
    
    $$Mode < Median < Mean$$
    
    The "tail" extends to the right (positive direction). The Mean is pulled upward by high outliers.
    
    - _Example:_ Income distribution.
        
- Negatively Skewed (Left-Skewed):
    
    $$Mean < Median < Mode$$
    
    The "tail" extends to the left. The Mean is dragged down by low outliers.
    
    - _Example:_ Exam scores in an easy test.

### 5. Other Measures of Location (The "Knowledge Gap" Fill)

While standard courses focus on the "Big Three," advanced analysis often requires specialized means.

- Weighted Mean:
    
    Used when values have different degrees of importance (weights).
    
    $$\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum (w_i \cdot x_i)}{\sum w_i}$$
    
    - _Example:_ GPA calculation (where a 4-credit course counts more than a 2-credit course).
        
- Geometric Mean ($GM$):
    
    Used for data involving rates of change, growth percentages, or ratios (like investment returns). It prevents the overestimation bias of the arithmetic mean in compounding scenarios.
    
    $$GM = \sqrt[n]{x_1 \cdot x_2 \cdot ... \cdot x_n}$$
    
- Harmonic Mean ($HM$):
    
    Used for averaging rates (e.g., speed, density). If you drive to work at 60km/h and return at 40km/h, your average speed is the Harmonic Mean (48km/h), not the Arithmetic Mean (50km/h).
    
    $$HM = \frac{n}{\sum (\frac{1}{x_i})}$$
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

- **Exam Tip (Choice of Measure):**
    
    - If the data is **Nominal** $\rightarrow$ Use **Mode**.
        
    - If the data is **Ordinal** OR **Skewed Numerical** $\rightarrow$ Use **Median**.
        
    - If the data is **Symmetric Numerical** $\rightarrow$ Use **Mean**.
        
- **Resistance:** In exams, if asked "Which measure is resistant?", the answer is the **Median**. The Mean is non-resistant.
    
- **Quartiles:** The Median is also known as the **Second Quartile ($Q_2$)** or the **50th Percentile**.
