### 1. Introduction to Bivariate Analysis

Descriptive statistics is not limited to single variables. Science is often concerned with how two variables move in relation to one another. **Correlation** is a statistical measure that describes the size and direction of a relationship between two or more variables.

- **Positive Correlation:** As variable $X$ increases, variable $Y$ also increases (e.g., Study Time and Exam Score).
    
- **Negative Correlation:** As variable $X$ increases, variable $Y$ decreases (e.g., Absences and Exam Score).
    
- **Zero Correlation:** There is no systematic relationship between $X$ and $Y$.

### 2. Covariance: The Precursor

Before we calculate correlation, we calculate **Covariance**. This measures the joint variability of two random variables.

- **The Logic:** We look at the product of deviations.
    
    - If $X$ is above its mean and $Y$ is also above its mean, the product is positive.
        
    - If $X$ is high and $Y$ is low, the product is negative.
        
- The Formula (Sample Covariance):
    
    $$Cov(X,Y) = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{n-1}$$
    
- **The Problem with Covariance:** It is **unstandardized**. If you measure height in meters, the covariance might be 0.15. If you measure it in centimeters, the covariance jumps to 15. This makes it impossible to judge the "strength" of the relationship just by looking at the number.
    

---

### 3. Pearson Correlation Coefficient ($r$)

To fix the scaling issue of covariance, we divide it by the product of the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$. This normalizes the metric, creating a unitless coefficient known as **Pearson's $r$**.

- **Definition:** A measure of the strength and direction of the **linear** relationship between two continuous variables.
    
- The Formula:
    
    $$r = \frac{Cov(X,Y)}{s_x s_y} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$
    
- **Range:** The value always falls between **-1.0 and +1.0**.
    
    - **$r = +1.0$:** Perfect positive linear relationship.
        
    - **$r = -1.0$:** Perfect negative linear relationship.
        
    - **$r = 0$:** No linear relationship.
        

---

### 4. Interpretation of Magnitude

While there are no strict rules, the following guidelines are commonly used in social sciences to interpret the absolute value $|r|$:

- **$0.00 - 0.30$:** Negligible / Weak correlation.
    
- **$0.30 - 0.50$:** Moderate correlation.
    
- **$0.50 - 0.70$:** Strong correlation.
    
- **$0.70 - 1.00$:** Very strong correlation.
    

---

### 5. Knowledge Gap Fill: Coefficient of Determination ($r^2$)

While $r$ tells us the strength, it is not a percentage. To get a percentage, we square the correlation.

- **Definition:** $r^2$ represents the proportion of the variance for a dependent variable that's explained by an independent variable.
    
- **Example:** If the correlation between rainfall and crop yield is $r = 0.8$, then $r^2 = 0.64$.
    
    - _Interpretation:_ "64% of the variation in crop yield can be explained by the variation in rainfall." The remaining 36% is due to other factors (error).
        

---

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Correlation $\neq$ Causation:** This is the golden rule. Just because ice cream sales and shark attacks are correlated ($r > 0$) does not mean ice cream causes shark attacks. There is likely a **Confounding Variable** (e.g., Summer Heat) driving both.
    
2. **Linearity Assumption:** Pearson's $r$ _only_ measures linear relationships. If the data is shaped like a curve (parabola), $r$ might be close to 0 even though there is a perfect relationship. Always look at the Scatterplot first.
    
3. **Sensitivity to Outliers:** Like the Mean, Pearson's $r$ is very sensitive. A single extreme data point can artificially inflate or deflate the correlation coefficient.
    
4. **Notation:**
    
    - Sample Correlation: $r$
        
    - Population Correlation: $\rho$ (Rho)
