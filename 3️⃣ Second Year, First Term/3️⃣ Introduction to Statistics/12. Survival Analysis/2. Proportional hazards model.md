# 12.2 Proportional Hazards Model (Cox Regression) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the most widely used regression model in medical research. It allows us to answer the question: _"Does this drug keep people alive longer, even after adjusting for their Age, Sex, and Weight?"_
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Questions often focus on interpreting the **Hazard Ratio (HR)**. You rarely solve the full partial likelihood equation by hand, but you must understand the "Proportionality Assumption."
    

---

### **1. The Need for Cox Regression**

In Subtopic 12.1, we looked at survival curves ($S(t)$) for a single group. But in reality, we want to predict survival based on multiple variables (covariates) like Age ($x_1$), Treatment ($x_2$), and Genetics ($x_3$).

- **Problem:** Standard Linear Regression ($y = mx + c$) fails because survival data is censored (some people don't die) and highly skewed.
    
- **Solution:** The **Cox Proportional Hazards Model** (1972). It models the _Hazard Rate_ as a function of these covariates.
    

---

### **2. The Model Formula**

The model describes the hazard at time $t$ for an individual with covariates $X$:

$$h(t | X) = h_0(t) \times \exp(\beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + ... + \beta_k x_k)$$

- **$h(t | X)$:** The hazard rate for the individual.
    
- **$h_0(t)$:** The **Baseline Hazard Function**. This represents the risk for someone where all $x=0$ (the reference group). It can have _any_ shape (bathtub, increasing, etc.).
    
- **$\exp(...)$:** The exponential component that shifts the baseline hazard up or down based on the risk factors.
    

---

### **3. The Hazard Ratio (HR)**

The key output of a Cox model is the coefficient $\beta$. However, standard output converts this to a **Hazard Ratio**.

$$HR = e^{\beta}$$

- **Interpretation:**
    
    - **$HR = 1$ ($\beta = 0$):** No effect. The variable does not change the risk of death.
        
    - **$HR > 1$ ($\beta > 0$):** Risk Factor. The variable increases the risk of death (e.g., Smoking, HR = 1.5 implies 50% higher risk at any moment).
        
    - **$HR < 1$ ($\beta < 0$):** Protective Factor. The variable decreases the risk (e.g., Vaccine, HR = 0.4 implies 60% lower risk).
        

---

### **4. The "Proportionality" Assumption**

The critical assumption of this model is that the **Hazard Ratio is constant over time**.

- **Meaning:** If Smokers have 2x the risk of dying compared to Non-smokers at Year 1, they must also have 2x the risk at Year 5, Year 10, etc.
    
- **Visualization:** If you plot the $\ln(-\ln(S(t)))$ vs. $\ln(t)$ for two groups, the lines should be **parallel**. If the lines cross, the assumption is violated (e.g., a surgery is risky immediately but beneficial long-term).
    

---

### **5. Semi-Parametric Nature**

Cox Regression is called "Semi-Parametric" because:

1. **Parametric Part ($\beta$):** We explicitly estimate the coefficients for the risk factors.
    
2. **Non-Parametric Part ($h_0(t)$):** We leave the baseline hazard shape unspecified. We don't care if risk goes up or down over time; we only care that the _ratio_ between groups stays constant.
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Interpreting "Risk" vs. "Time":**
    
    - A Hazard Ratio of 2.0 means "You are dying twice as fast." It does **not** mean "You will live half as long." The relationship between Hazard and Median Survival Time is complex.
        
- **Assumption Checking:** In exams, if asked "Is this model valid?", look for the **Schoenfeld Residuals** test. If the test is significant ($P < 0.05$), the proportional hazards assumption has failed.
