### Introduction

**Survival Analysis** (also known as Reliability Theory in engineering) is the set of statistical methods used to analyze data where the outcome variable is the **time until the occurrence of an event**. While "death" is the classic event, the event can be anything: machine failure, divorce, relapse of a disease, or customer churn.

Standard regression methods (like OLS) fail here for two reasons:

1. **Non-Normality:** Survival times are strictly positive and typically skewed (not a Bell Curve).
    
2. **Censoring:** In most studies, the event does not occur for everyone by the time the study ends. We have partial information (e.g., "Patient A survived _at least_ 5 years"), which standard regression cannot handle.
    

---

### 1. Death-density, Survival, and Hazard Functions

To mathematically model "time to event" (random variable $T$), we define three interrelated functions.

- The Survival Function ($S(t)$):
    
    This is the probability that an individual survives longer than time $t$. It starts at 1 (100% survival at time 0) and declines toward 0 as time passes.
    
    $$S(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - F(t)$$
    
    (Where $F(t)$ is the Cumulative Distribution Function, or the probability of dying by time $t$).
    
- The Probability Density Function (Death Density) ($f(t)$):
    
    This indicates the unconditional probability of the event occurring at a specific instant $t$. It is the slope (rate of decline) of the Survival curve.
    
    $$f(t) = - \frac{d S(t)}{dt}$$
    
- The Hazard Function ($h(t)$ or $\lambda(t)$):
    
    This is the most critical concept for interpreting risk. It represents the instantaneous failure rate. It answers: "Given that the individual has survived up to time $t$, what is the immediate probability they will die in the next instant?"
    
    $$h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)}$$
    
    - _Note:_ Unlike probability, the Hazard rate can be greater than 1 (because it is a _rate_, not a proportion).

### 2. Proportional Hazards Model

While Kaplan-Meier _describes_ survival, we often want to _predict_ it based on variables (Age, Sex, Treatment). The **Cox Proportional Hazards Model** is the standard regression method for this.

- The Logic:
    
    It assumes that covariates (risk factors) act multiplicatively on the hazard.
    
    $$h(t | x) = h_0(t) \exp(\beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + ...)$$
    
    - $h(t | x)$: The hazard for an individual with covariates $x$.
        
    - $h_0(t)$: The **Baseline Hazard** (the risk when all $x=0$).
        
    - $\exp(\beta x)$: The change in risk.
        
- Why "Proportional"?
    
    The model assumes that the ratio of hazards between any two individuals is constant over time. If Drug A reduces your risk by 50% at Day 1, it must also reduce it by 50% at Year 5. The hazard curves should never cross.
    
- Interpreting Coefficients (Hazard Ratios):
    
    We exponentiate the coefficient ($e^{\beta}$) to get the Hazard Ratio (HR).
    
    - $HR = 1$: No effect.
        
    - $HR > 1$: Risk factor (Increases hazard/speed of death).
        
    - $HR < 1$: Protective factor (Reduces hazard/extends life).

### 3. Quantitative Risk Assessment

This subtopic broadens the scope from medical survival to general risk analysis, often in environmental or engineering contexts.

- Definition:
    
    The systematic process of estimating the likelihood and magnitude of adverse effects. It combines the Probability of an Event ($P$) with the Consequence of the Event ($C$).
    
    $$\text{Risk} = \text{Probability} \times \text{Consequence}$$
    
- **Steps in Assessment:**
    
    1. **Hazard Identification:** What could go wrong? (e.g., Chemical leak).
        
    2. **Dose-Response Assessment:** How does the probability of "death" (hazard) increase as exposure increases? (modeled using Survival/Hazard functions).
        
    3. **Exposure Assessment:** How many people are exposed and for how long?
        
    4. **Risk Characterization:** Combining the above to predict excess deaths or failures.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Censoring Types:** You must know the difference for exams.
    
    - **Right Censoring:** The most common. The study ends, or the patient leaves, before the event happens. We know survival time $t > X$.
        
    - **Left Censoring:** The event happened _before_ we started observing (less common).
        
    - **Interval Censoring:** We know the event happened between Time A and Time B, but not exactly when.
        
2. **Median Survival Time:** In survival analysis, we rarely use the Mean (Average) survival time because it requires everyone to die (which takes forever). Instead, we report the **Median Survival Time**: the time $t$ at which $S(t) = 0.5$ (when 50% of the group remains).
    
3. **Checking Proportionality:** If the survival curves of two groups cross each other (e.g., Surgery is risky at first but better long-term compared to Medicine), the Proportional Hazards assumption is violated. You cannot use the Cox model in this case.
