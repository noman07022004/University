# 12.1 Death-Density, Survival, and Hazard Functions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Survival analysis is unique because the data is "censored" (not everyone dies during the study). Therefore, we cannot simply look at a histogram of death times. We must describe the process using three mathematically linked functions: $f(t)$, $S(t)$, and $h(t)$.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** You will frequently be asked to convert between these functions (e.g., "Given the Survival function, calculate the Hazard rate") or interpret the shape of a hazard curve.
    

---

### **1. The Random Variable ($T$)**

In this topic, the primary random variable is **Time ($T$)**.

- It is a non-negative continuous random variable ($T \ge 0$).
    
- It measures the duration until a specific event occurs (e.g., death, machine failure, relapse).
    

---

### **2. The Death-Density Function ($f(t)$)**

This is the standard **Probability Density Function (PDF)** that you learned in Topic 4.

- **Definition:** It describes the instantaneous probability that the event occurs at _precisely_ time $t$.
    
- **Notation:** $f(t)$
    
- Formula: It is the derivative of the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF), $F(t)$.
    
    $$f(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t + \Delta t)}{\Delta t}$$
    
- **Interpretation:** The height of the curve represents the concentration of deaths at that moment. The total area under the curve from $0$ to $\infty$ is 1.
    

---

### **3. The Survival Function ($S(t)$)**

This is the most intuitive function in medical research. It describes the probability of "surviving" past a certain time.

- **Definition:** The probability that the event has **not** yet happened by time $t$ (i.e., the subject is still alive).
    
- Formula:
    
    $$S(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - F(t) = \int_{t}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx$$
    
- **Properties:**
    
    1. **Starts at 1:** $S(0) = 1$ (Everyone is alive at the start).
        
    2. **Monotonically Decreasing:** $S(t)$ can only go down (dead people do not come back to life).
        
    3. **Ends at 0:** $S(\infty) = 0$ (Eventually, everyone dies).
        

---

### **4. The Hazard Function ($h(t)$)**

This is the most critical concept for "risk." It is also called the **Instantaneous Failure Rate** or **Force of Mortality**.

- **Definition:** Given that a subject has survived up to time $t$, what is the probability they die in the very next instant?
    
- **The Nuance:** It is a _rate_, not a probability (so it can be greater than 1).
    
- Formula:
    
    $$h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)}$$
    
    (Density divided by Survival)
    
- **Interpretation:**
    
    - **Increasing Hazard:** Aging (e.g., Human mortality). The older you get, the higher the risk of dying _now_.
        
    - **Decreasing Hazard:** Infant mortality or "Burn-in" period. If you survive the dangerous beginning, you are likely to survive a long time.
        
    - **Constant Hazard:** Accidents (e.g., Radioactive decay). The risk is the same every day. (This implies an Exponential Distribution).
        

---

### **5. Mathematical Relationships (The "Golden Triangle")**

These three functions are mathematically interchangeable. If you know one, you can derive the others.

1. From Survival to Density:
    
    $$f(t) = -\frac{d}{dt} S(t)$$
    
    (The rate at which the survival curve drops).
    
2. From Hazard to Survival:
    
    $$S(t) = \exp \left( - \int_{0}^{t} h(x) \, dx \right) = e^{-H(t)}$$
    
    - Where $H(t)$ is the **Cumulative Hazard Function**.
        
3. From Survival to Hazard:
    
    $$h(t) = -\frac{d}{dt} \ln S(t)$$
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The Bathtub Curve:** In reliability engineering (and human biology), the hazard function often looks like a bathtub:
    
    1. **High decreasing hazard** early on (infant mortality / manufacturing defects).
        
    2. **Low constant hazard** in the middle (adulthood / useful life).
        
    3. **High increasing hazard** at the end (senescence / wear-out).
        
- **Censoring:** The main reason we need these specific functions is to handle **Censoring** (Topic 12.2). We compute $S(t)$ using methods like Kaplan-Meier because we can't observe the true $f(t)$ for everyone (some people quit the study).
