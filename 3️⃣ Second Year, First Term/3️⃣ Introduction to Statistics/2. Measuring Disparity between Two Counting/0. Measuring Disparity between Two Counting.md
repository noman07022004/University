## 2. Measuring Disparity(তারতম্য/বৈষম্য) between Two Counting ⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This unit bridges the gap between simple data description and probability theory. Understanding "how to count" (Combinatorics) is a prerequisite for calculating any probability in later topics.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While fundamental, direct questions on combinatorics are often distinct, smaller problems. However, the concept of **Proportions** becomes the primary focus in later inferential statistics (Topic 5 & 6), making it critical for long-term exam success.

In statistics, before we can model continuous data (like height or time), we must master **Discrete(বিচ্ছিন্ন) Mathematics**—the mathematics of counting. This topic, "Measuring Disparity between Two Counting," focuses on quantifying events. It moves from the fundamental rules of counting arrangements (Permutations and Combinations) to the simplest statistical metric (Proportions) and finally to the study of how counts deviate from expected equilibrium(ভারসাম্য) in random processes (Fluctuation Theory).

To build a logical foundation for Probability (Topic 3), we will start with the rules of counting, move to the calculation of relative frequency (proportions), and conclude with the theoretical behavior of these counts over time.

---

### 1. Permutations(বিন্যাস) and Combinations(সমন্বয়)

This subtopic represents the core of **Combinatorics(সমন্বয়বিদ্যা)**: determining the number of ways to select or arrange items from a group.

- The Fundamental Counting Principle:
    
    If one event can occur in $m$ ways and a second independent event can occur in $n$ ways, together they can occur in $m \times n$ ways.
    
- Factorial Notation ($n!$):
    
    The product of all positive integers less than or equal to $n$.
    
    $$n! = n \times (n-1) \times (n-2) \times ... \times 1$$
    
    (Note: By definition, $0! = 1$).
    
- Permutations ($P$):
    
    Used when the Order Matters. It calculates the number of ways to arrange $r$ items selected from a set of $n$ distinct items.
    
    - Formula:
        
        $$^nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$$
        
    - _Example:_ Determining the Gold, Silver, and Bronze winners in a race of 10 people (Order matters: Gold $\neq$ Silver).
        
- Combinations ($C$):
    
    Used when the Order Does Not Matter. It calculates the number of ways to select $r$ items from a set of $n$, disregarding arrangement.
    
    - Formula:
        
        $$^nC_r = \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$$
        
    - _Example:_ Selecting a committee of 3 students from a class of 10. (The group {A, B, C} is the same as {C, B, A}).

### 2. Proportions

Once we can count specific outcomes (using combinatorics) and total possibilities, we can measure the **disparity** or relationship between a specific count and the total.

- Definition:
    
    A proportion represents a part of a whole. If we have a population of size $N$ and a count of success $X$, the population proportion ($P$) is:
    
    $$P = \frac{X}{N}$$
    
    (For a sample, the notation is $\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}$).
    
- Disparity between Two Counts:
    
    This refers to comparing two proportions or counts to see if they differ significantly. While formal testing comes in Topic 5, understanding the Difference in Proportions ($p_1 - p_2$) is established here. It quantifies how much "Count A" disparities from "Count B" relative to their totals.
    
- Knowledge Gap Fill: The Binomial(দ্বিপদী) Coefficient(সহগ) Connection
    
    Proportions are intrinsically linked to the Binomial Theorem. The coefficients in a binomial expansion $(a+b)^n$ are actually the Combinations $\binom{n}{r}$. This mathematical link explains why proportions follow a Binomial Distribution (Topic 4).

### 3. Fluctuation Theory

This is a more advanced theoretical concept often associated with **Random Walks** (e.g., coin tossing sequences). It challenges the intuition that "things average out" quickly. It studies the disparity between two counting processes (e.g., Heads vs. Tails) over time.

- The Concept:
    
    If two candidates (A and B) are receiving votes (or we are tossing a coin), Fluctuation Theory analyzes the probability that one side stays ahead of the other for a specific duration.
    
- The Ballot Problem:
    
    A classic theorem by Bertrand (1887). If Candidate A scores $a$ votes and Candidate B scores $b$ votes, and $a > b$, the probability that A leads B throughout the entire counting process is:
    
    $$P = \frac{a - b}{a + b}$$
    
- The Arcsine Law:
    
    Counter-intuitively, in a fair coin toss game of length $2n$, it is least likely that the lead is split 50/50 time-wise. It is much more likely that one side leads for the vast majority of the time. This describes the "fluctuation" or "disparity" from the expected equilibrium(ভারসাম্য).

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Calculator Proficiency:** Ensure you know exactly where the $nCr$ and $nPr$ buttons are on your scientific calculator. You will not have time to calculate factorials manually in an exam.
    
2. **The "Order" Keyword:** To distinguish Permutations from Combinations in word problems, look for keywords:
    
    - **Permutation:** Arrange, Schedule, Order, Rank, Titles (President/VP).
        
    - **Combination:** Select, Choose, Group, Committee, Team.
        
3. **Proportions bound:** Remember that a proportion must always be between 0 and 1 inclusive. If you calculate a proportion of 1.2 or -0.5, you have made a math error.
    
4. **Zero Factorial:** Do not forget that $0! = 1$. This is a common "gotcha" in algebra questions involving combinations.
