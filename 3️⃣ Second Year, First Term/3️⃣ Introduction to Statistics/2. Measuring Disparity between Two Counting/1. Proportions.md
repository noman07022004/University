## 2.1 Proportions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Proportions are the fundamental building block for categorical data analysis. While mathematically simple (it's just a fraction), understanding the distinction between a _sample proportion_ ($\hat{p}$) and a _population proportion_ ($P$ or $\pi$) is critical for the inferential statistics that follow in Topics 5 and 6.  
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** You will rarely be asked to "calculate a proportion" in isolation because it is too easy. However, you will constantly calculate proportions as the first step in Z-tests, Chi-Square tests, and Confidence Intervals. If you get this basic step wrong, the entire calculation follows suit.
    

---

### 1. Definition and Notation

In the previous section (Descriptive Statistics), we dealt largely with continuous data (Means). When we switch to **Categorical Data** (Qualitative), we cannot calculate a mean. Instead, we count the number of "Successes" and divide by the total number of observations.

- **The Proportion ($p$):** A ratio describing the part of the whole that possesses a specific characteristic.
    
- Formula:
    
    $$p = \frac{x}{n}$$
    
    - $x$: The count of "successes" (or the category of interest).
        
    - $n$: The total sample size.
        
- **Notation:**
    
    - **Population Proportion ($P$ or $\pi$):** The true ratio in the entire population. (e.g., "The true approval rating of the President").
        
    - **Sample Proportion ($\hat{p}$ - read as "p-hat"):** The ratio observed in your specific data. (e.g., "The approval rating in a poll of 1000 people").
        
    - **Complement ($q$):** The proportion of failures. $q = 1 - p$.
        

---

### 2. Properties of Proportions

1. Bounded Range: A proportion must always lie between 0 and 1, inclusive.
    
    $$0 \le p \le 1$$
    
    - _Note:_ In exams, if you calculate a Z-score and get a proportion of 1.5, you have made a calculation error.
        
2. Summation: The sum of proportions for all mutually exclusive categories in a variable must equal 1.
    
    $$\sum p_i = 1$$
    
3. Standard Error of a Proportion: unlike a mean where we need a separate standard deviation ($s$), the variability of a proportion is determined entirely by the proportion itself.
    
    $$\sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$$
    
    - _Insight:_ This means a proportion close to 0.5 (50/50) has the highest variance (uncertainty), while proportions close to 0 or 1 have very low variance.
        

---

### 3. Measuring Disparity (The "Difference in Proportions")

The title of this main topic is "Measuring Disparity." In statistics, we are often less interested in a single number (e.g., "40% of Group A smokes") and more interested in the **Disparity** or **Gap** between two groups.

- The Difference:
    
    $$D = \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2$$
    
- **Interpretation:**
    
    - If $D = 0$: There is no disparity (Equality).
        
    - If $D > 0$: Group 1 has a higher prevalence than Group 2.
        
- **Context:** This simple subtraction is the foundation for the **Two-Sample Z-Test for Proportions** (Topic 5). It quantifies how far apart two counting processes are.
    

---

### 4. Connection to Probability Distributions

Proportions are not just arithmetic; they are intrinsically linked to probability distributions.

- **Bernoulli Trial:** A single experiment with two outcomes (Success/Failure).
    
- **Binomial Distribution:** If you repeat a Bernoulli trial $n$ times, the count of successes $X$ follows a Binomial distribution. The proportion $\hat{p} = X/n$ is simply the _average_ outcome of these Bernoulli trials.
    
- **Normal Approximation:** Thanks to the Central Limit Theorem, if $n$ is large enough (usually $np > 5$ and $nq > 5$), the sampling distribution of proportions becomes a Normal (Bell) Curve. This allows us to use Z-tables to calculate probabilities for proportions.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

- **Percentage vs. Proportion:** In academic formulas, **ALWAYS** use the decimal proportion (0.50), never the percentage (50). Using "50" in a formula like $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ will give you a mathematical disaster.
    
- **"Success" is Arbitrary:** In statistics, "Success" just means the category you are counting. It doesn't mean "Good." If you are counting death rates, "Death" is the "Success" ($x$) and "Survival" is the "Failure."
    
- **Sample Size Sensitivity:** The precision of a proportion depends heavily on $n$.
    
    - $1$ success in $2$ trials = 50% (High uncertainty).
        
    - $500$ successes in $1000$ trials = 50% (High certainty).
        
    - This is why we calculate Standard Error.
