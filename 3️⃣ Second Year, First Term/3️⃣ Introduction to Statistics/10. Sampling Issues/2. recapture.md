# 10.2 Capture/Recapture ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This is a specialized sampling technique used when you cannot simply "count" the population because it is moving, hidden, or too large (e.g., fish in a lake, homeless populations, or software bugs). It relies on simple algebra but requires strict adherence to assumptions.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** This is a favorite for calculation-based questions in applied statistics exams. You are usually given the numbers for the two catches and asked to estimate the total population or critique the validity of the estimate based on the assumptions.
    

---

### **1. The Scenario**

How do you count the number of fish in a lake without draining the water? You cannot use Simple Random Sampling because you don't have a list of all fish to pick from.

Instead, we use the **Capture-Recapture Method** (also known as the **Lincoln-Petersen Index**). It uses probability ratios to infer the total size based on the overlap between two samples.

---

### **2. The Procedure**

The method involves two distinct sampling events separated by time.

1. **First Capture (Time 1):**
    
    - You capture a random sample of individuals from the population.
        
    - Count them ($M$).
        
    - **Mark** them (using tags, paint, leg bands, etc.) so they can be identified later.
        
    - **Release** them back into the population and allow them time to mix thoroughly.
        
2. **Second Capture (Time 2):**
    
    - You capture a second random sample of individuals.
        
    - Count the total size of this second sample ($n$).
        
    - Count how many of them have the mark from the first capture ($m$ or $R$).
        

---

### **3. The Formula (Lincoln-Petersen Estimator)**

The logic is based on a simple proportion:

- The proportion of _marked_ animals in the **Second Sample** should theoretically equal the proportion of _marked_ animals in the **Total Population**.
    

$$\frac{\text{Marked in Sample 2}}{\text{Total Size of Sample 2}} = \frac{\text{Total Marked (Time 1)}}{\text{Total Population (Unknown)}}$$

$$\frac{m}{n} = \frac{M}{N}$$

Rearranging to solve for the Total Population ($N$):

$$\hat{N} = \frac{M \times n}{m}$$

- $\hat{N}$: Estimated Total Population.
    
- $M$: Number captured and marked in the first round.
    
- $n$: Total number captured in the second round.
    
- $m$: Number of recaptured (marked) individuals in the second round.
    

---

### **4. Critical Assumptions**

For this estimate to be valid, the following conditions (often asked in exams) must be met. If any are violated, the estimate $\hat{N}$ will be biased.

1. **Closed Population:** The population size ($N$) does not change between the two captures.
    
    - No **Births** or **Deaths**.
        
    - No **Immigration** (entering) or **Emigration** (leaving).
        
2. **Perfect Marking:**
    
    - Tags do not fall off (if tags are lost, $m$ decreases, and $\hat{N}$ is overestimated).
        
    - Tags are easily recognized.
        
3. **Random Mixing:** The marked animals must mix randomly with the unmarked ones. If they stay huddled together, your second sample might catch all of them (underestimating $N$) or none of them (overestimating $N$).
    
4. **Equal Catchability:** Being caught once does not affect the probability of being caught again.
    
    - _Trap Shy:_ Animals learn to avoid the trap. ($m$ is too low $\to$ $\hat{N}$ overestimated).
        
    - _Trap Happy:_ Animals return for the bait. ($m$ is too high $\to$ $\hat{N}$ underestimated).
        

---

### **5. Knowledge Gap Fill: The Chapman Estimator**

The basic Lincoln-Petersen formula has a flaw: if you catch **zero** marked animals in the second round ($m=0$), you divide by zero, and the population estimate is undefined (infinity).

To fix this bias in small samples, statisticians use the **Chapman Estimator**:

$$\hat{N}_{Chapman} = \frac{(M+1)(n+1)}{m+1} - 1$$

- _Note:_ Use this formula if asked for a "less biased" estimate or if $m$ is very small.
    

---

### **6. Application in Software Engineering**

Interestingly, this biology technique is widely used in IT to estimate the **number of bugs** remaining in a piece of software.

- **Tester A (Capture 1):** Finds 50 bugs ($M=50$).
    
- **Tester B (Capture 2):** Independently tests the same code and finds 40 bugs ($n=40$).
    
- **Overlap:** They compare lists and find that 20 bugs were found by _both_ testers ($m=20$).
    
- Estimate:
    
    $$\hat{N} = \frac{50 \times 40}{20} = 100 \text{ total bugs}$$
    
- **Conclusion:** Since 70 unique bugs were found ($50+40-20$), there are likely about **30 bugs still hidden** ($100 - 70$).
