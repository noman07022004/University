### Introduction

In Topic 5, we compared two proportions (e.g., Treatment vs. Control). However, real-world research often involves categorical variables with **multiple levels**. For instance, we might want to compare political affiliation (Democrat, Republican, Independent) across multiple regions (North, South, East, West).

This requires analyzing **Multiple Proportions** simultaneously. The primary tool for this is the **Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) Distribution**, which measures how far our observed data deviates from what we would expect if there were no relationship (independence) or if the data followed a specific pattern (goodness of fit).

Once a test shows that a difference exists, we face two subsequent problems:

1. **Multiple Comparisons:** Which specific groups are different? (Handled by Bonferroni).
    
2. **Strength of Association:** Is the difference strong or weak? (Handled by Phi-squared and Tau B).
    

---

### 1. Using Chi-Squared to Test Goodness of Fit

The Chi-Squared test is a non-parametric test used for categorical data. It comes in two primary forms relevant to "Multiple Proportions."

#### A. Goodness of Fit Test
This tests a single categorical variable to see if it follows a hypothesized distribution.

- **Question:** _"Does this die roll fairly?"_ or _"Does the distribution of blood types in this sample match the national average?"_
    
- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The observed probabilities match the theoretical probabilities (e.g., $P_1 = 0.2, P_2 = 0.3...$).
    
- **Degrees of Freedom ($df$):** $k - 1$ (where $k$ is the number of categories).
    

#### B. Test of Independence (Homogeneity)

This extends the concept to two variables, testing if they are related.

- **Question:** _"Is voting preference independent of gender?"_
    
- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The two variables are independent (Proportions are equal across all groups).
    
- The Statistic:
    
    $$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$
    
    - $O_{ij}$: Observed count in row $i$, column $j$.
        
    - $E_{ij}$: Expected count (calculated as $\frac{\text{Row Total} \times \text{Column Total}}{\text{Grand Total}}$).
        
- **Degrees of Freedom ($df$):** $(r-1)(c-1)$, where $r$ is the number of rows and $c$ is the number of columns.
---

### 2. Bonferroni's Inequality and Multiple Comparisons

If the Chi-Squared test rejects the Null Hypothesis (i.e., "The proportions are different"), it doesn't tell us _where_ the difference lies. A naive researcher might run many separate t-tests comparing every possible pair of groups (Group A vs B, B vs C, A vs C...). This leads to the **Multiple Testing Problem**.

- **The Problem:** Every time you run a test, there is a risk of a Type I error (False Positive), usually $\alpha = 0.05$. If you run 20 tests, the probability of getting at least one false positive approaches 100%.
    
- Bonferroni's Inequality:
    
    The probability of the union of events is less than or equal to the sum of their individual probabilities.
    
    $$P(\cup E_i) \leq \sum P(E_i)$$
    
- The Bonferroni Correction:
    
    To maintain an overall family-wise error rate of $\alpha$ (e.g., 0.05) when performing $m$ number of tests, you must test each individual hypothesis at a stricter significance level:
    
    $$\alpha_{new} = \frac{\alpha_{original}}{m}$$
    
    - _Implication:_ This makes it much harder to claim significance, essentially "penalizing" you for fishing for results.

### 3. Phi-Squared and Tau B

A Chi-Squared test only tells you if a relationship is _statistically significant_. It does not tell you if the relationship is _strong_. A very weak relationship can be "significant" if the sample size is huge. To measure the **magnitude** (strength), we use measures of association.

#### A. Phi-Squared ($\phi^2$)

Used primarily for $2 \times 2$ tables (and related to Cramer's V for larger tables).

- **Definition:** It normalizes the Chi-squared value by the sample size to create a measure of correlation for categorical data.
    
- Formula:
    
    $$\phi^2 = \frac{\chi^2}{n}$$
    
    $$\phi = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n}}$$
    
- **Interpretation:** Ranges from 0 (no association) to 1 (perfect association). It is similar to the correlation coefficient $r$.
    

#### B. Kendall's Tau-b ($\tau_b$)

Used when the categorical variables are **Ordinal** (ranked data).

- **Context:** Unlike Phi, which treats categories as nominal (names only), Tau-b respects the order (e.g., Low, Medium, High).
    
- **Logic:** It looks at "concordant pairs" (rankings that agree) versus "discordant pairs" (rankings that disagree).
    
- **Value:** Ranges from -1 to +1.
    
    - $+1$: Perfect positive association (as one rank goes up, the other goes up).
        
    - $-1$: Perfect negative association.
        
    - $0$: No association.
        
- _Academic Note:_ Tau-b is preferred over Tau-a or Tau-c when the contingency table is square ($r=c$) and there are "ties" in the data.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **The "Rule of 5":** For the Chi-Squared approximation to be valid, the **Expected Count ($E$)** in every cell must be at least 5.
    
    - _Tip:_ If you have cells with $E < 5$ in an exam problem, you must mention that the test might be invalid. The solution is often to combine (collapse) adjacent categories to increase the count.
        
2. **Chi-Squared is One-Sided:** Even though we look for differences, the Chi-Squared test is mathematically always a **right-tailed** test. A value of 0 indicates perfect fit; only a large positive value indicates a discrepancy.
    
3. **Bonferroni Conservatism:** While Bonferroni is safe, it is considered very "conservative." It often fails to detect real differences (Type II error) because it lowers the alpha so much. In advanced stats, methods like Tukey's HSD are often preferred for this reason, but for this syllabus, stick to Bonferroni.
    
4. **Phi vs. Chi:** Remember the distinction:
    
    - **Chi-Squared:** Evidence of existence (Yes/No).
        
    - **Phi/Tau:** Measure of strength (How much?).
