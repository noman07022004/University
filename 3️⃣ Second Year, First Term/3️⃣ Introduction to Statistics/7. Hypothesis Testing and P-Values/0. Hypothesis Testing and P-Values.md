## 7. Hypothesis Testing and P-Values ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the conceptual climax of statistical inference. The "P-value" is the most widely used (and abused) metric in science. Understanding exactly what it means—and what it _doesn't_ mean—is the hallmark of a competent statistician.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** While calculation questions are common, exam boards frequently ask "interpretation" questions here (e.g., "Explain what a p-value of 0.03 implies in this context"). You must be precise with your wording to get full marks.

---

### Introduction

We have already used hypothesis testing in previous topics (comparing proportions), but this section formalizes the **General Theory of Hypothesis Testing**.

In any statistical test, we condense complex data into a single number called a **Test Statistic**. We then ask: _"How likely is it to get a statistic this large just by random chance?"_ To answer this, we need three components:

1. A specific method to calculate the statistic (e.g., **The Wald Test**).
    
2. A theoretical distribution to compare it against (e.g., **$\chi^2$ Distribution**).
    
3. A standardized measure of evidence (e.g., **P-Values**).
    

We will organize the subtopics by starting with the theoretical distribution, moving to the specific testing method, and concluding with the universal metric of evidence.

---

### 1. The $\chi^2$ Distribution (Chi-Squared)

Before we can test anything, we need a reference map. The Chi-Squared distribution is the mathematical backbone for many tests involving variance, counts, and squared deviations.

- Definition:
    
    If you take a standard normal random variable $Z$ (where $\mu=0, \sigma=1$) and square it, you get a Chi-Squared random variable with 1 degree of freedom ($df$). If you sum $k$ independent squared standard normal variables, the result follows a Chi-Squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom.
    
    $$Q = \sum_{i=1}^{k} Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2_k$$
    

- **Key Properties:**
    
    - **Non-negative:** Since it is a sum of squares, $\chi^2$ can never be negative. Range is $[0, \infty)$.
        
    - **Skewed:** It is highly skewed to the right, especially for low degrees of freedom. As $df$ increases, it looks more like a Normal distribution.
        
    - **Role in Testing:** It serves as the reference distribution for the Wald Test, Goodness of Fit (Topic 6), and tests of independence.
        

---

### 2. The Wald Test

The Wald Test is a general parametric statistical test. While the T-test is used for simple means, the Wald test is the "Swiss Army Knife" used for testing parameters in more complex models (like Maximum Likelihood Estimation in Logistic Regression).

- The Logic:
    
    It assesses how far an estimated parameter ($\hat{\theta}$) is from a hypothesized value ($\theta_0$), relative to its standard error. It is essentially a "squared Z-test."
    
- The Formula:
    
    $$W = \frac{(\hat{\theta} - \theta_0)^2}{Var(\hat{\theta})}$$
    
    Alternatively, using standard error ($SE$):
    
    $$W = \left( \frac{\hat{\theta} - \theta_0}{SE(\hat{\theta})} \right)^2$$
    
- Distribution:
    
    Under the Null Hypothesis, the Wald statistic ($W$) follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with 1 degree of freedom.
    
- Usage:
    
    You will see this most often when reading computer output for regression coefficients (Topic 15). If the Wald statistic is large, the coefficient is significantly different from zero, meaning the variable matters.
    

---

### 3. P-Values

Once we calculate a test statistic (like $W$ or $Z$), we need to interpret it. The P-value is the universal translator that converts these arbitrary statistics into a probability.

- Formal Definition:
    
    The P-value is the probability of observing a test statistic as extreme or more extreme than the one observed, assuming that the Null Hypothesis ($H_0$) is true.
    
    $$P\text{-value} = P(\text{Data} | H_0)$$
    
- **Decision Rule:**
    
    - Compare the P-value to your Significance Level ($\alpha$), typically 0.05.
        
    - **If $P < \alpha$:** The observed data is extremely unlikely under $H_0$. Therefore, we **Reject $H_0$** (Statistically Significant).
        
    - **If $P \ge \alpha$:** The observed data is consistent with chance. We **Fail to Reject $H_0$** (Not Statistically Significant).
        
- Misconception Alert:
    
    The P-value is NOT the probability that the Null Hypothesis is true.
    
    - _Wrong:_ "P=0.04 means there is a 96% chance the hypothesis is correct."
        
    - _Correct:_ "P=0.04 means if the null were true, we'd see data this extreme only 4% of the time."
        

---

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **The "Holy Trinity" of Tests:** In advanced statistics (and likely your Section B), the Wald Test is often compared to two others:
    
    - **Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT):** Comparing the fit of two models (usually most accurate).
        
    - **Score Test:** Checking the slope of the likelihood function.
        
    - **Wald Test:** Checking the distance from the estimate. (Approximation, easiest to compute).
        
2. **P-Value Arbitrariness:** The threshold of 0.05 is purely historical (Fisher used it). In some fields (particle physics), the threshold is $0.0000003$ ($5\sigma$). In social sciences, $0.10$ is sometimes acceptable. Always check the $\alpha$ given in your exam question.
    
3. **One-Sided P-values:** If you perform a one-sided test (e.g., $H_1: \mu > 0$), the P-value is half of what it would be for a two-sided test ($H_1: \mu \neq 0$). Be careful when reading tables.
    
4. **Relationship:** $\text{Wald Statistic} \approx Z^2$. If you know the Z-score is 2, the Wald statistic is 4.
