
# 7.3 $\chi^2$ Distribution (Chi-Squared) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This distribution is the theoretical backbone for analyzing categorical data (counts) and variance. While the Normal Distribution models the _mean_ of data, the Chi-Squared Distribution models the _variance_ (or "squared deviations") of data.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** You will use the Chi-Squared table in almost every exam for Goodness of Fit (Topic 6), Independence testing, and the Wald Test (Topic 7.1). Understanding how to read the table (especially regarding Degrees of Freedom) is mandatory.
    

---

### **1. Definition and Derivation**

The **Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) Distribution** is a continuous probability distribution. It is not fundamental in nature like the Normal distribution; rather, it is mathematically **derived** from the Standard Normal Distribution.

- The Derivation:
    
    If you take a Standard Normal random variable $Z$ (where $\mu=0, \sigma=1$) and square it, the result follows a Chi-Squared distribution with 1 degree of freedom.
    
    $$Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1$$
    
- General Definition:
    
    If you take $k$ independent Standard Normal variables ($Z_1, Z_2, ..., Z_k$), square each one, and sum them up, the resulting sum follows a Chi-Squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom.
    
    $$Q = \sum_{i=1}^{k} Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2_k$$
    
- **Conceptual Link:** This explains why the formula for Variance (which sums squared differences: $\sum(x-\bar{x})^2$) uses the Chi-Squared distribution for inference.
    

---

### **2. Properties of the Curve**

Unlike the Normal curve, which is symmetric and centered at zero, the $\chi^2$ distribution has distinct properties:

1. **Non-Negative:** Since it is a sum of squares, $\chi^2$ can never be negative. The domain is $[0, \infty)$.
    
2. **Right-Skewed (Positively Skewed):** The tail extends to the right. Most of the probability mass is concentrated near 0 for low degrees of freedom.
    
3. **Shape Depends on $df$ ($k$):**
    
    - **Small $df$ (e.g., 1 or 2):** The curve is L-shaped (highest at 0) or highly skewed.
        
    - **Large $df$:** As degrees of freedom increase, the curve becomes more symmetric and bell-shaped.
        
    - **Limit:** As $df \to \infty$, the $\chi^2$ distribution converges to a **Normal Distribution** (Central Limit Theorem).
        

---

### **3. Parameters: Mean and Variance**

The shape of the distribution is determined entirely by the parameter $k$ (degrees of freedom).

- Mean (Expected Value):
    
    $$\mu = k$$
    
    - _Intuition:_ Since $E[Z^2] \approx 1$, if you sum $k$ of them, the expected total is $k$.
        
- Variance:
    
    $$\sigma^2 = 2k$$
    
- Mode (Peak):
    
    For $k \ge 2$, the mode is at $k-2$.
    

---

### **4. Role in Hypothesis Testing**

The $\chi^2$ distribution serves as the "Reference Map" for three major families of tests covered in your syllabus:

1. **Tests of Count Data (Topic 6):**
    
    - Goodness of Fit and Independence tests calculate a statistic based on $(O-E)^2$. Since this measures "squared error," it naturally follows the $\chi^2$ distribution.
        
2. **Inference about Variance:**
    
    - To test if a population variance equals a specific value ($H_0: \sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2$), we use the statistic:
        
        $$\chi^2 = \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2}$$
        
3. **The Wald Test (Topic 7.1):**
    
    - As noted previously, the Wald statistic for a single parameter is approximately $Z^2$, which is $\chi^2_1$.
        

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Degrees of Freedom (The Critical Parameter):** You cannot look up a critical value without knowing $df$.
    
    - For Goodness of Fit: $df = k - 1$.
        
    - For Contingency Tables: $df = (rows-1)(cols-1)$.
        
    - For Variance Tests: $df = n - 1$.
        
- **One-Tailed Nature:** Almost all Chi-Squared tests in introductory statistics are **Right-Tailed**.
    
    - We reject $H_0$ only if the calculated statistic is **large** (in the right tail).
        
    - A value near 0 means the Observed data matches the Expected theory perfectly, which is never grounds for rejection.
        
- **Notation:** The critical value is denoted as $\chi^2_{\alpha, df}$. Ensure you do not confuse the significance level $\alpha$ with the shape parameter in other distributions.
