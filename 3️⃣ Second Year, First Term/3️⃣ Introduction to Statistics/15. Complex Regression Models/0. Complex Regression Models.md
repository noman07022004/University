### Introduction

Simple Linear Regression (OLS) assumes a continuous dependent variable ($Y$), independent observations, and linear relationships. **Complex Regression Models** are specialized tools designed to handle data that breaks these rules.

We can categorize these advanced techniques into three families:

1. **Discrete Outcome Models:** When $Y$ is a category or a count (Logit, Probit, Poisson).
    
2. **Structural Modifications:** When the relationship depends on time or other variables (Time Series, Interactions, Systems).
    
3. **Resampling Methods:** Computational techniques to validate our models (Bootstrap, Cross-Validation).
    

---

### Part I: Discrete Outcome Models

Standard regression can predict a value of $Y = -0.5$ or $Y = 105$, which makes no sense if $Y$ is a probability (0 to 1) or a count (must be a positive integer).

#### 1. Logit and Probit Regression

Used when the dependent variable ($Y$) is **Binary** (0 or 1 / No or Yes).

- **The Problem with OLS:** If you use a straight line for binary data (Linear Probability Model), predictions can exceed 1 or drop below 0.
    
- **The Solution:** We map the linear equation into an **"S-shaped" curve** (Sigmoid function) bounded between 0 and 1.
    
- **Logit (Logistic Regression):** Uses the cumulative logistic distribution.
    
    - _Interpretation:_ Results are interpreted as **Odds Ratios**. If the coefficient for Age is positive, older people have higher _odds_ of the outcome.
        
    - Formula: $\ln(\frac{P}{1-P}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$
        
- **Probit:** Uses the cumulative Normal distribution ($\Phi$).
    
    - _Usage:_ Often preferred in economics and toxicology. The results are similar to Logit but computationally different.
        

#### 2. Poisson and Negative Binomial Regression

Used when the dependent variable ($Y$) is a **Count** (e.g., Number of car accidents, Number of doctor visits).

- **Poisson Regression:**
    
    - _Assumption:_ The data follows a Poisson distribution where **Mean = Variance** ($\mu = \sigma^2$).
        
    - _Model:_ Models the _log_ of the expected count: $\ln(\hat{Y}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$.
        
- **Negative Binomial Regression:**
    
    - _The Fix:_ In real data, the variance is often much larger than the mean (called **Overdispersion**). Poisson regression underestimates the standard errors in this case, leading to false significance.
        
    - _Solution:_ Negative Binomial regression adds an extra parameter ($\alpha$) to account for this overdispersion, making it the robust alternative to Poisson.

### Part II: Structural and System Modifications

#### 3. Time Series

Used when observations are measured over time (e.g., Stock prices, GDP).

- **Violation:** Data points are not independent (today's price depends on yesterday's price), violating the "Independence" assumption of OLS.
    
- **Autocorrelation:** We must test if residuals are correlated with themselves (using the **Durbin-Watson** test).
    
- **Modeling:** We often include "lagged" variables (using $Y_{t-1}$ to predict $Y_t$).
    

#### 4. Interactive Models

Used when the effect of one variable ($X_1$) depends on the level of another variable ($X_2$).

- **Concept:** In a simple model, we assume the effect of Education on Income is the same for everyone. But what if Education pays off more for Men than Women?
    
- The Interaction Term: We add a product term to the regression:
    
    $$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 (X_1 \times X_2)$$
    
- **Interpretation:** $\beta_3$ tells us how the slope of $X_1$ changes as $X_2$ changes.
    

#### 5. System of Equations

Sometimes $Y$ depends on $X$, but $X$ also depends on $Y$ (Simultaneity Bias).

- _Example:_ Supply depends on Price, but Price depends on Supply.
    
- **Solution:** We cannot use a single equation. We use **Simultaneous Equation Models (SEM)** or Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) to estimate the feedback loop correctly.
    

#### 6. Locally Weighted Regression (LOESS/LOWESS)

This is a **Non-parametric** regression method.

- Instead of fitting one straight global line through all data, it fits many small local lines to subsets of the data.
    
- _Result:_ A smooth curve that hugs the data closely, revealing complex non-linear trends that OLS would miss.
    

#### 7. Under-adjustment Bias in Employment Regressions

This is a specific application of "Omitted Variable Bias" in econometrics (often discussed in labor statistics).

- **The Scenario:** When analyzing discrimination (e.g., Gender Pay Gap), researchers "adjust" for job level (e.g., comparing men and women _at the same managerial level_).
    
- **The Bias:** If the discrimination itself prevents women from reaching high levels, adjusting for "job level" hides the discrimination mechanism. The regression "under-adjusts" or improperly controls for a variable that is part of the causal chain, leading to a biased coefficient for gender.

### Part III: Validation and Resampling

How do we know our complex model is stable? We use computer simulation.

#### 8. Jack Knife

An older resampling technique used to reduce bias and estimate standard errors.

- **Method:** "Leave-One-Out." If you have $n$ observations, you calculate the statistic $n$ times, each time removing exactly one observation.
    
- _Purpose:_ It checks if a single outlier is driving your entire result.
    

#### 9. Cross-Validation

The gold standard for checking predictive power (Machine Learning).

- **K-Fold Cross-Validation:**
    
    1. Split data into $K$ groups (e.g., 5 folds).
        
    2. Train the model on 4 folds.
        
    3. Test it on the 5th (unseen) fold.
        
    4. Repeat until every fold has been used as a test set.
        
- _Goal:_ Prevents **Overfitting** (fitting the noise rather than the signal).
    

#### 10. Bootstrap

A powerful modern simulation method.

- **Method:** "Sampling with Replacement." We create thousands of "fake datasets" by randomly picking rows from our original dataset (some rows picked twice, some not at all). We run the regression on all these fake datasets.
    
- _Result:_ This gives us an empirical distribution of the coefficients, allowing us to calculate Confidence Intervals even when the theoretical math is too difficult or assumptions (like Normality) are violated.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Overfitting Warning:** In complex models (especially with interaction terms or polynomials), you can get a very high $R^2$ (e.g., 0.99), but the model might be useless for prediction. Always prefer a simpler model (Parsimony) unless Cross-Validation proves the complex one is better.
    
2. **Logit vs. Probit:** In exams, if asked "Which one should I use?", the answer is usually: "It doesn't matter much, they produce similar results. Logit is easier to interpret (Odds Ratios); Probit is standard in Normal-theory contexts."
    
3. **Poisson Assumption:** Remember that for a Poisson distribution, Mean = Variance. If your count data has "Clumping" (lots of zeros, or massive outliers), Variance > Mean. You **must** switch to Negative Binomial, or your P-values will be falsely optimistic (Type I Error).
