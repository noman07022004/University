# 15.9 Jack Knife ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 3/5):** The Jackknife is the grandfather of modern resampling methods (like the Bootstrap). It uses a brute-force approach: "What would happen to my result if I deleted this one person?" It is conceptually simple but computationally repetitive.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 2/5):** You will likely not be asked to perform a full Jackknife calculation by hand for a large dataset. Exam questions typically focus on the **Logic** (Leave-one-out) or comparing it to the Bootstrap.
    

---

### **1. Definition and Purpose**

The **Jackknife** (or Jackknife Estimation) is a resampling technique used to estimate the **Bias** and **Variance** of a statistic.

- **The Problem:** Standard formulas for Standard Error (e.g., $s / \sqrt{n}$) work great for the Mean. But what is the formula for the Standard Error of a _Correlation Coefficient_? Or a _Ratio_? Or a _Regression Slope_? Often, no simple formula exists.
    
- **The Solution:** Instead of using a formula, we use the computer to re-calculate the statistic many times by systematically modifying the dataset.
    
- **Origin:** Invented by Maurice Quenouille (1949) and named by John Tukey (1958) because, like a boy scout's jackknife, it is a rough-and-ready tool that can do many things, even if it's not the perfect tool for any single one.
    

---

### **2. The "Leave-One-Out" Procedure**

The Jackknife relies on a deterministic process of deleting one observation at a time.

Step 1: Calculate the Original Statistic ($\hat{\theta}$)

Calculate your metric (e.g., the mean or correlation) using the full dataset of size $n$.

Step 2: Create Jackknife Samples

Create $n$ new datasets, each of size $n-1$, by removing exactly one observation.

- Sample 1: Remove observation $x_1$.
    
- Sample 2: Remove observation $x_2$.
    
- ...
    
- Sample $n$: Remove observation $x_n$.
    

Step 3: Re-calculate ($\hat{\theta}_{(i)}$)

Calculate the statistic for each of these $n$ reduced samples. These are called Partial Estimates.

---

### **3. Estimating Bias and Variance**

Once we have the list of partial estimates ($\hat{\theta}_{(1)}, \hat{\theta}_{(2)}, ...$), we can quantify the uncertainty.

#### **A. Bias Estimation**

Many statistics are biased (e.g., the sample variance divides by $n$ instead of $n-1$, underestimating the truth). The Jackknife can detect and fix this.

$$Bias_{jack} = (n-1) (\bar{\theta}_{jack} - \hat{\theta})$$

- Where $\bar{\theta}_{jack}$ is the average of all the partial estimates.
    

#### **B. Standard Error Estimation**

The Jackknife Standard Error tells us how stable our result is. If removing one person changes the result drastically, the SE will be high.

$$SE_{jack} = \sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n} \sum (\hat{\theta}_{(i)} - \bar{\theta}_{jack})^2}$$

---

### **4. Jackknife vs. Bootstrap**

This is the most common comparison in Section B of the syllabus.

|**Feature**|**Jackknife**|**Bootstrap (Subtopic 15.10)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Method**|**Leave-One-Out**. Systematic and deterministic.|**Random Sampling with Replacement**. Stochastic (random).|
|**Sample Size**|Each new sample is size **$n-1$**.|Each new sample is size **$n$**.|
|**Iterations**|Exactly **$n$** samples (one for each data point).|Usually **1,000+** samples (user defined).|
|**Performance**|Fails for non-smooth statistics (like the **Median**).|Works well for almost everything, including Medians.|

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The "Median" Failure:** The Jackknife is notorious for failing when trying to estimate the error of a **Median**. Because the median depends only on the middle 1-2 numbers, removing an outlier (leave-one-out) changes nothing, leading the Jackknife to wrongly conclude the variance is zero.
    
- **Influence:** The Jackknife is essentially measuring the **Influence** of each individual data point. If $\hat{\theta}_{(i)}$ is very different from $\hat{\theta}$, it means observation $i$ is an outlier or influential point.
