# 15.3 Locally Weighted Regression (LOESS/LOWESS) ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 3/5):** This method abandons the idea of finding _one_ single equation to describe the entire universe of your data. Instead, it adopts a "neighbors only" approach, fitting thousands of tiny, temporary regressions. It bridges the gap between Scatterplots and formal Regression.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 2/5):** You will almost never be asked to perform this calculation by hand (it is computationally massive). Exam questions usually ask you to explain the difference between **Global** (OLS) and **Local** (LOESS) methods or determining how the "Bandwidth" parameter changes the curve's smoothness.
    

---

### **1. The Problem with Global Models**

Standard Linear Regression (OLS) is a **Global** model.

- It assumes that the relationship between $X$ and $Y$ is the exact same everywhere (a straight line from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$).
    
- **Flaw:** What if the relationship changes? For example, "Income vs. Happiness." Money increases happiness for poor people (steep slope), but has little effect on rich people (flat slope). A single straight line would miss this nuance.
    

### **2. The Solution: Local Fitting**

**Locally Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing (LOWESS or LOESS)** fits the data by breaking it into many small segments.

- **The Logic:** To predict the value of $Y$ at a specific point $x_0$, the model looks **only** at the data points nearby (the neighbors) and ignores points far away.
    
- **The Process:**
    
    1. Select a target point on the X-axis ($x_0$).
        
    2. Identify the $k$ nearest data points (neighbors).
        
    3. Assign **Weights** to these neighbors:
        
        - Points closest to $x_0$ get high weight.
            
        - Points at the edge of the neighborhood get low weight.
            
    4. Run a weighted linear regression using _only_ these points to find the predicted $y$ for that specific $x_0$.
        
    5. **Repeat** this entire process for every single point in the dataset.
        

---

### **3. The Smoothing Parameter (Bandwidth)**

The most critical decision in Locally Weighted Regression is choosing the **Bandwidth** (often denoted as $\alpha$, $f$, or $k$). This determines how many neighbors are included in each local calculation.

- **Small Bandwidth (e.g., 5% of data):**
    
    - The model looks at very few neighbors.
        
    - **Result:** The curve is very "wiggly" and follows every random fluctuation in the data.
        
    - **Risk:** Overfitting (modeling the noise).
        
- **Large Bandwidth (e.g., 50% of data):**
    
    - The model looks at half the dataset at once.
        
    - **Result:** The curve becomes smoother and straighter, approaching a global linear regression line.
        
    - **Risk:** Underfitting (missing the real curvature).
        

---

### **4. Weights: The Kernel Function**

How do we decide how much "say" a neighbor gets? We use a **Kernel Function** (usually the **Tricube** weight function).

$$w_i = (1 - |d|^3)^3$$

- **Center:** The exact point we are predicting gets maximum weight (1).
    
- **Edge:** The furthest neighbor in the bandwidth gets zero weight (0).
    
- **Meaning:** This ensures the transition between segments is smooth, creating a continuous, flowing line rather than a jagged, piecewise one.
    

---

### **5. Pros and Cons**

|**Feature**|**Global Regression (OLS)**|**Locally Weighted (LOESS)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Equation**|Simple ($y = mx + b$). Easy to write down.|None. The "model" is the entire dataset.|
|**Shape**|Rigid (Linear/Parametric).|Flexible (Non-parametric).|
|**Computation**|Fast (calculated once).|Slow (re-calculated for every point).|
|**Usage**|Hypothesis Testing & Prediction.|Visual Exploration & Trend Discovery.|

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **"Lazy Learning":** LOESS is often called a "lazy" learning method because it doesn't learn a fixed formula. It waits until you ask for a prediction at point $x$, and _then_ it does the work of looking up neighbors.
    
- **No P-Values:** Because there is no single slope coefficient $\beta$, you generally do not get a P-value or a simple interpretation like "Slope = 5." This makes it less useful for proving causality and more useful for **Descriptive Statistics**.
