# 15.6 Logit and Probit Regression ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the definitive solution for binary outcomes (Yes/No, Win/Lose, Sick/Healthy). Since many real-world questions are binary, this is one of the most practical tools in a statistician's kit.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** You will frequently be asked to interpret the coefficients. Unlike OLS, where "Slope = Change in Y," here you must convert the slope into an **Odds Ratio** to make sense of it.
    

---

### **1. The Problem with Linear Regression (LPM)**

If our dependent variable $Y$ is binary ($0$ or $1$), we technically could run a standard OLS line. This is called a Linear Probability Model (LPM).

However, it fails for three mathematical reasons:

1. **Unbounded Predictions:** The line extends to infinity, eventually predicting probabilities like $1.2$ or $-0.4$, which are impossible.
    
2. **Heteroscedasticity:** The variance of a binary variable depends on its mean ($p(1-p)$). Since probabilities change as X changes, the variance cannot be constant.
    
3. **Non-Linearity:** Real-world probability usually follows an "S-curve" (diminishing returns near 0 and 1), not a straight line.
    

---

### **2. The Solution: Link Functions**

To solve this, we transform the $Y$ variable using a **Link Function** ($G(z)$) that squashes any input into the range $[0, 1]$.

$$P(Y=1) = G(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X)$$

Depending on which function $G(\cdot)$ we choose, we get a different model:

- **Logistic Function** $\rightarrow$ **Logit Model**.
    
- **Normal CDF** $\rightarrow$ **Probit Model**.
    

---

### **3. Logistic Regression (Logit)**

This is the standard model in health and social sciences because its coefficients are easily interpretable.

- The Function: It uses the Sigmoid (S-shaped) curve.
    
    $$P(Y=1) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X)}}$$
    
- The Log-Odds (Logit):
    
    If we rearrange the algebra, we model the natural log of the odds.
    
    $$\ln \left( \frac{p}{1-p} \right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$$
    
- Interpretation (The Odds Ratio):
    
    The raw coefficient $\beta_1$ is the change in the log-odds. To understand it, we exponentiate it:
    
    $$OR = e^{\beta_1}$$
    
    - **If $\beta_1 = 0.693$, then $OR = e^{0.693} \approx 2.0$.**
        
    - _Sentence:_ "For every 1-unit increase in X, the **odds** of Y occurring double (increase by factor of 2)."
        

---

### **4. Probit Regression**

This model is preferred in econometrics and engineering. It assumes there is a hidden, underlying continuous variable (a **Latent Variable** $Y^*$) that is normally distributed.

- The Function: It uses the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of the Standard Normal Distribution ($\Phi$).
    
    $$P(Y=1) = \Phi(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X)$$
    
- **Interpretation:**
    
    - Probit coefficients represent the change in the **Z-score** of the latent variable.
        
    - This is much harder to explain to a layperson ("Increasing X increases the Z-score by 0.5").
        
    - There is no equivalent to the "Odds Ratio" in Probit.
        

---

### **5. Comparison: Logit vs. Probit**

|**Feature**|**Logit**|**Probit**|
|---|---|---|
|**Distribution**|Logistic Distribution (Fatter tails)|Normal Distribution (Thinner tails)|
|**Math**|Algebraically simple (Odds Ratios).|Integral calculus (Normal CDF).|
|**Field**|Epidemiology, Medicine, Psychology.|Economics, Political Science.|
|**Results**|Nearly identical for mid-range probabilities.|Nearly identical for mid-range probabilities.|

- _Note:_ The choice is usually cultural (what your field prefers) rather than mathematical.
    

---

### **6. Evaluating Model Fit**

Since we cannot use $R^2$ (which relies on sums of squares), we use alternatives:

1. **Pseudo-$R^2$ (e.g., McFadden's):** Compares the likelihood of your model to a null model. Values are typically low ($0.2$–$0.4$ is considered excellent).
    
2. **Percent Correctly Classified:** We check if the model correctly predicts 1s as 1s and 0s as 0s.
    
3. **Deviance / Likelihood Ratio Test:** A "Goodness of Fit" test using the Chi-squared distribution.
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The "S" Shape:** Both models produce an S-curve. This captures the reality that changing a predictor has a huge effect in the middle (probability 0.5) but very little effect at the extremes (probability 0.99 or 0.01).
    
- **Coefficient Scaling:** Probit coefficients are usually about **$\frac{1}{1.6}$** times the size of Logit coefficients because the Logistic distribution has a variance of $\pi^2/3$ while Normal has variance 1.
    
- **Maximum Likelihood:** Remember Subtopic 5.6? Both of these models are solved using **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)**, not Least Squares. There is no simple formula; the computer must solve it iteratively.
