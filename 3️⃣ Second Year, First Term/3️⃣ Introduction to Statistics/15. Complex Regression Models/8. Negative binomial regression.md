# 15.8 Negative Binomial Regression ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 3/5):** This model is the "Plan B" for Poisson regression. It is mathematically slightly more complex because it adds an extra parameter to handle the variance, but conceptualizing it as "Poisson + Noise" makes it approachable.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** Questions here almost always ask: _"Why did we choose Negative Binomial instead of Poisson?"_ The answer is always **Overdispersion**.
    

---

### **1. The Problem: Overdispersion**

As discussed in Subtopic 15.7, the Poisson model is rigid. It assumes the Mean equals the Variance ($\mu = \sigma^2$).

However, in real-world count data (e.g., "Number of days absent from school"), the data is often Overdispersed: the variance is much larger than the mean ($\sigma^2 > \mu$).

- **Causes:**
    
    1. **Clustering:** Events happen in bunches (e.g., if you are sick today, you are likely sick tomorrow).
        
    2. **Heterogeneity:** The population is not uniform. Some people are inherently "high risk" in ways our variables ($X$) didn't capture.
        
- **Consequence of ignoring it:** If you use Poisson on overdispersed data, your standard errors will be artificially tiny, leading you to claim "Significance" where none exists (Type I Error).
    

---

### **2. The Negative Binomial Solution**

The Negative Binomial (NB) model is a generalization of the Poisson model that **relaxes** the assumption that mean equals variance.

- Conceptual View:
    
    It assumes that the individual subject's rate ($\lambda$) is not fixed but is itself a random variable (Gamma distributed). It creates a "mixture" model:
    
    $$Count \sim Poisson(\lambda)$$
    
    $$\lambda \sim Gamma(mean, noise)$$
    
- The Variance Formula:
    
    While Poisson says $Var = \mu$, Negative Binomial says:
    
    $$Var(Y) = \mu + \alpha \mu^2$$
    
    - **$\mu$:** The expected mean (same as Poisson).
        
    - **$\alpha$ (Alpha):** The **Dispersion Parameter**.
        
        - If $\alpha \to 0$, the term $\alpha \mu^2$ disappears, and the model collapses back into a standard Poisson.
            
        - If $\alpha > 0$, the variance is allowed to be larger than the mean.
            

---

### **3. Interpretation**

The interpretation of the coefficients ($\beta$) in Negative Binomial regression is **identical** to Poisson regression.

- **Log-Link:** $\ln(\mu) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$
    
- **Incidence Rate Ratio (IRR):** $IRR = e^{\beta}$
    
- **Sentence:** "For every 1-unit increase in X, the expected count increases by a factor of [IRR], holding other variables constant."
    

The only difference is in the _significance_ (P-values). Because the NB model admits more noise (higher variance), the confidence intervals will be wider, making it "harder" (but more honest) to achieve statistical significance.

---

### **4. Testing for Overdispersion**

How do you know which model to use? You run a formal test.

1. **Run the Negative Binomial Model.**
    
2. **Check the Alpha ($\alpha$):** Most software outputs the estimate for the dispersion parameter.
    
3. **Likelihood Ratio Test:**
    
    - $H_0$: $\alpha = 0$ (The data is Poisson).
        
    - $H_1$: $\alpha > 0$ (The data is Negative Binomial).
        
    - _Decision:_ If the test is significant ($P < 0.05$), you **must** use Negative Binomial. If not, you can stick with Poisson.
        

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Why the name?** It is called "Negative Binomial" because the mathematical formula involves combinatorial terms similar to the Binomial theorem but with negative exponents. Historically, it modeled "the number of failures before the $r$-th success" in a sequence of coin flips.
    
- **Zero-Inflation:** Just like Poisson, NB models can suffer from "too many zeros." In that case, you use **ZINB (Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial)** regression.
    
- **The "Variance > Mean" Rule:** In exams, if you see summary statistics where Mean = 5 and Variance = 25, immediately state: _"This indicates overdispersion; a Negative Binomial model is preferred over Poisson."_
