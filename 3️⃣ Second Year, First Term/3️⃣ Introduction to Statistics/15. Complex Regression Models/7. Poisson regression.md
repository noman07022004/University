# 15.7 Poisson Regression ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Standard linear regression works for continuous numbers (height, salary). Logistic regression works for binary choices (Yes/No). But what about **Count Data** (e.g., number of arrests, number of emails, number of heart attacks)? Poisson regression is the specialized tool for modeling these non-negative integers.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** Questions typically focus on the **interpretation** of the coefficients (using Rate Ratios) or identifying **Overdispersion** (when the variance is too high for the Poisson model).
    

---

### **1. The Problem with OLS for Counts**

If our dependent variable $Y$ is a count ($0, 1, 2, 3...$), using OLS Linear Regression ($Y = \beta X$) fails for three reasons:

1. **Predicated Negatives:** OLS might predict -2.5 arrests, which is impossible.
    
2. **Non-Normality:** Count data is usually right-skewed with many zeros, violating the Normality assumption.
    
3. **Heteroscedasticity:** In count data, the variance usually increases as the mean increases (people who tweet 100 times have more variance than people who tweet 1 time). OLS assumes constant variance.
    

---

### **2. The Poisson Model**

We assume the outcome $Y$ follows a **Poisson Distribution** (from Topic 4.7).

- Key Property: For a Poisson distribution, the Mean equals the Variance.
    
    $$E[Y] = Var(Y) = \lambda$$
    
- The Link Function: We model the Natural Log of the expected count ($\lambda$). This ensures predictions are always positive (since $e^x$ is always positive).
    
    $$\ln(\lambda) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + ... + \beta_k X_k$$
    
- The Equation (Exponentiated):
    
    $$\lambda = e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 X}$$
    

---

### **3. Interpretation: Incidence Rate Ratios (IRR)**

Like Logistic Regression, the raw coefficients ($\beta$) are logs, which are hard to interpret. We exponentiate them to get a ratio.

- **Calculation:** $IRR = e^{\beta_1}$.
    
- **Interpretation:**
    
    - **If $\beta_1 > 0$ ($IRR > 1$):** The predictor increases the rate of events.
        
    - **If $\beta_1 < 0$ ($IRR < 1$):** The predictor decreases the rate.
        
- **Example:** Predicting "Number of Hospital Visits" based on "Age".
    
    - If $\beta_{age} = 0.05$, then $IRR = e^{0.05} \approx 1.05$.
        
    - _Sentence:_ "For every additional year of age, the rate of hospital visits increases by a factor of 1.05 (or increases by 5%)."
        

---

### **4. Handling "Exposure" (The Offset)**

Sometimes counts depend on time.

- _Person A:_ 5 heart attacks in 1 year.
    
- _Person B:_ 5 heart attacks in 100 years.
    
- These are not the same! We must account for the **Exposure Time**.
    

We add the log of time ($\ln(t)$) as an Offset variable (a predictor with a coefficient fixed at 1).

$$\ln(\lambda) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \ln(\text{Time})$$

This effectively turns the model from modeling "Counts" to modeling "Rates" (Counts per unit of time).

---

### **5. The Major Flaw: Overdispersion**

The strict assumption of Poisson is that Mean = Variance.

In the real world, data is almost always "messier" than this. The variance is usually larger than the mean (e.g., many people have 0 events, but a few "super-users" have 1,000 events).

- **Condition:** If $Variance > Mean$, we have **Overdispersion**.
    
- **Consequence:** The standard errors in Poisson regression will be too small, leading to significant P-values for variables that are not actually significant (Type I Error).
    
- **Solution:** Use **Negative Binomial Regression** (Subtopic 15.8).
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Zero-Inflation:** Another common problem is having "too many zeros" (more than the Poisson math predicts).
    
    - _Example:_ "Number of Cigarettes Smoked." Non-smokers always have 0 (Structural Zeros). Smokers might have 0 just by chance today (Sampling Zeros).
        
    - _Fix:_ Use a **Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP)** model, which combines a Logistic model (Smoker vs Non-Smoker) with a Poisson model (Count of cigarettes).
        
- **Continuous vs. Count:** If the count is massive (e.g., "Number of white blood cells" = 5,000+), the distribution looks Normal, and you can often get away with standard OLS regression. Poisson is most critical for small counts (0 to 20).
