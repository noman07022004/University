### Introduction

In Section A, we focused largely on proportions (categorical data). In Section B, we shift to **Numerical Data** (continuous variables like height, weight, salary, time). The most fundamental question here is: _"Is the average value of Group A significantly different from Group B?"_

If we know the population variance ($\sigma^2$), we use the Z-test (covered in Topic 4/7). However, in real research, we almost never know the true population variance; we only have the sample variance ($s^2$). This uncertainty necessitates the use of the **Student's t-distribution**. When we expand our comparison to _more_ than two groups, we must switch methods entirely to the **Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)** to avoid the multiple testing errors discussed in Topic 6.

---

### 1. Student's T-Test

This is the standard parametric test for comparing the means of **two** groups when the population standard deviation is unknown.

- **The T-Distribution:**
    
    - Like the Normal distribution (Z), it is bell-shaped and symmetric and centered at zero.
        
    - _Difference:_ It has "fatter tails" (more area in the extremes). This extra width accounts for the extra uncertainty introduced by estimating $\sigma$ with $s$.
        
    - As the sample size ($n$) increases, the T-distribution becomes identical to the Normal Z-distribution.

- **Types of T-Tests:**
    
    1. **One-Sample T-Test:** Compares one sample mean to a known standard (e.g., "Is the average IQ of this class different from 100?").
        
    2. Independent Samples T-Test: Compares the means of two unrelated groups (e.g., Men vs. Women).
        
        $$t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$
        
        (Note: If variances are assumed equal, we use a "pooled" variance formula).
        
    3. **Paired Samples T-Test:** Compares means where data points are linked (e.g., Before vs. After treatment for the _same_ person). This is mathematically equivalent to a One-Sample T-Test on the _difference_ scores ($d$).
        
- Degrees of Freedom ($df$):
    
    For a single sample, $df = n-1$. For two independent samples, $df = n_1 + n_2 - 2$.
    

---

### 2. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) for Comparing Several Means

When we need to compare **three or more** groups (e.g., Drug A, Drug B, Drug C), we cannot simply run three separate T-tests (A vs B, B vs C, A vs C) because this inflates the Type I error rate (as discussed in Topic 6). Instead, we use ANOVA.

- The Logic:
    
    ANOVA asks a clever question: "Is the variation between the groups significantly larger than the variation within the groups?"
    
    - **Signal:** Variance Between Groups (caused by the treatment).
        
    - **Noise:** Variance Within Groups (random error/natural variation).
        
    - If Signal > Noise, the groups are different.
        
- The F-Test:
    
    The test statistic for ANOVA is F (named after Ronald Fisher).
    
    $$F = \frac{\text{Between-Group Variability}}{\text{Within-Group Variability}} = \frac{MS_{between}}{MS_{within}}$$
    
    - **$MS$ (Mean Square):** This is the Sum of Squares ($SS$) divided by the degrees of freedom ($df$).
        
    - **Interpretation:** If $F$ is large (typically > 3 or 4, depending on the table), the means are likely different.
        
- The ANOVA Table:
    
    In exams, you are often given a partially filled table and asked to complete it.
    
    - **Sum of Squares Total ($SST$):** Total variation in the data.
        
    - **Sum of Squares Between ($SSB$):** Variation due to the factor (treatment).
        
    - **Sum of Squares Error ($SSE$):** Variation due to random error.
        
    - _Equation:_ $SST = SSB + SSE$.
        
- Post-Hoc Tests:
    
    A significant ANOVA result only tells us "At least one group is different." It does not say which one. To find out, we run post-hoc tests (like Tukey's HSD or Bonferroni), which correct for multiple comparisons.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Assumptions are Critical:** Both T-tests and ANOVA rely on three key assumptions. In essay questions, listing these earns marks:
    
    - **Normality:** The data in each group follows a normal distribution.
        
    - **Independence:** Observations are independent of each other.
        
    - **Homogeneity of Variance (Homoscedasticity):** The variance ($s^2$) in each group is roughly the same. (Check this with Levene's Test).
        
2. **One-Tailed vs. Two-Tailed:**
    
    - T-tests can be one-tailed (Testing if Mean A > Mean B).
        
    - **ANOVA is always one-tailed (Right-tailed):** We only care if the F-ratio is _large_. An F-ratio near 0 implies the groups are _too_ similar (which usually suggests data fabrication), but for hypothesis testing, we only look for large positive F values.
        
3. Relationship:
    
    $F = t^2$. If you perform an ANOVA on just two groups, the resulting F-statistic will be exactly the square of the t-statistic from a T-test.
