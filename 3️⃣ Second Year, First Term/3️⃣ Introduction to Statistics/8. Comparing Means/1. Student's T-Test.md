### **1. Introduction: The Problem of Unknown Sigma ($\sigma$)**

In Section A (Topics 4 and 7), we used the Z-test to compare means. The formula was:

$$Z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

This formula has a fatal flaw for real-world research: it requires us to know **$\sigma$** (the true Population Standard Deviation). In reality, if we don't know the population mean $\mu$ (which is why we are testing!), we almost certainly don't know $\sigma$ either.

The Solution: We estimate $\sigma$ using the Sample Standard Deviation ($s$).

However, replacing $\sigma$ with $s$ introduces extra uncertainty. To account for this "error on top of error," we cannot use the Normal Distribution. We must use the Student's t-distribution.

---

### **2. The Student's t-Distribution**

Published by William Sealy Gosset in 1908 under the pseudonym "Student," this distribution is the theoretical foundation for small-sample inference.

- **Properties:**
    
    1. **Bell-Shaped and Symmetric:** Like the Normal curve, it is centered at 0.
        
    2. **Fatter Tails (Platykurtic):** The tails are higher/thicker than the Normal distribution. This reflects the higher probability of extreme values when sample sizes are small.
        
    3. **Defined by Degrees of Freedom ($df$):** The shape changes based on sample size.
        
        - As $n$ increases ($df \to \infty$), the t-distribution converges to the **Standard Normal (Z) Distribution**.
            

---

### **3. Types of T-Tests**

There are three specific versions of the T-test, each designed for a different study design.

#### **A. One-Sample T-Test**

Tests if a single group mean is different from a known standard (hypothesized population mean).

- **Hypothesis:** $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$
    
- Formula:
    
    $$t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}$$
    
- **Degrees of Freedom:** $df = n - 1$.
    

#### **B. Independent Samples T-Test (The most common)**

Tests if the means of two **unrelated** groups are different (e.g., Treatment Group vs. Control Group).

- **Hypothesis:** $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ (or $\mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0$).
    
- Formula (Assuming Equal Variance):
    
    $$t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - 0}{s_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}$$
    
    - Where $s_p$ is the Pooled Standard Deviation:
        
        $$s_p = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}}$$
        
- **Degrees of Freedom:** $df = n_1 + n_2 - 2$.
    

#### **C. Paired Samples T-Test (Dependent)**

Tests if the means of two **related** measurements are different (e.g., Before vs. After for the _same_ person).

- **Method:** We calculate the **Difference ($d$)** for each pair ($d = x_{after} - x_{before}$) and run a One-Sample T-test on the differences.
    
- Formula:
    
    $$t = \frac{\bar{d} - 0}{s_d / \sqrt{n}}$$
    
- **Degrees of Freedom:** $df = n_{pairs} - 1$.
    

---

### **4. Assumptions of the T-Test**

To interpret the P-value correctly, the "Robustness" of the test depends on these assumptions:

1. **Normality:** The dependent variable should be approximately normally distributed. (If sample size is large, $n>30$, the CLT relaxes this).
    
2. **Independence:** Observations must be independent of each other.
    
3. **Homogeneity of Variance (Homoscedasticity):** For the Independent Samples test, the variance in Group A should roughly equal the variance in Group B ($s_1^2 \approx s_2^2$).
    
    - _Exam Tip:_ If variances are **not** equal, you must use **Welch’s T-Test** (which uses a different, messier formula for $df$).
        

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The "Zero" Logic:** In the numerator $(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)$, the second term represents the difference predicted by the Null Hypothesis. Since $H_0$ usually predicts "No Difference," this term is almost always **0**.
    
- **One-Tailed vs. Two-Tailed:**
    
    - If $H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2$: Use a Two-Tailed critical value (divide $\alpha$ by 2).
        
    - If $H_1: \mu_1 > \mu_2$: Use a One-Tailed critical value (put all $\alpha$ in one tail).
        
- **Relationship to F-Test:** If you square the t-statistic from an Independent Samples T-Test, you get the F-statistic for a One-Way ANOVA with two groups ($t^2 = F$).
