# 14.8 Assumptions of the Regression Model ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** You can calculate a regression line for _any_ data, even if it's completely inappropriate (like fitting a straight line to a circle). However, the P-values, Confidence Intervals, and Prediction Intervals (Topics 14.4 & 14.7) are **only valid** if specific assumptions are met.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** A classic exam question involves showing you a "Residual Plot" and asking: _"Which assumption is violated?"_ You must recognize specific patterns like the "Fan Shape" or "U-Shape."
    

---

### **1. The Core Assumptions (The "LINE" Acronym)**

For the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model to be valid, four key conditions regarding the **Errors (Residuals)** must be met. We remember them using the acronym **LINE**.

#### **L - Linearity**

- **Assumption:** The relationship between $X$ and the mean of $Y$ is linear. The errors have a mean of zero.
    
- **Violation:** The data is curved (e.g., quadratic or exponential).
    
- **Diagnostic:** Look at a scatterplot of **Residuals vs. Predicted Values**.
    
    - _Good:_ Random scatter around 0.
        
    - _Bad:_ A U-shape or inverted U-shape.
        

#### **I - Independence**

- **Assumption:** The errors are independent of each other. Knowing one error tells you nothing about the next one.
    
- **Violation:** **Autocorrelation**. Common in time-series data (e.g., stock prices today are related to stock prices yesterday).
    
- **Diagnostic:** The **Durbin-Watson Test**. (Values near 2 are good; values near 0 or 4 imply dependence).
    

#### **N - Normality**

- **Assumption:** The errors ($\epsilon$) are normally distributed at any given value of $X$.
    
- **Violation:** Skewed errors or heavy outliers.
    
- **Diagnostic:** A **Q-Q Plot** of residuals or a Histogram of residuals.
    
    - _Note:_ This assumption is less critical for large sample sizes due to the Central Limit Theorem.
        

#### **E - Equal Variance (Homoscedasticity)**

- **Assumption:** The variance of the errors is constant across all values of $X$. The "spread" or "noise" should be the same for small $X$ and large $X$.
    
- **Violation:** **Heteroscedasticity**.
    
- **Diagnostic:** Look at the **Residual Plot**.
    
    - _Good:_ A uniform rectangular band of dots.
        
    - _Bad:_ A **"Fan Shape"** or "Cone Shape" (dots fanning out as you move right).
        

---

### **2. Visualizing Diagnostics (The Residual Plot)**

The most important tool for checking these assumptions is the **Residual Plot**.

- **X-axis:** Predicted Values ($\hat{y}$) or the Independent Variable ($x$).
    
- **Y-axis:** Residuals ($e = y - \hat{y}$).
    

|**Pattern**|**Diagnosis**|**Assumption Violated**|
|---|---|---|
|Random Cloud (No pattern)|Model is Good|None|
|Curved (U-Shape)|Missing a term (need $x^2$)|**L**inearity|
|Fan Shape (Cone)|Variance changes with X|**E**qual Variance|
|Sine Wave|Seasonal/Time effect|**I**ndependence|

---

### **3. Consequences of Violations**

What happens if you ignore these rules?

1. **If Linearity is violated:** Your predictions ($\hat{y}$) will be biased (systematically wrong).
    
2. **If Homoscedasticity is violated:** Your predictions ($\hat{y}$) are still unbiased, but your **Standard Errors ($S_e$)** are wrong. This makes your T-statistics and P-values invalid (you might claim significance when there is none).
    
3. **If Independence is violated:** Your sample size effectively looks larger than it actually is, leading to artificially tiny P-values (False Positives).
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Residuals, not Raw Data:** A common misconception is that the _Dependent Variable Y_ must be normally distributed. This is false. Only the **Residuals** (the error term) need to be normal.
    
- **Robustness:** Regression is quite "robust" to violations of Normality (it still works okay), but it is very sensitive to **Outliers** and **Non-Linearity**.
    
- **The "Fan" Logic:** Heteroscedasticity (The Fan) is common in financial data. For example, predicting "Spending" based on "Income." Poor people have low variance (they _must_ spend to survive), while rich people have high variance (they can spend a little or a lot).
