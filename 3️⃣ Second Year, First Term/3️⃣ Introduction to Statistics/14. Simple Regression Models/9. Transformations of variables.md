# 14.9 Transformations of Variables ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 3/5):** When the "LINE" assumptions (Topic 14.8) are violated—specifically Linearity or Homoscedasticity—we don't just throw away the data. Instead, we use mathematical transformations to "fix" the data shape so that OLS regression can still be used.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** You are unlikely to be asked to perform a Box-Cox transformation by hand. However, you will frequently be asked to interpret the meaning of a coefficient in a **Log-Log** or **Semi-Log** model (e.g., elasticity).
    

---

### **1. Why Transform?**

Linear Regression assumes a straight-line relationship ($Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$). If the true relationship is curved (e.g., exponential growth, diminishing returns) or if the variance expands (heteroscedasticity), the standard model fails.

**Transformations** (applying a function like $\ln$, $\sqrt{}$, or $1/x$) can:

1. **Linearize the relationship:** Make a curve look straight.
    
2. **Stabilize Variance:** Shrink large outliers to fix heteroscedasticity.
    
3. **Normalize Errors:** Fix skewness in the residuals.
    

---

### **2. Common Logarithmic Transformations**

The most common transformation in statistics and economics is the **Natural Logarithm ($\ln$)**. This creates three special types of regression models with unique interpretations.

#### **A. Log-Level (Semi-Log) Model**

- **Equation:** $\ln(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$
    
- **Scenario:** $Y$ grows exponentially (e.g., Salary) based on a linear $X$ (e.g., Years of Experience).
    
- **Interpretation of $\beta_1$:**
    
    - A 1-unit increase in $X$ leads to a **$(\beta_1 \times 100)\%$** change in $Y$.
        
    - _Example:_ If $\beta_1 = 0.05$, then "One extra year of experience increases salary by approximately 5%."
        

#### **B. Level-Log Model**

- **Equation:** $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(x)$
    
- **Scenario:** $Y$ grows rapidly at first but slows down (diminishing returns). Example: Test Score ($Y$) vs. Study Hours ($X$). The first hour helps a lot; the 10th hour helps less.
    
- **Interpretation of $\beta_1$:**
    
    - A 1% increase in $X$ leads to a **$(\beta_1 / 100)$** unit change in $Y$.
        
    - _Example:_ If $\beta_1 = 10$, then "A 1% increase in study hours leads to a 0.1 point increase in score."
        

#### **C. Log-Log (Double Log) Model**

- **Equation:** $\ln(y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(x)$
    
- **Scenario:** Elasticity (Economics). Demand vs. Price.
    
- **Interpretation of $\beta_1$:**
    
    - A 1% increase in $X$ leads to a **$\beta_1\%$** change in $Y$.
        
    - This coefficient $\beta_1$ is literally the **Elasticity**.
        

---

### **3. Ladder of Powers (Tukey's Ladder)**

If logs don't work, statisticians look at the "Ladder of Powers" to decide which exponent ($p$) to use ($y^p$).

|**Power (p)**|**Transformation**|**Use Case**|
|---|---|---|
|**2**|$y^2$|U-shaped data (parabolic).|
|**1**|$y$|No transformation (Linear).|
|**0.5**|$\sqrt{y}$|Count data (Poisson) to stabilize variance.|
|**0**|$\ln(y)$|Exponential growth or Heteroscedasticity.|
|**-1**|$1/y$|Rates (e.g., Speed vs. Time).|

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The Back-Transformation Trap:** If you predict $\ln(y) = 5$, your prediction for $y$ is $e^5 \approx 148$. However, simply exponentiating the prediction introduces a small bias (geometric mean vs arithmetic mean). In introductory courses, this is usually ignored, but be aware of it.
    
- **Can't Log Zero:** You cannot take the logarithm of zero or negative numbers. If your data contains 0s (e.g., Income = 0), statisticians often use $\ln(y + 1)$ to avoid error messages.
    
- **Polynomials:** Another common "transformation" is simply adding an $x^2$ term to the model ($y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2$). This fits a U-shape parabola.
    
    - _Exam Tip:_ If the coefficient $\beta_2$ is significant, the relationship is non-linear.
