- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is guaranteed to appear. You will almost certainly be given a computer software printout (like from SPSS, Stata, or R) and asked to interpret the coefficients, the $R^2$, and the p-values. You may also be asked to calculate a prediction interval.

### Introduction

**Simple Linear Regression** is a statistical method that allows us to summarize and study relationships between two continuous (quantitative) variables:

- **Dependent Variable ($Y$):** The outcome we want to explain (e.g., Salary).
    
- **Independent Variable ($X$):** The explanatory factor (e.g., Years of Experience).
    

Unlike Correlation, which only measures the _strength_ of association, Regression quantifies the _nature_ of the relationship mathematically, allowing for prediction.

The theoretical population model is:

$$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_i + \epsilon_i$$

Where $\epsilon_i$ is the random error term (residuals).

---

### 1. Estimating and Interpreting Coefficients

Since we cannot measure the entire population, we estimate the parameters ($\beta_0, \beta_1$) using sample data to produce the Prediction Equation:

$$\hat{Y} = b_0 + b_1 X$$

- Method of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS):
    
    We choose the line that minimizes the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE). That is, we find the line where the vertical distance between the actual data points and the predicted line is as small as possible.
    
- Interpreting the Slope ($b_1$):
    
    This is the most critical sentence in regression analysis.
    
    - _Definition:_ The estimated change in the mean value of $Y$ for every **one-unit increase** in $X$.
        
    - _Example:_ If $\hat{Y} = 100 + 20X$ (where $Y$=Sales, $X$=Ad Spend), then "For every additional $1 spent on ads, Sales increase by $20 on average."
        
- Interpreting the Intercept ($b_0$):
    
    The predicted value of $Y$ when $X = 0$.
    
    - _Warning:_ This often has no physical meaning (e.g., if $X$ is height and $Y$ is weight, $X=0$ implies a person with zero height).
        

---

### 2. Measures of Indeterminacy for the Regression Equation

Once we fit a line, we must ask: _"How well does this line actually fit the data?"_

- Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):
    
    The proportion of the variance in the dependent variable ($Y$) that is predictable from the independent variable ($X$).
    
    $$R^2 = \frac{\text{Explained Variation (SSR)}}{\text{Total Variation (SST)}} = 1 - \frac{\text{Unexplained Variation (SSE)}}{\text{Total Variation (SST)}}$$
    
    - _Range:_ $0 \le R^2 \le 1$.
        
    - _Interpretation:_ If $R^2 = 0.80$, then "80% of the variation in Sales is explained by Advertising Spend."
        
- Standard Error of the Estimate ($S_e$):
    
    A measure of the accuracy of predictions. It represents the standard deviation of the residuals. It tells us roughly how far the data points fall from the regression line on average.
    

---

### 3. Statistical Significance and Reading Computer Printouts

Just because we calculated a slope $b_1$ does not mean the relationship is real. It could be due to sampling noise. We must test the hypothesis.

- **The Hypothesis:**
    
    - $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$ (There is **no** linear relationship; $X$ does not help predict $Y$).
        
    - $H_1: \beta_1 \neq 0$ (There is a significant relationship).
        
- Reading a Regression Printout:
    
    Exam questions typically present a table like this:
    

|**Predictor**|**Coef**|**SE Coef**|**T**|**P**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Constant|12.5|2.0|6.25|0.000|
|Experience|3.4|0.5|**6.80**|**0.000**|

```
* **Coef:** The estimate ($b_0 = 12.5$, $b_1 = 3.4$).
* **SE Coef:** The standard error of the slope ($s_{b_1}$).
* **T-statistic:** Calculated as $t = \frac{b_1 - 0}{SE}$. Here, $3.4 / 0.5 = 6.8$.
* **P-value:** Since $P < 0.05$, we reject $H_0$. "Experience is a significant predictor of the outcome."
```

---

### 4. Confidence and Prediction Intervals

Regression is often used to predict the future. However, a single point estimate ($\hat{Y}$) is rarely exactly correct. We use intervals to express uncertainty.

- Confidence Interval (for the Mean):
    
    Estimates the average $Y$ for all individuals with a specific $X$ value.
    
    - _Example:_ "What is the average weight of **all** men who are 180cm tall?"
        
    - _Width:_ Narrower.
        
- Prediction Interval (for an Individual):
    
    Estimates the $Y$ value for a single specific individual with value $X$.
    
    - _Example:_ "What is the weight of **John**, who is 180cm tall?"
        
    - _Width:_ Wider (because individual behavior is more random than group averages).
        
    - _Formula Note:_ The formula includes an extra "1" under the square root ($\sqrt{1 + \dots}$), accounting for the extra variance of a single observation.
        

---

### 5. Assumptions of the Regression Model

The results of a regression (P-values, Intervals) are only valid if the "LINE" assumptions are met regarding the **Residuals** ($\epsilon$):

1. **L**inearity: The relationship between $X$ and $Y$ is actually linear. (Check: Scatterplot).
    
2. **I**ndependence: Errors are independent of each other (No autocorrelation). Important for time-series data.
    
3. **N**ormality: The errors are normally distributed at each value of $X$. (Check: Histogram of residuals or Q-Q plot).
    
4. **E**qual Variance (Homoscedasticity): The variance of the errors is constant across all values of $X$.
    
    - _Violation:_ **Heteroscedasticity** (The "fan shape" in a residual plot). If variance increases as $X$ increases, standard errors are wrong.
        

---

### 6. Transformations of Variables

If the assumptions above are violated (e.g., the relationship is curved, or variance increases), we cannot use standard SLR. We must transform the data to "force" it into a linear shape.

- **Log Transformation ($\ln Y$):** Used to handle exponential growth or to stabilize variance (fix Heteroscedasticity). Interpretation changes to percentage terms (elasticity).
    
- **Polynomial Transformation ($X^2$):** Used if the relationship is U-shaped (quadratic).
    
- **Reciprocal Transformation ($1/X$):** Used for relationships that approach an asymptote.
    

---

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. Extrapolation Danger:
    
    Never predict a $Y$ value for an $X$ that is outside the range of your original data.
    
    - _Example:_ If you modeled children's height from ages 2 to 10, using the model to predict the height of a 30-year-old will give a ridiculous result (like 3 meters tall).
        
2. **Correlation vs. Slope:**
    
    - **Correlation ($r$):** Unitless. Measures "tightness" of fit.
        
    - **Slope ($b_1$):** Has units (e.g., dollars per year). Measures "steepness."
        
    - _Fact:_ A line can be very steep (high $b_1$) but have low correlation (loose scatter). Conversely, a line can be nearly flat (low $b_1$) but have perfect correlation.
        
3. **Outliers and Leverage:**
    
    - **Outlier:** A point with a large residual (unusual $Y$).
        
    - **High Leverage Point:** A point with an unusual $X$ value (far from the mean of $X$). These points act like a lever and can drastically swing the regression line (Influential Points).
