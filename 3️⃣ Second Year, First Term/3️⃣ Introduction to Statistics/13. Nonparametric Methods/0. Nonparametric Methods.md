### Introduction

Thus far, most tests we have discussed (Z-test, T-test, ANOVA) are **Parametric Tests**. They assume the data follows a specific distribution (usually the Normal Distribution) and estimate parameters like the Mean ($\mu$) and Standard Deviation ($\sigma$).

But what if the data is heavily skewed? Or what if the sample size is too small ($n < 30$) to invoke the Central Limit Theorem? Or what if the data is merely ordinal (ranks) rather than true numbers?

In these cases, we use **Nonparametric Methods** (also called Distribution-Free Tests). Instead of analyzing the raw data values (which might be distorted by outliers), these tests analyze the **Ranks** (order) or **Signs** of the data. They generally test hypotheses about the **Median** rather than the Mean.

---

### 1. Sign Test

This is the simplest and most primitive nonparametric test. It discards almost all information about the magnitude of the data, focusing solely on the _direction_ of the difference.

- **Usage:** It is the nonparametric alternative to the **One-Sample T-Test** or **Paired T-Test**.
    
- The Logic:
    
    If a treatment has no effect, the probability of a "positive" change should be equal to the probability of a "negative" change ($p=0.5$).
    
    - We compare pairs of data (e.g., Before vs. After).
        
    - If After > Before, record a Plus (+).
        
    - If After < Before, record a Minus (-).
        
    - Ties are discarded.
        
- **Testing:** We count the number of pluses ($x$) and treat it as a **Binomial Experiment** with $p=0.5$. If the number of pluses is extremely high or low, we reject the Null Hypothesis.
    
- **Limitation:** Because it ignores _how large_ the differences are, it has very low statistical power.
    

---

### 2. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test

This test improves upon the Sign Test by incorporating the **magnitude** of the differences, making it more powerful. It is the standard nonparametric alternative to the **Paired Samples T-Test**.

- **The Procedure:**
    
    1. Calculate the difference ($d$) for each pair: $x_2 - x_1$.
        
    2. Discard pairs where $d=0$.
        
    3. Rank the absolute values of the differences $|d|$ from smallest to largest.
        
    4. Restore the signs to the ranks (e.g., if the difference was $-5$, and it was the 3rd largest difference, it gets a rank of $-3$).
        
    5. Sum the positive ranks ($W^+$) and negative ranks ($W^-$).
        
- **Test Statistic ($W$):** The smaller of the two sums ($W^+$ or $W^-$) is taken as the test statistic. We compare this to a critical value table.
    
- **Interpretation:** If the treatment had no effect, the sum of positive ranks should roughly equal the sum of negative ranks.
    

---

### 3. Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test

While the previous two tests dealt with _paired_ data, this test is for **Two Independent Samples**. It is the nonparametric equivalent of the **Independent Samples T-Test**.

- **Note:** This test is mathematically equivalent to the **Mann-Whitney U Test**. In exams, these names are often used interchangeably.
    
- **The Procedure:**
    
    1. Combine data from both Group A and Group B into one single list.
        
    2. Rank all values from smallest (1) to largest ($N$).
        
    3. Sum the ranks for Group A ($R_1$) and Group B ($R_2$).
        
- The Logic:
    
    If Group A is significantly larger than Group B, most of the high ranks will belong to Group A. Consequently, the sum of ranks $R_1$ will be much larger than expected.
    
- Test Statistic ($U$):
    
    $$U_1 = R_1 - \frac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2}$$
    
    We calculate $U$ for both groups and take the smaller value to compare against significance tables.

### 4. Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient ($r_s$ or $\rho$)

This is the nonparametric alternative to **Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient ($r$)**.

- Usage:
    
    Used when the relationship between variables is monotonic but not necessarily linear, or when the data is ordinal (e.g., Survey ranks).
    
    - _Linear:_ A constant rate of change (straight line).
        
    - _Monotonic:_ As X increases, Y increases (or decreases), but the curve can wobble or curve, provided it doesn't reverse direction.
        
- **The Procedure:**
    
    1. Rank the X values from 1 to $n$.
        
    2. Rank the Y values from 1 to $n$.
        
    3. Calculate the difference ($d$) between the ranks for each observation ($d_i = \text{Rank}(x_i) - \text{Rank}(y_i)$).
        
- The Formula:
    
    $$r_s = 1 - \frac{6 \sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}$$
    
- **Interpretation:**
    
    - $+1$: Perfect positive monotonic relationship.
        
    - $-1$: Perfect negative monotonic relationship.
        
    - $0$: No monotonic relationship.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Efficiency Trade-off:**
    
    - If your data _is_ Normal, using a Nonparametric test is "wasteful." It has about 95% of the **Power Efficiency** of a T-test. This means you need a slightly larger sample size in a nonparametric test to detect the same effect that a T-test would find.
        
    - However, if data is _Non-Normal_, nonparametric tests can be _much_ more powerful than T-tests.
        
2. **Handling Ties:**
    
    - In ranking methods, if two values are identical (e.g., two people have the same score), they tie for a rank.
        
    - _Rule:_ Assign them the **average** of the ranks they would have occupied. (e.g., If the 4th and 5th values are equal, both get a rank of 4.5).
        
3. **Choosing the Right Test (Exam Strategy):**
    
    - One Group / Paired Differences + Normal $\rightarrow$ **Paired T-test**.
        
    - One Group / Paired Differences + Non-Normal $\rightarrow$ **Wilcoxon Signed-Rank**.
        
    - Two Independent Groups + Normal $\rightarrow$ **Independent T-test**.
        
    - Two Independent Groups + Non-Normal $\rightarrow$ **Wilcoxon Rank-Sum**.
