# 13.2 Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This test is the "smart sibling" of the Sign Test. Instead of just asking "Did patients get better?", it asks "Did they get _a lot_ better or just _a little_ better?" It incorporates the **magnitude** of the change using ranks.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** This is the most common nonparametric calculation in exams. You will likely be given a table of paired data (e.g., $N=8$) and asked to manually calculate the test statistic $W$ or $T$.
    

---

### **1. Purpose and Logic**

The **Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test** is the nonparametric equivalent of the **Paired Samples T-Test**.

- **When to use:**
    
    1. The data is **Paired** (Dependent), e.g., Before/After or Twin studies.
        
    2. The sample size is small ($n < 30$).
        
    3. The data violates the Normality assumption required for the T-test (skewed data).
        
- **The Improvement:** unlike the Sign Test (Topic 13.1), which discards magnitude, this test **ranks** the differences. A difference of $+50$ receives a higher rank (more weight) than a difference of $+1$.
    

---

### **2. The Calculation Procedure (Step-by-Step)**

To perform this test manually (for small samples), follow these five steps:

1. **Calculate Differences ($d_i$):** Find the difference for each pair ($x_{after} - x_{before}$).
    
    - _Note:_ If $d = 0$ (no change), **discard** that data point and reduce $n$.
        
2. **Rank Absolute Differences ($|d_i|$):** Ignore the signs (+/-) for a moment. Rank the absolute values from smallest to largest.
    
    - Smallest $|d|$ gets Rank 1.
        
    - **Handling Ties:** If two differences are equal (e.g., both are 5), assign them the **average** of the ranks they would have occupied. (e.g., if they are tied for ranks 3 and 4, give both 3.5).
        
3. **Restore Signs:** Attach the original sign of the difference to the rank.
    
    - If $d_i$ was negative, the rank becomes negative.
        
4. **Sum the Ranks:** Calculate two sums:
    
    - $T^+$: Sum of positive ranks.
        
    - $T^-$: Sum of negative ranks (absolute value).
        
5. Determine Test Statistic ($T$ or $W$):
    
    The test statistic is usually the smaller of the two sums.
    
    $$T_{stat} = \min(T^+, T^-)$$
    

---

### **3. Hypothesis Testing**

- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The median difference is zero. The distribution of differences is symmetric around 0.
    
- **Decision Rule:**
    
    - Consult the **Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Table** (not the Z-table or T-table).
        
    - **Critical Value:** Look up the value for your $n$ and $\alpha$.
        
    - **Rejection Region:** Unlike most tests where "Big is Significant," here **Small is Significant**. If $T_{stat} \le T_{critical}$, reject $H_0$.
        
    - _Logic:_ If $H_0$ is true, positives and negatives should cancel out, so $T^+$ and $T^-$ should be roughly equal (large). If $T_{stat}$ is tiny, it means one side dominates.
        

---

### **4. Large Sample Approximation ($n > 20$)**

If the sample size is large, the sampling distribution of the statistic $T$ converges to a Normal Distribution.

- Mean ($\mu_T$):
    
    $$\mu_T = \frac{n(n+1)}{4}$$
    
- Standard Deviation ($\sigma_T$):
    
    $$\sigma_T = \sqrt{\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{24}}$$
    
- Z-Score Formula:
    
    $$Z = \frac{T - \mu_T}{\sigma_T}$$
    
    (Interpret this Z-score using the standard normal table).
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Sign Test vs. Wilcoxon:**
    
    - **Scenario:** Patient A improves by 1 point. Patient B improves by 100 points.
        
    - **Sign Test:** Treats them equally ("2 successes").
        
    - **Wilcoxon:** Patient B gets a much higher rank, acknowledging the stronger effect. Therefore, Wilcoxon is **more powerful** than the Sign Test.
        
- **Symmetry Assumption:** While Wilcoxon doesn't require _Normality_, it strictly assumes the distribution of differences is **Symmetric**. If the data is wildly asymmetric, this test loses validity.
    
- **Ties in Data:** Ties (zero differences) reduce your sample size $n$. Ties in ranks (same magnitude) affect the standard deviation formula slightly (though usually ignored in introductory hand calculations).
