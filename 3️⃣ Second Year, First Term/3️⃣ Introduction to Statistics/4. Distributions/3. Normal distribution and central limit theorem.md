

# 4.3 Normal Distribution and Central Limit Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** The Normal Distribution is the "King of Statistics." It is the mathematical assumption underlying almost every parametric test (Z-test, T-test, ANOVA, Regression). If you do not grasp this, you cannot understand inferential statistics.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** You will undoubtedly use the Z-score formula in exams. Additionally, the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is a favorite topic for theoretical essay questions asking _why_ we can assume normality for non-normal populations.

### **1. The Normal Distribution (Gaussian Distribution)**

The Normal Distribution is a continuous probability distribution that describes a symmetric, bell-shaped curve. It is the most common distribution in nature (e.g., height, blood pressure, IQ scores, measurement error).

- **Notation:** $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$
    
    - Defined entirely by two parameters: the **Mean ($\mu$)** and the **Variance ($\sigma^2$)**.
        
- **Key Properties:**
    
    1. **Symmetry:** The Mean, Median, and Mode are all equal and located at the exact center peak.
        
    2. **Asymptotic:** The tails approach the x-axis but never touch it (ranges from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$).
        
    3. **Area:** The total area under the curve is exactly 1.

### **2. The Standard Normal Distribution (Z)**

Because there are infinite combinations of $\mu$ and $\sigma$, we cannot have a table for every possible normal curve. Instead, we transform any normal variable $X$ into the **Standard Normal Variable $Z$**.

- **Definition:** A normal distribution with a Mean of 0 and a Standard Deviation of 1.
    
    - Notation: $Z \sim N(0, 1)$.
        
- The Z-Score Formula:
    
    This formula calculates how many standard deviations a specific value $x$ is away from the mean.
    
    $$Z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$
    
- Using the Z-Table:
    
    Once we have a Z-score, we look it up in a standard statistical table to find the probability (Area under the curve).
    
    - _Example:_ If $Z = 1.96$, the table tells us that 97.5% of the data lies to the left (CDF).

### **3. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)**

The CLT is often called the "Fundamental Theorem of Statistics" because it allows us to use Normal probability logic even when the raw data is **not** Normal.

- The Theorem:
    
    Given a population with any shape (even highly skewed or uniform) with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the sampling distribution of the sample mean ($\bar{x}$) will approach a Normal Distribution as the sample size ($n$) gets larger.
    
- **Key Conditions:**
    
    1. **Random Sampling:** Samples must be independent.
        
    2. **Sample Size:** A general rule of thumb is **$n \ge 30$**. If the original population is normal, $n$ can be smaller. If it is extremely skewed, $n$ might need to be $>50$.
        
- The Resulting Distribution (Sampling Distribution):
    
    The distribution of the sample means will have:
    
    - **Mean:** $\mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu$ (Unbiased).
        
    - **Standard Deviation (Standard Error):** $\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$.
        
    - _Note:_ The spread of the averages is always _smaller_ than the spread of the individuals.
        

---

### **4. Calculating Probabilities for Means**

Because of the CLT, when calculating the probability of a **Sample Mean** (not a single individual), we must use the **Standard Error** in the denominator of the Z-score formula.

- Single Value Z-Score:
    
    $$Z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$
    
- Sample Mean Z-Score:
    
    $$Z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7):**
    
    - 68% of data falls within $\pm 1\sigma$.
        
    - 95% of data falls within $\pm 1.96\sigma$ (often rounded to 2).
        
    - 99.7% of data falls within $\pm 3\sigma$.
        
- **Continuity Correction:** When approximating a Discrete distribution (like the Binomial) using the Normal distribution, you must expand the interval by 0.5.
    
    - _Example:_ To find $P(X=10)$ in binomial, calculate the Normal area between $9.5$ and $10.5$.
        
- **Misconception Alert:** The CLT does _not_ say "Data becomes normal." The data stays skewed. It says "The _Average_ of the data becomes normal."
