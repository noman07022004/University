# 4.6 Tests of Normality ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is a critical "gatekeeper" topic. Before you can perform a T-test, ANOVA, or Linear Regression (the bulk of Section B), you **must** verify the assumption that your data is normally distributed. If you skip this step, your subsequent P-values may be invalid.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** You are rarely asked to perform a Shapiro-Wilk test by hand (it's too complex). Instead, exams provide computer output or Q-Q plots and ask you to interpret whether the normality assumption is met.
    

---

### **1. Why Test for Normality?**

Most parametric statistical tests (like the T-test) rely on the mathematical assumption that the population from which the sample is drawn follows a **Normal Distribution**.

- **If Data is Normal:** We use parametric tests (more powerful).
    
- **If Data is NOT Normal:** We must either:
    
    1. Transform the data (e.g., Log transformation).
        
    2. Use Non-parametric tests (Topic 13, like Wilcoxon).
        
    3. Rely on the Central Limit Theorem (if $n$ is very large).
        

---

### **2. Graphical Methods (Visual Inspection)**

The first step in testing normality is always visual.

#### **A. Histogram**

- **Method:** Plot the frequency of data values.
    
- **Check:** Does it look like a bell curve? Is it symmetric?
    
- **Limitation:** Subjective. Bin width choice can distort the shape.
    

#### **B. Q-Q Plot (Quantile-Quantile Plot)**

- **Method:** A scatterplot that compares two sets of quantiles:
    
    - **X-axis:** Theoretical Quantiles (What the data _should_ be if Normal).
        
    - **Y-axis:** Sample Quantiles (Your actual data).
        
- **Interpretation:**
    
    - **Straight Diagonal Line:** Data is Normal.
        
    - **Curved (Banana shape):** Data is Skewed.
        
    - **S-shaped:** Data has Heavy Tails (Kurtosis).
        
- **Exam Tip:** If the points hug the line but stray at the very ends, the data is usually considered "Approximately Normal."
    

---

### **3. Formal Statistical Tests**

Visuals are subjective; formal tests give us a P-value.

#### **A. Shapiro-Wilk Test ($W$)**

- **Usage:** The "Gold Standard" for small to medium sample sizes ($n < 50$ to $2000$).
    
- **Hypotheses:**
    
    - **$H_0$:** The data **is** Normally distributed.
        
    - **$H_1$:** The data is **not** Normally distributed.
        
- **Decision Rule:**
    
    - If **P-value < 0.05**: Reject $H_0$. The data is **Non-Normal**.
        
    - If **P-value $\ge$ 0.05**: Fail to Reject $H_0$. We assume Normality.
        
- **Note:** This is one of the few times in statistics where we _want_ a high P-value (to keep $H_0$).
    

#### **B. Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test (K-S Test)**

- **Usage:** Often used for larger sample sizes ($n > 50$).
    
- **Method:** It compares the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) of your data with the theoretical Normal CDF. It measures the maximum distance ($D$) between the two curves.
    
- **Weakness:** It is less powerful than Shapiro-Wilk and sensitive to outliers.
    

#### **C. Anderson-Darling Test**

- **Usage:** A modification of the K-S test that gives more weight to the **tails** of the distribution. It is often preferred in engineering and reliability analysis.
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The Sample Size Paradox:**
    
    - **Small Samples ($n < 30$):** Normality tests have low power. They might fail to detect non-normality even if it exists. (Visual inspection is safer here).
        
    - **Large Samples ($n > 1000$):** Normality tests are _too_ sensitive. They will reject Normality for tiny, irrelevant deviations. In these cases, the Central Limit Theorem usually protects us anyway, so the test result matters less.
        
- **Box Plots:** Another visual tool. If the median line is in the center of the box and the whiskers are equal length, the data is symmetric (likely Normal). If the median is near the top/bottom or there are many outlier dots, it is Non-Normal.
