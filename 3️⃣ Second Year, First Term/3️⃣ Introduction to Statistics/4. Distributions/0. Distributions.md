### Introduction to Probability Distributions

A **Probability Distribution** is a mathematical function that provides the probabilities of occurrence of different possible outcomes for an experiment. It acts as a bridge between raw data and theoretical probability.

In university statistics, we strictly categorize these into two families based on the type of random variable:

1. **Discrete Distributions:** For countable data (e.g., number of students, coin tosses). These use a **Probability Mass Function (PMF)**, denoted as $P(X=x)$.
    
2. **Continuous Distributions:** For measurable data (e.g., height, time, temperature). These use a **Probability Density Function (PDF)**, denoted as $f(x)$. _Note: For continuous variables, the probability of a single exact point is zero; we calculate probabilities over intervals (area under the curve)._
    

To build a logical narrative, we will first cover the **Discrete** family (Binomial, Hypergeometric, Poisson, Geometric), then the **Continuous** family (Normal, Exponential, Gamma), and finally discussing how we validate and use these models (CLT, Testing).

---

### Part I: Discrete Distributions

#### 1. Binomial Distribution

This is the standard model for a series of "Yes/No" experiments. It applies when:

- There are a fixed number of trials ($n$).
    
- Each trial is independent.
    
- There are only two outcomes: Success ($p$) or Failure ($q = 1-p$).
    
- The probability of success ($p$) remains constant.
    

The Formula:

$$P(X=x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}$$

- Mean: $\mu = np$
    
- Variance: $\sigma^2 = np(1-p)$
    

#### 2. Hypergeometric Distribution

This is the "cousin" of the Binomial distribution, but for sampling without replacement.

If you draw cards from a deck and do not put them back, the probability of success changes with each draw. Therefore, trials are dependent, and the Binomial model fails.

- **Scenario:** A population of size $N$ contains $K$ successes. We draw a sample of size $n$. What is the probability of getting exactly $k$ successes?
    

#### 3. Poisson Distribution

The Poisson distribution models **rare events** occurring over a fixed interval of **time or space**.

- **Key Assumption:** The events occur independently and at a constant average rate ($\lambda$).
    
- **Examples:** Number of emails received per hour; number of decay events in a radioactive source per second.
    
- The Formula:
    
    $$P(X=x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^x}{x!}$$
    
    - _Unique Property:_ The Mean and Variance are equal ($\mu = \sigma^2 = \lambda$).
        

#### 4. Geometric Distribution

While the Binomial counts successes in fixed trials, the Geometric distribution counts **how many trials are needed to get the _first_ success**.

- **Memoryless Property:** Past failures do not influence future probabilities. If you flipped a coin 10 times and got tails, the probability of heads on the 11th flip is still 0.5.
    
- **Formula:** $P(X=x) = (1-p)^{x-1}p$

### Part II: Continuous Distributions

#### 5. Normal Distribution

The "Bell Curve." This is the most important distribution in statistics because many natural phenomena (height, IQ, error terms) approximate it.

- Defined entirely by two parameters: Mean ($\mu$) and Standard Deviation ($\sigma$).
    
- Symmetric around the mean.
    
- Standard Normal Distribution ($Z$): To calculate probabilities, we often transform any normal distribution into the "Standard" version where $\mu=0$ and $\sigma=1$ using the Z-score formula:
    
    $$Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$$
    

#### 6. Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

This is the "Fundamental Theorem of Statistics."

- **Definition:** Given a population with _any_ shape (even non-normal), the sampling distribution of the sample mean ($\bar{x}$) will approach a **Normal Distribution** as the sample size ($n$) gets large (usually $n \ge 30$).
    
- **Significance:** This allows us to use Normal probability theories to analyze data even if the original population is not Normal.
    

#### 7. Exponential Distribution

The continuous counterpart to the Geometric distribution. It models the **time waiting between events** in a Poisson process.

- **Example:** Time until the next earthquake; time until a lightbulb fails.
    
- **Formula:** $f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$.
    
- Like the Geometric, it is **memoryless**.
    

#### 8. Gamma Distributions

A generalization of the Exponential distribution.

- While Exponential models the wait time for the _first_ event, the Gamma distribution models the waiting time for the **$\alpha$-th event** to occur.
    
- It is defined by shape parameter $\alpha$ and rate parameter $\beta$ (or scale $\theta$).
    
- _Note:_ The Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) distribution (used later in Topic 6 & 7) is a special case of the Gamma distribution.

### Part III: Testing & Validation

#### 9. Tests of Normality

Before using advanced statistical tests (like T-tests), we must verify if our data actually follows a Normal Distribution. If it doesn't, our results may be invalid.

- **Graphical Methods:** Q-Q Plots (Quantile-Quantile plots). If the data points fall on a straight diagonal line, the data is Normal.
    
- **Formal Tests:**
    
    - **Shapiro-Wilk Test:** The gold standard for small sample sizes.
        
    - **Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test:** Used for larger samples.
        

#### 10. Testing Statistical Hypothesis (Introduction)

In the context of distributions, hypothesis testing is the process of asking: _"Is this sample result consistent with the distribution defined by the Null Hypothesis?"_

- We set up a **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$)** (e.g., "The coin is fair, $p=0.5$").
    
- We assume the data follows a specific distribution (e.g., Binomial) based on $H_0$.
    
- We calculate the probability of observing our specific data under that distribution.
    
- If that probability (p-value) is too low (usually $< 0.05$), the data falls into the "tail" or **Critical Region** of the distribution, and we reject the Null Hypothesis.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Binomial vs. Hypergeometric:**
    
    - Small population + Sampling _without_ replacement $\rightarrow$ **Hypergeometric**.
        
    - Large population (or infinite) + Sampling _with_ replacement $\rightarrow$ **Binomial**.
        
    - _Tip:_ If the population size $N$ is very large relative to sample size $n$ (usually $N > 20n$), you can approximate Hypergeometric with Binomial.
        
2. **Poisson Approximation:** You can use the Poisson distribution to approximate the Binomial distribution when $n$ is large and $p$ is very small (rare events). Set $\lambda = np$.
    
3. **Continuity Correction:** When approximating a discrete distribution (like Binomial) using a continuous one (like Normal), you must adjust the discrete value by 0.5 (e.g., $P(X=10)$ becomes the area from 9.5 to 10.5).
    
4. **The "Memoryless" Trap:** In exams, a trick question might ask: "A lightbulb has lasted 100 hours. What is the probability it lasts another 10?" If it follows an Exponential distribution, the fact that it lasted 100 hours is irrelevant. The probability is exactly the same as a new bulb lasting 10 hours.
