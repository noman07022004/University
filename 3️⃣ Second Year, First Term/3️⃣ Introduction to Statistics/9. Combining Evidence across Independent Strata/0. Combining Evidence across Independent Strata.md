### Introduction

In previous topics, we compared groups directly (e.g., Treatment vs. Control). However, looking at the "Big Picture" can sometimes be deceptively wrong due to **Confounding Variables**.

- **The Problem:** Imagine a study showing that coffee drinkers have higher cancer rates. If you do not account for the fact that coffee drinkers are also more likely to smoke (the confounder), your "crude" analysis is biased.
    
- **The Solution:** We **Stratify** the data. We separate people into "Smokers" and "Non-Smokers" (strata) and look at the effect of coffee _within_ each stratum.
    

This section covers the statistical methods used to recombine these separated strata back into a single, valid conclusion.

---

### 1. Mantel-Haenszel and Fisher Methods for Combining Evidence

These are two distinct approaches to aggregating data from multiple independent strata (or multiple studies).

#### A. The Mantel-Haenszel (M-H) Method

This is the gold standard technique for analyzing stratified categorical data. It is primarily used to calculate a **Weighted Average of Odds Ratios** across different strata (e.g., across different age groups or hospitals).

- **Purpose:** To estimate the "common odds ratio" ($OR_{MH}$) effectively removing the effect of a confounding variable.
    
- **The Logic:** It gives more weight to strata with more data (larger sample sizes) and less weight to smaller strata.
    
- **The Procedure:**
    
    1. Create a $2 \times 2$ table for _each_ stratum $i$.
        
    2. Calculate the numerator ($O_i - E_i$) and variance for each.
        
    3. Sum them up to get a pooled estimate.
        
- Formula (Simplified Concept):
    
    $$OR_{MH} = \frac{\sum (a_i d_i / T_i)}{\sum (b_i c_i / T_i)}$$
    
    Where $a, b, c, d$ are cell counts and $T$ is the total for stratum $i$.
    

#### B. Fisher’s Method (Combined Probability Test)

While M-H combines _effect sizes_ (Odds Ratios), Fisher's method combines **P-values**. It is used when you have several independent studies testing the same hypothesis, and you want to know if there is an overall effect, even if individual studies were not significant.

- **The Logic:** If the Null Hypothesis is true for all studies, the P-values should be uniformly distributed. Fisher proved that $-2 \ln(p)$ follows a Chi-squared distribution.
    
- The Formula:
    
    $$\chi^2 = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{k} \ln(p_i)$$
    
    - Where $p_i$ is the p-value from the $i$-th study.
        
    - The result is compared to a $\chi^2$ distribution with $2k$ degrees of freedom.

### 2. Meta-Analysis

Meta-Analysis takes the mathematical concepts of "combining evidence" and applies them to the highest level of scientific research: combining entire studies.

- **Definition:** A statistical technique for combining the findings from independent studies to determine an overall effect size. It is the quantitative component of a **Systematic Review**.
    
- **Why do it?**
    
    - Increases **Statistical Power** (combining small samples = one huge sample).
        
    - Resolves uncertainty when individual studies disagree (some positive, some negative).
        
- The Forest Plot:
    
    The primary visualization tool for meta-analysis.
    
    - **Squares:** Represent the effect size of individual studies. The size of the square represents the weight (sample size).
        
    - **Horizontal Lines:** The Confidence Interval for each study.
        
    - **The Diamond:** The final combined result (pooled effect). If the diamond touches the "Line of No Effect" (usually 1 for Odds Ratios), the result is not significant.
        
- Heterogeneity ($I^2$):
    
    A measure of how different the studies are from each other.
    
    - If Heterogeneity is **low**, we trust the combined result (Fixed Effects Model).
        
    - If Heterogeneity is **high** (studies show wildly different results), we must use caution (Random Effects Model).

### Knowledge Gap Fill: Simpson's Paradox

You cannot fully understand this topic without understanding **Simpson's Paradox**. This is the phenomenon that necessitates the Mantel-Haenszel test.

- **Definition:** A trend appears in several different groups of data but **disappears or reverses** when these groups are combined.
    
- **Classic Example (UC Berkeley Admissions):**
    
    - _Aggregate Data:_ Men seemed to be admitted at a much higher rate than women (bias?).
        
    - _Stratified Data (by Department):_ Women actually had higher admission rates than men in almost every department.
        
    - _The Cause:_ Women applied disproportionately to "hard" departments (low acceptance rates), while men applied to "easy" departments. The aggregate data was "confounded" by department choice.
        
    - **Lesson:** Always stratify before concluding.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **Odds Ratio (OR) Interpretation:** In M-H and Meta-analysis, the "Line of No Effect" is **1**, not 0.
    
    - $OR > 1$: Exposure increases risk.
        
    - $OR < 1$: Exposure decreases risk (protective).
        
    - $OR = 1$: No effect.
        
2. **Publication Bias:** A major risk in Meta-Analysis. "Positive" studies (finding a cure) are published; "Negative" studies (finding nothing) are put in a file drawer. This makes the meta-analysis biased towards positive results. We check for this using a **Funnel Plot**.
    
3. **Fisher's Method Constraint:** It requires independent data sets. You cannot use Fisher's method to combine P-values from different tests run on the _same_ dataset.
