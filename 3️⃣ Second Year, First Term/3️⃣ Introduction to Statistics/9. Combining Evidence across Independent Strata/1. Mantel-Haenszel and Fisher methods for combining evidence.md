# 9.1 Mantel-Haenszel and Fisher Methods for Combining Evidence ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This topic addresses a fundamental challenge in research: what to do when a "hidden variable" (confounder) distorts the relationship between two other variables. The Mantel-Haenszel test provides the mathematical fix for this, making it a cornerstone of epidemiology and evidence-based medicine.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** You typically won't be asked to calculate the full M-H statistic by hand for large datasets. However, questions often focus on the _concept_ of stratification (e.g., "Why did the Odds Ratio change when we split the data by age?") or calculating the statistic for small $2 \times 2$ tables.
    

---

### **1. The Problem: Confounding and Simpson’s Paradox**

Before learning the method, you must understand the problem it solves. Sometimes, combining data from different groups (strata) leads to a false conclusion.

- **Simpson’s Paradox:** A trend that appears in different groups of data but disappears or reverses when these groups are combined.
    
- **Example:** Hospital A has a higher death rate than Hospital B. Is Hospital A worse? Not necessarily. Hospital A might be a trauma center receiving critically ill patients (high risk), while Hospital B treats minor injuries (low risk). If you simply combine all patients without controlling for "Severity of Illness" (the confounder), you wrongly penalize Hospital A.
    
- **Solution:** We must "stratify" the analysis—calculate the relationship separately for each subgroup (stratum) and then combine them mathematically.
    

---

### **2. The Mantel-Haenszel (M-H) Method**

The **Mantel-Haenszel procedure** allows us to combine results from multiple $2 \times 2$ contingency tables (strata) to produce a single, adjusted summary statistic (usually an Odds Ratio) that removes the effect of the confounding variable.

#### **A. The Structure**

Assume we have $k$ strata (e.g., Age Groups). For the $i$-th stratum, the table is:

||**Success (Case)**|**Failure (Control)**|**Total**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Exposed (Group 1)**|$a_i$|$b_i$|$n_{1i}$|
|**Unexposed (Group 2)**|$c_i$|$d_i$|$n_{2i}$|
|**Total**|$m_{1i}$|$m_{2i}$|$N_i$|

#### **B. The Common Odds Ratio ($\hat{OR}_{MH}$)**

Instead of a simple average, M-H calculates a weighted average of the individual Odds Ratios from each stratum. The weight comes from the sample size of each stratum (specifically, $b_i c_i / N_i$).

$$\hat{OR}_{MH} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} (a_i d_i / N_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} (b_i c_i / N_i)}$$

- **Numerator:** Sum of the "cross-product" ($a \times d$) weighted by total $N$.
    
- **Denominator:** Sum of the "cross-product" ($b \times c$) weighted by total $N$.
    

#### **C. The Chi-Squared Test for Significance**

To test if this common Odds Ratio is significantly different from 1 (i.e., is there an association after adjusting for the confounder?), we use the **Mantel-Haenszel Chi-Squared Statistic**.

- It tests the null hypothesis that there is no association in any of the strata.
    
- _Note:_ The formula is complex and rarely tested by hand, but knowing it follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $df=1$ is important.
    

---

### **3. Fisher’s Method for Combining P-Values**

While Mantel-Haenszel combines _Effect Sizes_ (Odds Ratios), **Fisher’s Method** combines _Significance Levels_ (P-values).

- **Scenario:** You have five different studies testing the same hypothesis (e.g., "Does Coffee cause Cancer?"). None of them are statistically significant on their own (e.g., $P = 0.08, 0.12, 0.09...$), but they all lean in the same direction.
    
- **Goal:** To determine if the _aggregate_ evidence is significant.
    
- The Statistic ($\chi^2$):
    
    Fisher proved that if you take the natural log of a P-value and multiply by -2, it follows a Chi-Squared distribution.
    
    $$\chi^2 = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{k} \ln(p_i)$$
    
    - $p_i$: The P-value from the $i$-th study.
        
    - $k$: The number of studies combined.
        
    - **Degrees of Freedom:** $2k$.
        
- **Interpretation:** If this calculated $\chi^2$ is large (greater than the critical value for $2k$ degrees of freedom), we conclude that the collective evidence rejects the Null Hypothesis, even if no single study did.
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

1. **Homogeneity Assumption:** Before using the Mantel-Haenszel estimator, you should technically check if the Odds Ratios are roughly similar across all strata (using a Test of Homogeneity, like Breslow-Day). If the OR is 2.0 in Young people but 0.5 in Old people, combining them into a single number is misleading (this is called **Effect Modification** or Interaction).
    
2. **Relation to Meta-Analysis:** Fisher's method is a rudimentary form of Meta-Analysis. Modern meta-analysis (Subtopic 9.2) is more sophisticated because it weights studies by their variance/precision, not just their P-values.
    
3. **Terminology:** In exams, you might see this referred to as the "Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) Test." It is the same fundamental procedure.
