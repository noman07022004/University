# 11.2 Attributable Risk (AR) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** While Relative Risk (RR) tells you _how strong_ an association is (e.g., "Smoking doubles your risk"), Attributable Risk tells you the _magnitude_ of the problem (e.g., "Smoking causes 50 extra deaths per 1,000 people"). This distinction is critical for public health policy.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Calculation questions are standard here. You will often be given a $2 \times 2$ table or incidence rates and asked to calculate the "Excess Risk" or the "Proportion of cases preventable if the exposure were eliminated."
    

---

### **1. Definition: The "Excess" Risk**

**Attributable Risk (AR)** (also called Risk Difference) measures the absolute difference in incidence rates between the exposed group and the non-exposed group.

- **The Question:** "How much of the disease in the exposed group is actually _caused_ by the exposure?"
    
- **The Logic:** Even unexposed people get sick (background risk). We want to subtract that background risk to find the "excess" risk added by the exposure.
    

---

### **2. The Formulas**

We rely on two key incidence rates:

- $I_e$: Incidence in the **Exposed** group.
    
- $I_u$: Incidence in the **Unexposed** (Reference) group.
    

#### **A. Attributable Risk (Risk Difference)**

This gives the _number_ of extra cases attributable to the exposure.

$$AR = I_e - I_u$$

- **Interpretation:** If $AR > 0$, the exposure increases risk. If $AR = 0$, there is no association.
    

#### **B. Attributable Risk Percent (AR%)**

This is often more intuitive. It tells us the _proportion_ of disease in the exposed group that would be eliminated if the exposure were removed.

$$AR\% = \frac{I_e - I_u}{I_e} \times 100$$

(Alternatively: $\frac{RR - 1}{RR} \times 100$)

- **Example:** If Smokers have a cancer risk of 10% and Non-smokers have a risk of 1%, then 90% of the cancer cases _among smokers_ are attributable to smoking.
    

---

### **3. Population Attributable Risk (PAR)**

While AR focuses on the specific group of exposed people, **Population Attributable Risk (PAR)** asks: _"How much impact does this exposure have on the entire community?"_

This metric combines the _risk_ of the exposure with the _prevalence_ of the exposure.

- Formula:
    
    $$PAR = I_{pop} - I_u$$
    
    (Incidence in Total Population minus Incidence in Unexposed)
    
- **Why it matters:**
    
    - A high-risk exposure (e.g., Ebola contact) might have a **low PAR** because almost nobody is exposed.
        
    - A low-risk exposure (e.g., Air pollution) might have a **huge PAR** because everyone is exposed.
        
    - _Public Health Implication:_ To improve community health, targeting high PAR factors is often more effective than targeting high RR factors.
        

---

### **4. Relative Risk (RR) vs. Attributable Risk (AR)**

Distinguishing these two is the most common conceptual challenge in exams.

|**Feature**|**Relative Risk (RR)**|**Attributable Risk (AR)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Formula**|Ratio: $I_e / I_u$|Difference: $I_e - I_u$|
|**Question**|"How much _more likely_ is the disease?"|"How many _excess cases_ are there?"|
|**Utility**|**Etiology (Causation):** Strong RR suggests a causal link.|**Public Health (Impact):** High AR suggests saving many lives by intervention.|
|**Example**|Smokers are **10x** more likely to die.|Smoking causes **900** extra deaths per 100k.|

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The "Preventable Fraction":** In exams, AR% is often phrased as the "Etiologic Fraction" or "Preventable Fraction." If a question asks, _"What percentage of cases could be prevented if we banned the toxin?"_, calculate **AR%**.
    
- **Assumptions:** These calculations assume **Causality**. Calculating AR implies you believe the exposure _causes_ the disease. If the association is just a correlation (confounding), eliminating the exposure will not actually eliminate the "excess" cases.
    
- **Units:**
    
    - **RR** has no units (it's a ratio).
        
    - **AR** has units (e.g., "cases per 1,000 person-years").
