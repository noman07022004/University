# 11.3 Epidemiologic Principles of Causation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the philosophical core of science. Statistical tests (like T-tests or Chi-Squared) can only prove **Association** (that two things happen together). They cannot prove **Causation** (that one thing _makes_ the other happen). This topic provides the logical framework to bridge that gap.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** You will frequently face essay questions or case studies asking you to "Evaluate whether Exposure X likely caused Disease Y" using standard criteria.
    

---

### **1. Association vs. Causation**

The golden rule of statistics is **"Correlation does not imply Causation."**

- **Spurious Association:** A relationship that is mathematically significant but false in reality, often due to chance or bias.
    
- **Confounded Association:** A relationship that is real but caused by a third variable (e.g., Ice cream sales correlate with shark attacks because both happen in summer).
    
- **Causal Association:** A relationship where a change in the exposure produces a direct change in the outcome.
    

---

### **2. The Bradford Hill Criteria (The Gold Standard)**

In 1965, Sir Austin Bradford Hill established a set of nine guidelines to help researchers decide if an observed association is truly causal. These are the standard "checklist" for epidemiology exams.

1. **Temporality (The Only Mandatory Rule):**
    
    - The Cause must _precede_ the Effect in time.
        
    - _Example:_ You must smoke _before_ you get lung cancer. If the cancer came first, smoking didn't cause it.
        
2. **Strength of Association:**
    
    - A large Effect Size (High Relative Risk or Odds Ratio) is more likely to be causal because it is harder for bias to explain it away.
        
    - _Example:_ Smoking increases lung cancer risk by 20x (Very Strong).
        
3. **Consistency (Replicability):**
    
    - The association is observed repeatedly by different persons, in different places, circumstances, and times.
        
4. **Biological Gradient (Dose-Response):**
    
    - Greater exposure leads to greater incidence of the effect.
        
    - _Example:_ People who smoke 2 packs a day have higher risk than those who smoke 1 pack.
        
5. **Plausibility:**
    
    - There is a known biological mechanism that explains _how_ the exposure causes the disease.
        
    - _Example:_ We know carcinogens in smoke damage DNA.
        
6. **Specificity:**
    
    - A single cause leads to a single effect. (This is the weakest criterion, as many things like smoking cause multiple diseases).
        
7. **Coherence:**
    
    - The cause-and-effect interpretation should not conflict with the generally known facts of the natural history and biology of the disease.
        
8. **Experiment:**
    
    - If you remove the exposure (intervention), the frequency of the outcome declines.
        
9. **Analogy:**
    
    - Similar exposures cause similar effects (e.g., if one drug causes birth defects, a chemically similar drug might too).
        

---

### **3. Models of Causation (Rothman’s Causal Pies)**

Real-world diseases rarely have a single cause. We use the **Sufficient-Component Cause Model** to explain complexity.

- **Necessary Cause:** A factor that _must_ be present for the disease to occur.
    
    - _Example:_ HIV virus is necessary for AIDS. (You cannot have AIDS without HIV).
        
- **Sufficient Cause:** A complete set of factors that inevitably produces the disease.
    
    - _Example:_ HIV + Weak Immune System + Time = AIDS. The virus alone might not be sufficient (some people live with it without symptoms), but the _combination_ ensures the disease.
        
- **Component Cause:** Any one slice of the pie. It is part of the sufficient mechanism but not enough on its own.
    

---

### **4. Koch’s Postulates (Historical Context)**

Before Bradford Hill, Robert Koch (19th century) defined causation for **Infectious Diseases**:

1. The organism must be present in every case of the disease.
    
2. It must be isolated and grown in pure culture.
    
3. It must cause the specific disease when inoculated into a healthy animal.
    
4. It must be recovered from the animal.
    

- _Note:_ These work for bacteria (Anthrax) but fail for chronic diseases (Heart Disease, Cancer) which have multiple causes and long latency periods.
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Exam Strategy:** If asked to "Discuss the likelihood of causation," explicitly list 3-4 Bradford Hill criteria (Start with Temporality, Strength, and Dose-Response) and apply them to the scenario provided.
    
- **The "Web of Causation":** Modern epidemiology rejects linear "Chain of Causation" models (A $\to$ B $\to$ C) in favor of a "Web" where multiple factors (genetics, environment, behavior) interact.
    
- **Inductive Logic:** Causation can never be proven with 100% certainty (unlike math). It is a judgment call based on the weight of evidence.
