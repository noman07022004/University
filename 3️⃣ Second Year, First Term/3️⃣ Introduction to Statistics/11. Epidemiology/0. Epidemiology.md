### Introduction

**Epidemiology** is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to control health problems. It moves beyond the clinical study of one patient to the statistical study of groups.

While a doctor asks, "What made _this_ patient sick?", an epidemiologist asks, "Why is the sickness rate higher in _this_ group than _that_ group?" To answer this, we must first measure the frequency of disease and then analyze the link between exposures (risk factors) and outcomes.

Knowledge Gap Fill: Incidence vs. Prevalence

Before calculating risk, you must distinguish between the two fundamental measures of disease frequency:

- **Prevalence:** The proportion of a population that _has_ the disease at a specific point in time. (Describes the burden of disease).
    
- **Incidence:** The rate of _new_ cases developing over a period of time. (Describes the risk of contracting the disease).

### 1. Attributable Risk

In Topic 9, we discussed the **Odds Ratio (OR)** and **Relative Risk (RR)**. These measure the _strength_ of an association (e.g., "Smokers are 10 times more likely to get cancer"). However, they do not tell us the _impact_. **Attributable Risk (AR)** answers the question: _"How much of the disease risk is actually due to the exposure?"_

- Definition:
    
    Attributable Risk (or Risk Difference) is the difference in incidence rates between the exposed group and the unexposed group. It quantifies the excess risk caused by the factor.
    
- The Formula:
    
    $$AR = I_e - I_u$$
    
    - $I_e$: Incidence rate in the **Exposed** group.
        
    - $I_u$: Incidence rate in the **Unexposed** group.
        
- Attributable Risk Percent (AR%):
    
    Often more useful, this tells us what percentage of cases in the exposed group would be eliminated if the risk factor were removed.
    
    $$AR\% = \frac{I_e - I_u}{I_e} \times 100$$
    
    - Alternative Formula using Relative Risk ($RR$):
        
        $$AR\% = \frac{RR - 1}{RR}$$
        
- Population Attributable Risk (PAR):
    
    This measures the excess risk in the entire population (both exposed and unexposed) caused by the risk factor. It depends on how common the exposure is (prevalence of exposure).
    
    - _Significance:_ A risk factor can have a high RR (very dangerous) but a low PAR if hardly anyone is exposed to it. Conversely, a weak risk factor (like obesity) can have a massive PAR because it is so common.

### 2. Epidemiologic Principles of Causation

Just because we find a statistical association (a high correlation or RR) does not mean the exposure _caused_ the disease. (Recall: Correlation $\neq$ Causation). To bridge the gap from statistics to biology, we use a set of logical criteria known as the **Bradford Hill Criteria** (1965).

You generally need to satisfy several of these to claim causation:

1. Temporality (The Essential Criterion):
    
    The cause must come before the effect. If the disease appeared before the exposure, the exposure cannot be the cause. This is the only non-negotiable criterion.
    
2. Strength of Association:
    
    A large Effect Size (high RR or OR) makes causation more likely. A Relative Risk of 20 (Smoking $\rightarrow$ Lung Cancer) is much harder to explain away as "bias" than a Relative Risk of 1.1.
    
3. Dose-Response Relationship (Biological Gradient):
    
    Higher exposure should lead to higher risk. (e.g., Smoking 2 packs/day should be riskier than 1 pack/day).
    
4. Consistency:
    
    The association is found repeatedly by different researchers, in different places, using different methods.
    
5. Plausibility:
    
    There is a known biological mechanism that explains how the exposure causes the disease.
    
6. Reversibility (Experiment):
    
    If the exposure is removed, the risk of disease should decline.

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **The "Bathtub" Analogy:** To visualize Prevalence vs. Incidence:
    
    - **Water flowing in from the tap:** Incidence (New cases).
        
    - **Water in the tub:** Prevalence (Existing cases).
        
    - **Water draining out:** Deaths or Cures.
        
    - _Fact:_ A new drug that _prevents death_ but does _not cure_ the disease will paradoxically **increase** prevalence (because sick people live longer, staying in the "tub").
        
2. Number Needed to Treat (NNT): This is a clinical application of Attributable Risk often found in exams. It is the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction.
    
    $$NNT = \frac{1}{I_u - I_e}$$
    
    (Note: Here $I_e$ is usually the treatment group with lower risk). It tells you: "How many people do I need to treat to prevent one bad outcome?"
    
3. **Confounding Reminder:** When evaluating causation, always mention that "Confounding variables must be ruled out" (linked to Topic 9). If a study didn't stratify, the causation claim is weak.
