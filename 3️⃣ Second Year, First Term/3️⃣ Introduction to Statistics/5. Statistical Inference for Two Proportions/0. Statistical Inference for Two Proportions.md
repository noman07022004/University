## 5. Statistical Inference(অনুমান) for Two Proportions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This topic marks the transition from theoretical probability to practical inferential statistics. It introduces the critical concept of comparing groups (e.g., Treatment vs. Control), which is the essence of scientific research.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Highly examinable. You will almost certainly face a problem asking you to compare two groups. The choice between using a Z-test, Chi-squared test, or Fisher's Exact test is a common "trap" area where students lose marks.

---

### Introduction

In the previous topic, we looked at distributions of single variables. Now, we move to **Comparative Inference**—specifically, comparing two categorical variables. The most common question in research is: _"Is the success rate in Group A different from Group B?"_ (e.g., Does the vaccine group have a lower infection rate than the placebo group?).

This section covers the tools used to test the equality of two proportions ($p_1 = p_2$). The choice of test depends on the sample size and the specific study design. We will also cover how to estimate the magnitude(মাত্রা) of this difference (Confidence Intervals) and the theoretical frameworks underlying these tests (MLE, Power, and Significance).

---

### Part I: Hypothesis Testing for Proportions

We start with the methods used to formally test if two proportions are significantly different.

#### 1. Chi-squared ($\chi^2$) and Z-score Tests for Equality of Two Proportions

When sample sizes are **large**, these are the standard tests.

- **Z-Score Test (Two-Sample z-test for proportions):**
    
    - Used to test the null hypothesis $H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0$.
        
    - It relies on the Normal approximation of the Binomial distribution (CLT).
        
    - The Statistic:
        
        $$Z = \frac{(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) - 0}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2})}}$$
        
        Where $\hat{p}$ is the pooled proportion (total successes / total observations).
        
- **Chi-Squared ($\chi^2$) Test of Homogeneity:**
    
    - Mathematically equivalent to the Z-test (specifically, $Z^2 = \chi^2$ for a $2\times2$ table).
        
    - It compares **Observed counts** vs. **Expected counts** (what we would expect if $H_0$ were true).
        
    - Formula:
        
        $$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$$
        
    - _Constraint:_ It requires that the Expected frequency in every cell is $\ge 5$. If counts are lower, this test is invalid.
        

#### 2. Fisher's Exact Test of Equality for Two Proportions

When sample sizes are **small** (typically when any cell in the contingency table has an expected count $< 5$), the Normal/Chi-squared approximation breaks down.

- **Method:** Instead of an approximation, it calculates the _exact_ probability of obtaining the observed data using the **Hypergeometric Distribution**.
    
- It assumes the row and column totals are fixed.
    
- _Academic Note:_ While computationally intensive for large numbers, it is the "Gold Standard" for accuracy in small medical trials or rare event studies.
    

---

### Part II: Estimation & Frameworks

Testing tells us _if_ there is a difference. Estimation tells us _how big_ the difference is.

#### 3. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

This is the theoretical method used to derive the formulas for $\hat{p}$.

- **Definition:** MLE is a method of estimating the parameters of a statistical model. It asks: _"What parameter value ($p$) would maximize the probability (likelihood) of observing the data we actually collected?"_
    
- For a Binomial distribution, the MLE for the proportion $p$ is simply the sample proportion $\hat{p} = x/n$.
    
- _Relevance:_ All the Z-tests and Confidence Intervals we use are mathematically derived from MLE principles.
    

#### 4. Confidence Intervals for Proportions

A point estimate (e.g., "The difference is 5%") is rarely enough. We need a range of plausible values.

- Confidence Interval (CI) for Difference ($p_1 - p_2$):
    
    $$(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm Z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}$$
    
- **Interpretation:** If the interval includes **0**, we cannot say there is a significant difference between the two groups (at the chosen confidence level).
    

---

### Part III: Interpretation & Validity

Finally, we look at the broader context of our results.

#### 5. Statistical Power in Hypothesis Testing

- **Definition:** Power ($1 - \beta$) is the probability of correctly rejecting a false Null Hypothesis. In plain English: _"If there really is an effect, what are the odds my study will detect it?"_
    
- **Components:** Power depends on:
    
    1. Sample Size ($n$): Larger samples $\rightarrow$ Higher Power.
        
    2. Effect Size ($\delta$): Bigger differences are easier to detect.
        
    3. Significance Level ($\alpha$): Being less strict (higher $\alpha$) increases power but risks False Positives.
        
- _Exam Tip:_ Underpowered studies (too small sample size) are a major cause of "non-significant" findings in research, even when an effect exists.
    

#### 6. Legal and Statistical Significance

This subtopic addresses the distinction between "proven" in a statistical sense vs. a legal/practical sense.

- **Statistical Significance:** Means the p-value $< \alpha$ (usually 0.05). It only tells us the result was unlikely to happen by chance. It does **not** measure importance.
    
    - _Example:_ With a massive sample size, a difference of 0.01% can be "statistically significant" but practically useless.
        
- **Legal/Practical Significance:** Focuses on the _magnitude_ of the effect and causality. In legal contexts (e.g., discrimination cases), stats must show a disparity large enough to imply intent or structural bias (often the "four-fifths rule" or specific standard deviation thresholds).

### Important Notes, Tips & Facts

1. **The "Pooled" Trap:**
    
    - When performing a **Hypothesis Test** (Null: $p_1 = p_2$), you must use the **Pooled Proportion** ($\hat{p}$) in the standard error calculation because you assume the groups are the same.
        
    - When calculating a **Confidence Interval**, you do **NOT** pool. You use the individual sample proportions ($\hat{p}_1$ and $\hat{p}_2$) because you are estimating the difference, not assuming equality.
        
2. **Continuity Correction:** When using the Z-test for proportions, we are approximating a discrete distribution (Binomial) with a continuous one (Normal). For greater accuracy (Yates' correction), we often subtract $0.5/n$ from the difference, though standard university courses often skip this unless specified.
    
3. **One-Sided vs. Two-Sided:** Always check the question.
    
    - "Is Group A _different_ from B?" $\rightarrow$ Two-sided ($H_0: p_1 = p_2$).
        
    - "Is Group A _better_ than B?" $\rightarrow$ One-sided ($H_0: p_1 \le p_2$).
        
4. **Fisher's Test limit:** In manual exams, you will usually only be asked to perform Fisher's Exact Test on extremely small tables (e.g., total $n < 20$) because the factorial calculations become unmanageable otherwise.
