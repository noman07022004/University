
# 5.6 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the theoretical engine room of modern statistics. Almost every formula you have memorized so far—the sample mean ($\bar{x}$), the sample proportion ($\hat{p}$), and regression coefficients—is actually derived using this method. It answers the fundamental question: _"Where do these formulas come from?"_
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** In introductory courses, you are rarely asked to perform the calculus derivation for complex models. However, you are frequently asked to explain the _logic_ of MLE or derived the estimator for a simple Binomial proportion.
    

---

### **1. The Philosophy of MLE**

Up until now, we have assumed we know the parameters ($\mu, \sigma, p$) and calculated the probability of data. **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)** reverses this logic.

- **The Scenario:** We have observed some data ($x_1, x_2, ..., x_n$), but we do not know the value of the parameter ($\theta$) that generated it.
    
- **The Goal:** We want to find the specific value of $\theta$ that makes the observed data **most probable** (most likely to have occurred).
    
- **The Intuition:**
    
    - _Scenario:_ You flip a coin 100 times and get 99 Heads.
        
    - _Hypothesis A:_ The coin is fair ($p=0.5$). Probability of data $\approx 0$.
        
    - _Hypothesis B:_ The coin is weighted ($p=0.99$). Probability of data is high.
        
    - _Conclusion:_ We choose $\hat{p}=0.99$ as our estimate because it maximizes the likelihood of seeing 99 heads.
        

---

### **2. The Likelihood Function ($L$)**

To formalize this, we distinguish between "Probability" and "Likelihood." They look mathematically identical but have different inputs.

- **Probability Function $f(x | \theta)$:** Fixed parameter $\theta$, Variable data $x$. (Predicting future data).
    
- **Likelihood Function $L(\theta | x)$:** Fixed data $x$, Variable parameter $\theta$. (Estimating past causes).
    

For independent observations, the joint likelihood is the product of individual probabilities:

$$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i | \theta)$$

---

### **3. The Log-Likelihood ($\ell$)**

Maximizing a product of many small probabilities (e.g., $0.5 \times 0.4 \times ...$) is computationally difficult because the number becomes vanishingly small.

- **The Trick:** We take the **Natural Logarithm ($\ln$)** of the Likelihood function.
    
- **Why?**
    
    1. Logs turn products into sums ($\ln(AB) = \ln A + \ln B$), which are easier to differentiate.
        
    2. The Logarithm is a monotonically increasing function. The value of $\theta$ that maximizes $L$ is exactly the same value that maximizes $\ln L$.
        

$$\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln f(x_i | \theta)$$

---

### **4. Derivation: The Binomial Proportion**

This is the classic derivation relevant to Topic 5. Let's prove why $\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}$.

1. Likelihood: For a Binomial experiment with $x$ successes in $n$ trials:
    
    $$L(p) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}$$
    
    (We can ignore the combinatorial constant $\binom{n}{x}$ as it doesn't depend on $p$).
    
2. Log-Likelihood:
    
    $$\ell(p) = x \ln(p) + (n-x) \ln(1-p)$$
    
3. Differentiate: Take the derivative with respect to $p$ (calculus):
    
    $$\frac{d\ell}{dp} = \frac{x}{p} - \frac{n-x}{1-p}$$
    
4. Maximize (Set to 0): To find the peak, set the slope to zero.
    
    $$\frac{x}{p} = \frac{n-x}{1-p}$$
    
    $$x(1-p) = p(n-x)$$
    
    $$x - xp = np - px$$
    
    $$x = np$$
    
5. Solve for $p$:
    
    $$\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}$$
    

- **Conclusion:** The sample proportion is the Maximum Likelihood Estimator for the population proportion.
    

---

### **5. Properties of MLEs**

Why do statisticians prefer MLE over other methods (like Method of Moments)? Because for large sample sizes, MLEs have optimal theoretical properties:

1. **Consistency:** As sample size $n \to \infty$, the estimate $\hat{\theta}$ converges to the true value $\theta$.
    
2. **Efficiency:** MLE has the smallest possible variance of any unbiased estimator (it makes the most use of the data).
    
3. **Invariance:** If $\hat{\theta}$ is the MLE for $\theta$, then $g(\hat{\theta})$ is the MLE for $g(\theta)$. (e.g., If $\hat{\sigma}$ is the MLE for standard deviation, then $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is the MLE for variance).
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Calculus Requirement:** You generally only need basic calculus (Power Rule, Chain Rule) to derive MLEs for this course level.
    
- **Local vs. Global Max:** The derivative finds a critical point. Technically, you should check the second derivative (curvature) to ensure it is a **Maximum** (concave down, second derivative negative) and not a minimum.
    
- **Iterative Solutions:** For simple distributions (Normal, Binomial), we can solve the equation algebraicaly (Closed Form). For complex models like **Logistic Regression** (Topic 15), there is no simple formula. The computer must use iterative guessing algorithms (like Newton-Raphson) to climb the likelihood hill and find the max.
