
# 5.4 Statistical Power in Hypothesis Testing ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Power is arguably the most neglected yet critical concept in research design. It explains why a "non-significant" result doesn't prove the null hypothesis is true (it might just mean your sample was too small). Without understanding power, researchers risk wasting resources on doomed experiments.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** Calculating power by hand is complex and rare in standard exams. However, _conceptual_ questions are very common (e.g., "How does increasing sample size affect Type II error?" or "Define Power").
    

---

### **1. Definition of Statistical Power**

**Statistical Power** is the probability of correctly rejecting a Null Hypothesis when it is, in fact, false. In plain English, it is the ability of a test to detect an effect that actually exists.

- **Notation:** Power $= 1 - \beta$
    
    - $\beta$ (Beta) is the probability of a **Type II Error** (Failing to detect a real effect).
        
- **Goal:** Researchers generally aim for a power of **0.80 (80%)** or higher. This means they accept a 20% chance of missing a real discovery.
    

---

### **2. The Four Pillars of Power**

The power of a statistical test is determined by the interplay of four components. If you change one, the others must shift.

1. **Sample Size ($n$):**
    
    - **Relationship:** Increasing $n$ increases Power.
        
    - _Logic:_ More data reduces the Standard Error, making the distributions narrower. This reduces the overlap between the Null distribution and the Alternative distribution, making it easier to distinguish them.
        
2. **Effect Size ($\delta$):**
    
    - **Relationship:** Larger effect sizes increase Power.
        
    - _Logic:_ It is easier to detect a "massive difference" (e.g., a drug that saves 100% of patients) than a "tiny difference" (e.g., a drug that saves 1% more than placebo).
        
3. **Significance Level ($\alpha$):**
    
    - **Relationship:** Increasing $\alpha$ (e.g., from 0.01 to 0.05) increases Power.
        
    - _Logic:_ By lowering the standard of evidence required to reject $H_0$ (making it easier to claim significance), you catch more real effects. However, you also increase the risk of False Positives (Type I Error).
        
4. **Variability ($\sigma$):**
    
    - **Relationship:** Lower variability (noise) increases Power.
        
    - _Logic:_ If the data is messy and scattered, the "signal" gets lost in the noise.
        

---

### **3. Calculating Power (Conceptual Steps)**

While hand calculation is tedious, understanding the steps illuminates the logic:

1. **Set the Critical Value:** Based on $H_0$ and your chosen $\alpha$ (e.g., $Z = 1.96$), calculate the specific cutoff value (e.g., $\bar{x}_{crit}$) in the raw units of the data.
    
2. **Shift to $H_1$:** Assume the Alternative Hypothesis is true (e.g., the true mean is actually $\mu_1$, not $\mu_0$).
    
3. **Calculate Probability:** Find the probability of obtaining a result _beyond_ that critical value $\bar{x}_{crit}$ under the _new_ distribution ($\mu_1$). This area is the Power ($1-\beta$).
    

---

### **4. Post-Hoc vs. A Priori Power Analysis**

- **A Priori (Before Study):** _Essential._
    
    - "I want to detect a 5% difference with 80% power. How many subjects ($n$) do I need?"
        
    - This is used to determine the budget and feasibility of a study.
        
- **Post-Hoc (After Study):** _Controversial._
    
    - "I didn't find a significant result. Was my power too low?"
        
    - Academic statisticians often discourage this because the calculated power will _always_ be low if the P-value is high. It is circular reasoning.
        

---

### **5. Knowledge Gap Fill: Effect Size Metrics (Cohen's $h$)**

When dealing with proportions (the theme of Topic 5), we cannot just subtract them ($p_1 - p_2$) to get effect size because the variance changes with $p$.

- Cohen's $h$: A transformation used to measure the effect size difference between two proportions.
    
    $$h = 2 \arcsin(\sqrt{p_1}) - 2 \arcsin(\sqrt{p_2})$$
    
- **Usage:** This standardized value allows us to use general power tables to determine sample sizes for proportion tests.
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **The Trade-off:** There is an unavoidable trade-off between $\alpha$ (Type I Error) and $\beta$ (Type II Error). You cannot minimize both simultaneously unless you increase the sample size ($n$).
    
- **Ethics:** Running an underpowered study (e.g., Power = 30%) is often considered **unethical** in medical research. You are exposing subjects to risks and using resources with a high probability of learning nothing (finding a false negative).
    
- **One-Tailed Tests:** Switching from a two-tailed test to a one-tailed test **increases power** (because you put all your $\alpha$ rejection risk in the one direction you care about). This is why researchers are tempted to use one-tailed tests, even when they shouldn't.
