# 5.1 Fisher's Exact Test of Equality for Two Proportions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This test represents the "gold standard" for accuracy in categorical data analysis. Unlike the Chi-Squared test, which is an approximation, Fisher's test provides the _exact_ probability. Understanding it reinforces the connection between the Hypergeometric distribution (Topic 4.5) and Hypothesis Testing.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While computationally intensive for large numbers, exams often feature small-sample problems (e.g., $N < 20$) where you must demonstrate the logic of "summing tail probabilities." It is also a favorite for conceptual questions asking, _"Why is Chi-squared invalid here?"_
    

---

### **1. Introduction: The Small Sample Problem**

When comparing two proportions (e.g., Success rates in Group A vs. Group B), the standard method is the **Chi-Squared Test** (Subtopic 5.2). However, that test relies on the Central Limit Theorem to approximate the data as Normal.

**The Problem:** If the sample size is small—specifically, if **any expected cell count is less than 5**—the approximation breaks down, and the Chi-Squared test yields inaccurate P-values (Type I errors).

**The Solution:** Fisher's Exact Test. It calculates the _exact_ probability of obtaining the observed data (and more extreme data) under the Null Hypothesis, without relying on any infinite-sample approximations.

---

### **2. The Contingency Table Setup**

Consider a standard $2 \times 2$ table comparing two groups.

||**Success**|**Failure**|**Row Total**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Group 1**|$a$|$b$|$R_1$|
|**Group 2**|$c$|$d$|$R_2$|
|**Col Total**|$C_1$|$C_2$|$N$|

- **Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):** The proportions are equal ($p_1 = p_2$). There is no association between the Group and the Outcome.
    
- **Assumption:** Under $H_0$, the marginal totals ($R_1, R_2, C_1, C_2$) are considered **fixed**.
    

---

### **3. The Mathematical Mechanism**

Fisher's test treats this setup as a **Hypergeometric Experiment** (Topic 4.5).

- Imagine putting all $N$ subjects in a box.
    
- $C_1$ of them are "Successes."
    
- You randomly draw $R_1$ subjects to be in "Group 1."
    
- What is the probability that exactly $a$ of them are successes?
    

The Formula:

The probability of observing a specific table configuration (with cell value $a$) is:

$$P(X=a) = \frac{\binom{R_1}{a} \binom{R_2}{c}}{\binom{N}{C_1}}$$

Alternatively, using factorials (easier for calculators):

$$P = \frac{R_1! R_2! C_1! C_2!}{N! a! b! c! d!}$$

---

### **4. Calculating the P-Value**

This is the tricky part that students often miss. The P-value is **not** just the probability of the _observed_ table. It is the sum of probabilities of the observed table **plus all more extreme tables**.

**Procedure:**

1. **Identify the Observed Table.** Calculate its probability $P_{obs}$.
    
2. **Generate More Extreme Tables.** Keep the marginal totals fixed, but decrease the smallest cell value ($a$ or $b$ or $c$ or $d$) towards 0 to create tables that show an even stronger difference between the groups.
    
3. **Calculate Probabilities for Extreme Tables.** Calculate $P_{extreme1}, P_{extreme2}, ...$
    
4. Sum Them:
    
    $$P\text{-value} = P_{obs} + \sum P_{more\_extreme}$$
    

---

### **5. Example Scenario**

- **Data:**
    
    - Group A: 1 Success, 4 Failures (Total 5).
        
    - Group B: 4 Successes, 1 Failure (Total 5).
        
    - Total Successes = 5. Total N = 10.
        
- **Question:** Is Group B significantly better?
    
- Observed Table ($a=1$):
    
    $$P(a=1) = \frac{\binom{5}{1}\binom{5}{4}}{\binom{10}{5}} = \frac{5 \times 5}{252} \approx 0.099$$
    
- More Extreme Table ($a=0$):
    
    Group A has 0 successes; Group B has 5.
    
    $$P(a=0) = \frac{\binom{5}{0}\binom{5}{5}}{\binom{10}{5}} = \frac{1 \times 1}{252} \approx 0.004$$
    
- **P-Value (One-Sided):** $0.099 + 0.004 = 0.103$.
    
- **Conclusion:** Since $0.103 > 0.05$, we fail to reject the Null. The difference is not statistically significant.
    

---

### **Important Notes, Tips & Facts**

- **Two-Tailed Complexity:** For a two-tailed test (testing for _any_ difference), you must sum the probabilities of extreme tables in _both_ directions. A common rule of thumb in exams is to simply calculate the one-sided P-value and multiply it by 2 (though this is only exact if the sampling distribution is symmetric).
    
- **Computational Limit:** Calculating factorials for large numbers (e.g., $100!$) causes calculator overflow errors. This is why we switch to Chi-Squared for larger samples.
    
- **"Exact" Meaning:** The word "Exact" refers to the P-value calculation. It does not mean the test is "perfect"; it just means the math is derived directly from discrete probability theory rather than an asymptotic curve.
