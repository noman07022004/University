## 3. Equitable Remedies ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ 

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4):** This topic bridges the gap between abstract equitable theories and practical courtroom results. It explains _what_ you actually get from an Equity court.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5):** Extremely high priority. In problem questions, the ultimate question is almost always "What remedy can the plaintiff get?" Understanding the specific conditions for these remedies is crucial for scoring high.

---

If the Common Law is known for "Damages" (monetary compensation), Equity is known for **Specific Relief**. The Common Law asks, "How much is the loss worth in money?" Equity asks, "How can we compel the defendant to do the right thing?" Equitable remedies are the "teeth" of Equity. They are coercive, discretionary, and powerful tools used when money is an inadequate solution.

---

### I. Nature and Scope of Equitable Remedies

_(Covering Subtopic: 3.1 Nature and scope of equitable remedies)_

The nature of equitable remedies differs fundamentally from Common Law remedies (like damages).

**1. Discretionary, Not Mandatory**

- **Common Law:** If a plaintiff proves a breach of contract, they have a _right_ to damages. The judge _must_ award them, even if it's a nominal amount.
    
- **Equity:** Even if a plaintiff proves a wrong, the judge is _not_ obligated to grant an equitable remedy (like specific performance). The judge will look at the conduct of both parties (remember the "Clean Hands" maxim). If granting the remedy would cause undue hardship to the defendant, the court may refuse it and award damages instead.
    

**2. Acts _In Personam_**

- Equitable remedies are directed at the _person_ (conscience) of the defendant. If a defendant refuses to obey an Injunction or Specific Performance order, they can be held in **Contempt of Court** and imprisoned until they comply.
    

**3. Supplemental(পরিপূরক)**

- Equitable remedies are generally granted only when **Common Law remedies (damages) are inadequate**. If money can fully fix the problem, Equity will not step in.
    

---

### II. Specific Performance

_(Covering Subtopic: 3.2 Specific performance)_

**1. Definition** Specific Performance is a judicial order compelling(বাধ্য করা) a party to perform their contractual obligations _exactly_ as promised. Instead of paying for the breach, the party must "fix" the breach by doing what they agreed to do.

**2. When is it Granted? (The "Adequacy" Test)** The court grants Specific Performance when "damages are not an adequate remedy."

- **Land/Real Estate:** Every piece of land is considered unique. Money cannot buy the exact same view or location. Therefore, Specific Performance is almost _always_ granted for contracts to sell land.
    
- **Unique Goods:** Rare antiques, original paintings, or heirlooms where market substitutes do not exist.
    

**3. When is it Refused?**

- **Personal Services:** The court will never force someone to work for another (e.g., a singer to perform, an employee to work), as this would be akin to slavery.
    
- **Constant Supervision:** If the contract requires the court to constantly check if the work is being done correctly (e.g., a complex construction contract), the court usually refuses.
    
- **Mutuality:** Historically, if the specific performance couldn't be enforced against the plaintiff, it wouldn't be enforced against the defendant (though this rule has softened).
    

---

### III. Injunction

_(Covering Subtopic: 3.3 Injunction)_

**1. Definition** An injunction is a judicial process or order requiring a person to do, or refrain from doing, a particular act. It is the "shield" of Equity.

**2. Classification of Injunctions** Injunctions are broadly classified based on _time_ and _nature_.

- **Based on Time:**
    
    - **Temporary (Interlocutory) Injunction:** Granted _during_ the lawsuit to preserve the "status quo" until the final decision. (e.g., "Don't knock down the building until we decide who owns it").
        
    - **Perpetual (Permanent) Injunction:** Granted _after_ the final hearing as part of the final judgment. It lasts forever.
        
- **Based on Nature:**
    
    - **Prohibitory Injunction:** Forbids a person from doing something (e.g., "Do not build a wall here"). This is the most common type.
        
    - **Mandatory Injunction:** Compels a person to _undo_ a wrong or perform a positive act (e.g., "Demolish the wall you illegally built"). This is rarer and requires stronger evidence.
        

---

### IV. Receiver

_(Covering Subtopic: 3.4 Receiver)_

**1. Definition** A Receiver is an impartial person appointed by the court to collect and receive the rents, issues, and profits of land or personal estate. They act as an officer of the court, not an agent of the parties.

**2. Purpose (Protection of Property)** The primary object is the **preservation** of the property in dispute. If two parties are fighting over a factory, and the current manager is ruining the business or stealing funds, the court appoints a Receiver to "hold" and manage the assets until the court decides the winner.

**3. Principle: "Just and Convenient"** The court appoints a receiver only when it appears to be "just and convenient." It is a harsh remedy because it takes possession away from the defendant before the trial is even finished, so courts are cautious.

    - **Injunction** = Preventing a tort or breach of contract.
