## 3.2 Specific Performance ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** This is the flagship remedy of Equity. It represents the most direct intervention of the court into a contract—forcing a person to keep their word.  
>- **Exam (⭐ 5):** This is statistically the most frequent exam topic in the entire course. Problem questions usually involve a breach of contract where the plaintiff wants the land/item, not money, and you must argue whether Specific Performance applies.
    

---

### I. Definition and Nature

**Specific Performance** is an equitable remedy in the form of a court order compelling a party to perform their contractual obligations.

- **Goal:** To place the parties in the physical position they would have been in if the contract had been performed, rather than just compensating them for the loss.
    
- **Maxims Involved:** It is grounded in the maxims: _“Equity looks on that as done which ought to be done”_ and _“Equity acts in personam.”_
    

---

### II. The Basic Principle: Adequacy of Damages

The golden rule for Specific Performance is simple: **It is only granted where Common Law damages are an inadequate remedy.**

**1. Contracts for Land (Real Property)**

- **Rule:** Damages are _presumed_ to be inadequate.
    
- **Reasoning:** Every piece of land is considered unique (location, view, neighbors). Even if you get money, you cannot buy an identical plot.
    
- **Result:** Specific Performance is almost always granted for the sale or lease of land.
    

**2. Contracts for Goods (Personal Property)**

- **Rule:** Damages are usually _presumed_ to be adequate.
    
- **Reasoning:** Ordinary goods (e.g., 100 tons of wheat, a Toyota Corolla) are fungible. If the seller breaches, the buyer can take the money and buy the same goods elsewhere.
    
- **Exception (Unique Goods):** If the goods are rare or unique (e.g., an original Picasso painting, an antique Ming vase, or a machine custom-built for a factory that cannot be found elsewhere), Specific Performance will be granted.
    

---

### III. When Specific Performance is Refused (Defenses)

Even if damages are inadequate, the court may still refuse Specific Performance based on discretionary grounds.

**1. Contracts for Personal Services**

- **Rule:** The court will never force a person to work for another (e.g., to sing, paint, or serve as an employee).
    
- **Reasoning:**
    
    - It would constitute a form of judicial slavery.
        
    - It would require the court to constantly judge the quality of the work (e.g., "Is she singing well enough?").
        
- _Case:_ _Lumley v Wagner (1852)_ – The court refused to force an opera singer to perform, though it did grant an injunction to stop her from singing elsewhere.
    

**2. Constant Supervision**

- **Rule:** The court will not enforce contracts that require continuous supervision over a long period.
    
- **Example:** A complex building contract. If the builder refuses to build, the court won't stand over him for 6 months to ensure he lays every brick correctly.
    

**3. Want of Mutuality**

- **Rule (Historical):** Specific Performance is not granted to the plaintiff unless the defendant could also have enforced it against the plaintiff.
    
- **Application:** A minor cannot get Specific Performance because the other party cannot enforce a contract against a minor.
    

**4. Severe Hardship**

- **Rule:** If enforcing the contract would cause unforeseen and excessive hardship to the defendant (far outweighing the benefit to the plaintiff), the court may refuse it.
    
- _Example:_ If compelling a sale would leave a defendant with a severe disability homeless, and the plaintiff just wants the land for investment.
    

---

### IV. The Doctrine of Part Performance

This is a sub-concept often linked to Specific Performance.

- **Statute of Frauds:** Historically, contracts for land had to be in writing.
    
- **The Problem:** Sometimes parties made an oral agreement, and one party started performing (e.g., moved in, paid rent), but then the other party tried to use the "lack of writing" to cheat them.
    
- **Equity's Solution:** If the plaintiff has performed a substantial part of their obligations in reliance on the contract, Equity will order Specific Performance _even if_ the written formalities are missing, to prevent fraud.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Link to Section 12 (SRA 1877):** When writing about the "Adequacy of Damages," explicitly mention that in Bangladesh, this is codified in **Section 12 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877**.
    
- **Pusey v Pusey (1684):** A classic case to cite for unique goods. The court granted Specific Performance for the return of an ancient horn (an heirloom) because it was unique and irreplaceable.
    
- **Problem Question Strategy:**
    
    1. **Identify the Subject Matter:** Is it Land (Yes SP) or Goods (Usually No SP)?
        
    2. **Check for Bars:** Is it a service contract? Does it require supervision?
        
    3. **Check Conduct:** Does the plaintiff have clean hands?
        
    4. **Conclude:** "Therefore, the court will likely grant/refuse specific performance."
