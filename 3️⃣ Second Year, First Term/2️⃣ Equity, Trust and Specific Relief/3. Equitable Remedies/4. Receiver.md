## 3.4 Receiver ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 3):** This is a procedural but powerful remedy. It represents the court taking physical control of assets. It’s less "theoretical" than specific performance but critical for practical litigation (preserving the pot of gold while the parties fight over it).
>- **Exam (⭐ 3):** Receivers are rarely the subject of a full essay question. They typically appear as "Short Notes" or as a small part of a problem question (e.g., "Also, advise X on how to stop Y from draining the company accounts").

---

### I. Definition and Nature

A **Receiver** is an impartial and independent person appointed by the court to collect and receive the rents, issues, and profits of land or personal estate.

- **Status:** The Receiver is an **Officer of the Court**. They are _not_ an agent of the plaintiff or the defendant. They act on behalf of the court to hold the property for the benefit of whoever ultimately wins the case.
    
- **Metaphor:** The Receiver is often called the **"Hand of the Court."** Through the Receiver, the court extends its arm to take possession of the property.
    
- **Source of Law:** The principle is equitable (protecting property), but the appointment mechanism in Bangladesh is governed by **Section 44** of the _Specific Relief Act, 1877_, which refers directly to **Order 40** of the _Code of Civil Procedure (CPC), 1908_.
    

---

### II. Object and Purpose

The primary object of appointing a receiver is the **preservation** of the subject matter of the litigation _pendente lite_ (while the lawsuit is pending).

- **Prevention of Wrongs:** It prevents a party in possession from destroying, damaging, or wasting the property.
    
- **Security:** It secures the funds/property so that if the plaintiff wins, there is actually something left to claim.
    
- **Example:** Two brothers are fighting over a family factory. The brother currently running it is selling off the machines and keeping the cash. The court appoints a Receiver to take over the factory, manage the accounts, and stop the theft until the judge decides who the real owner is.
    

---

### III. Principles of Appointment ("Just and Convenient")

The court exercises this power only when it appears **"Just and Convenient."** Because removing a person from possession _before_ a trial is finished is a harsh step, courts follow the "Five Principles" (often derived from the case _T. Krishnaswamy Chetty v. C. Thangavelu Chetty_):

1. **Bona Fide Claim:** The plaintiff must show a strong _prima facie_ case that they have a good title to the property.
    
2. **Danger to Property:** There must be a well-founded fear that the property will be dissipated, wasted, or damaged if left with the defendant.
    
3. **No Alternative:** The plaintiff must show that no other remedy (like a temporary injunction) is sufficient to protect the property.
    
4. **Clean Hands:** The plaintiff must come to court with clean hands.
    
5. **Balance of Convenience:** The court assesses whether the injury to the plaintiff (if receiver is refused) outweighs the injury to the defendant (if receiver is appointed).
    

---

### IV. Powers and Duties

Once appointed, the Receiver has specific powers and obligations under the direction of the court.

**1. Powers (Conferred by Court)**

- To institute and defend suits concerning the property.
    
- To realize (collect) rents, dividends, or profits.
    
- To execute documents (leases, receipts).
    
- _Note:_ A Receiver cannot generally sell the property without specific permission from the court.
    

**2. Duties (Liabilities)**

- **Furnish Security:** They must usually post a bond/security to cover any loss they might cause.
    
- **Submit Accounts:** They must file regular accounts of all money received and spent.
    
- **Pay Balances:** They must pay the money into the court treasury as directed.
    
- **Personal Liability:** If the Receiver causes loss to the property through their own **willful default or gross negligence**, they must compensate the estate personally.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **SRA Section 44:** In exams, always cite **Section 44 of the Specific Relief Act**. Even though it just says "Go look at the CPC," citing the SRA shows you understand the equitable context of the syllabus.
    
- **Distinction from Injunction:**
    
    - _Injunction:_ Orders a party to stop doing something (Defendant stays in possession, but is restricted).
        
    - _Receiver:_ Removes the party from possession entirely (Court takes over). Receiver is a much more drastic remedy.
        
- **"Officer of the Court":** This is the key phrase. If anyone interferes with a Receiver (e.g., threatens them, refuses to hand over keys), they are interfering with the Court itself and can be punished for **Contempt of Court**.
