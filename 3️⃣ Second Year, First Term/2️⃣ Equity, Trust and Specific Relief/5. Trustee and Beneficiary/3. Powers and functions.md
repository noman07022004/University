# 5.3 Powers and Functions of Trustees ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4):** While duties are about what a trustee _must_ do, powers are about what they _can_ do. This distinction is vital for understanding the flexibility of a trust. A trustee needs tools (powers) to manage the property effectively.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4):** Questions often ask about the "Power of Maintenance" (can the trustee pay for the beneficiary's school fees?) or the "Power of Sale" (can the trustee sell the house?).
    

---

### I. Distinction: Duties vs. Powers

It is essential to distinguish between these two concepts:

- **Duties (Imperative):** Obligations the trustee _must_ perform (e.g., "Invest the money"). If they fail, it is a breach of trust.
    
- **Powers (Discretionary):** Authorities the trustee _may_ exercise if they think it is fit (e.g., "Sell the land," "Use income for education"). If they choose _not_ to use a power, the court generally will not interfere, provided they considered it honestly.
    

---

### II. Key Powers of Trustees (Trusts Act, 1882)

The **Trusts Act, 1882** grants specific statutory powers to trustees to enable them to manage the trust efficiently.

**1. Power of Sale (Section 37)**

- **The Power:** Where the trustee is empowered to sell any trust property, they may sell it either by public auction or private contract.
    
- **Discretion:** They have the discretion to sell it all at once or in lots.
    
- **Duty:** Even when exercising this power, they must secure the **best available price**. Selling below market value due to negligence is a breach of duty.
    

**2. Power to Convey (Section 39)**

- **The Power:** The trustee has the power to convey (transfer) the property to complete a sale. This is necessary because they hold the Legal Estate.
    

**3. Power to Apply Property for Maintenance (Section 41)**

- **The Power:** This is critical for trusts involving **minors**. If a beneficiary is under 18, the trustee may use the income of the trust property for their **maintenance, education, or advancement** (e.g., paying school fees, medical bills).
    
- **Accumulation:** Any income _not_ used for maintenance must be accumulated (saved) and added to the capital.
    

**4. Power to Compound and Compromise (Section 43)**

- **The Power:** To avoid expensive lawsuits, a trustee may:
    
    - Accept a composition (partial payment) for a debt owed to the trust.
        
    - Allow time for payment of a debt.
        
    - Submit a dispute to arbitration.
        
    - **Compromise** (settle) any claim relating to the trust.
        
- **Condition:** They must do so _bona fide_ (in good faith) for the benefit of the trust. If they settle a 10 Lakh Taka claim for 1 Taka just to be lazy, they are liable.
    

**5. Power to Give Receipts (Section 42)**

- **The Power:** A trustee can give a valid written receipt for any money or property transferred to the trust.
    
- **Effect:** This receipt discharges the payer. (e.g., If a tenant pays rent to the trustee and gets a receipt, the tenant is safe even if the trustee later steals the money).
    

---

### III. Functions of a Trustee

The function of a trustee is broader than specific powers. It acts as a **Manager**.

- **Preservation:** The primary function is to preserve the capital for the "Remainderman" (the person who gets the property at the end).
    
- **Production:** The secondary function is to generate income for the "Life Tenant" (the person who gets the annual profits).
    
- **Balancing Act:** The trustee functions as a neutral referee between conflicting interests of beneficiaries.
    

---

### IV. Control of Powers by Court

While powers are discretionary, they are not absolute.

- **Interference:** The Court will generally **not interfere** with the _bona fide_ exercise of a discretion.
    
    - _Example:_ If a trustee has the power to choose which charity to donate to, and chooses Charity A over Charity B, the court will not stop him just because the judge prefers Charity B.
        
- **Intervention:** The Court **will intervene** if the power is exercised:
    
    - **Mala fide** (in bad faith).
        
    - For an **improper purpose** (e.g., to spite a beneficiary).
        
    - Arbitrarily or capriciously.
        

---

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Section 43 (Compromise):** This is very useful in problem questions where a trustee settles a lawsuit. If they acted reasonably to save legal fees, they are protected by Section 43.
    
- **Maintenance (Section 41):** This is the most "human" power. In exams involving orphan beneficiaries, always discuss the trustee's power to use funds for their **"maintenance and education."**
    
- **"Bona Fide":** This Latin term is the key to all powers. A trustee is safe as long as they act _bona fide_ (honestly).
