
# 5.5 Limitation ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** The interaction between Equity (which dislikes delay) and Statute (which sets rigid time limits) is unique in Trust law. You must understand why a Trustee is the _only_ person in the legal system who cannot use the "I waited too long" defense for theft.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4):** Problem questions often feature a trustee who stole property 20 years ago. You need to know **Section 10 of the Limitation Act** to advise the beneficiary that they can still sue.
    

---

### I. The General Rule vs. The Trust Exception

In ordinary law, "Time heals all wounds." If someone owes you money or takes your land, you must sue them within a fixed period (usually 3 or 12 years). If you wait too long, the **Limitation Act, 1908** bars your remedy.

**The Exception for Trusts:** Equity considers the breach of a fiduciary duty so serious that it refuses to let a dishonest trustee hide behind the clock. This equitable principle is codified in **Section 10 of the Limitation Act, 1908**.

---

### II. Section 10: The "No Limitation" Rule

**1. The Statutory Text**

> _"Notwithstanding anything hereinbefore contained, no suit against a person in whom property has become vested in trust for any specific purpose, or against his legal representatives... for the purpose of following in his or their hands such property... shall be barred by any length of time."_

**2. Meaning** If a trustee steals trust property, they can **never** argue "It is too late to sue me." The beneficiary can sue them 10, 20, or 50 years later. Time does not run in favor of an express trustee.

**3. Conditions for Applying Section 10** For this "forever" rule to apply, three strict conditions must be met:

- **Vested in Trust:** The trustee must have held the legal title (ownership) of the property. It does not apply to a mere manager or agent who didn't own the asset.
    
- **Specific Purpose:** It applies only to **Express Trusts** where the purpose was clearly defined. It generally does not apply to Constructive or Resulting trusts (where the trust arises by accident/law).
    
- **Following the Property:** The suit must be to recover the _trust property itself_ or its _proceeds_ (tracing). It is meant to stop the trustee from keeping what isn't theirs.
    

---

### III. Who is Protected? (Exceptions to the Rule)

While the Trustee is vulnerable forever, third parties are protected.

**1. Assigns for Valuable Consideration (Bona Fide Purchaser)**

- **The Rule:** If the trustee sells the trust property to a third party (X) who pays for it (valuable consideration) and acts in good faith, Section 10 does **not** apply to X.
    
- **Result:** You cannot sue X after the standard limitation period (usually 12 years for land) expires. X is not a trustee; they are an honest buyer.
    

**2. Volunteers (Gifts)**

- If the trustee gives the property to a friend (Y) for _free_ (no consideration), Y stands in the shoes of the trustee. Section 10 applies to Y, and they can be sued indefinitely, just like the trustee.
    

---

### IV. Limitation for Other Suits (When Section 10 doesn't apply)

If the specific conditions of Section 10 are not met (e.g., it is a suit for simple negligence, not for recovering property, or against a Constructive Trustee), standard limitation periods apply under the Schedule of the Limitation Act:

- **Breach of Trust (General):** **3 Years** (Article 98) – For suits to make good the loss occasioned by a breach of trust where Section 10 does not apply.
    
- **Recovery of Possession (Land):** **12 Years** (Article 142/144) – If a stranger (not the trustee) takes the land.
    
- **Account:** **3 Years** – For a suit to compel a trustee to show accounts.
    

---

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **The "Express Trust" Trap:** In exams, if the problem involves a **Constructive Trust** (e.g., a director making a secret profit), argue that **Section 10 might not apply** because it requires a "specific purpose" (Express Trust). Therefore, the limitation is likely **3 years** or **6 years**, not "forever."
    
- **Section 10 vs. Laches:** While the _Limitation Act_ (Statute) says "No Limit," remember that the equitable doctrine of **Laches** (Topic 2.3) can still bar a remedy if the delay caused extreme injustice (e.g., evidence is lost). However, in Bangladesh, where there is a direct Statute (Section 10), the Statute usually overrides Laches for express trusts.
    
- **Legal Representatives:** Note that Section 10 also applies to the **heirs** of the trustee. If the dishonest trustee dies, you can sue his son to recover the stolen property 20 years later (provided the son inherited the estate).
