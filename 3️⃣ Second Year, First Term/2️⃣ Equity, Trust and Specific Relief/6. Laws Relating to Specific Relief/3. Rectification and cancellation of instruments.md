
## 6.3 Rectification and Cancellation of Instruments ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4):** Mistake and fraud are common in legal documents. This topic explains the "clean-up" operation: how to fix a typo that changes a million-dollar contract (Rectification) or how to destroy a forged deed that threatens your title (Cancellation).
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4):** "Distinguish between Rectification and Cancellation" is a classic exam question. You must also know _Section 31_ (Rectification) and _Section 39_ (Cancellation) for problem questions.
    

### Introduction

In the course of legal transactions, written documents ("instruments") sometimes fail to record the true agreement between parties, or they may be void/voidable. The **Specific Relief Act (SRA), 1877** provides two distinct remedies to handle these defective documents: **Rectification** (fixing the error) and **Cancellation** (destroying the document).

---

### I. Rectification of Instruments (Sections 31-34)

1. Definition and Object

Rectification is the equitable process of "correcting" a written instrument so that it reflects the true intention of the contracting parties. The court does not make a new contract; it merely makes the written document match the actual oral agreement.

2. Grounds for Rectification (Section 31)

A suit for rectification can be filed only if two specific grounds exist:

1. **Fraud:** One party tricked the other into signing a document that didn't match their agreement.
    
2. **Mutual Mistake:** _Both_ parties made a mistake in writing down the terms (e.g., both agreed to sell "Plot A," but the typist wrote "Plot B").
    
    - _Crucial Note:_ A **Unilateral Mistake** (one party made a mistake, the other knew it and stayed quiet) is generally _not_ a ground for rectification, unless it amounts to fraud.
        

**3. Who Can Sue?**

- Either party to the instrument.
    
- Their representative in interest (heirs/assignees).
    

**4. Principles of Rectification**

- **Burden of Proof:** It lies heavily on the plaintiff to prove that the written document is wrong and that _both_ parties intended something else. The evidence must be "clear and convincing."
    
- **No Prejudice:** The court will not rectify an instrument if it would prejudice the rights acquired by a third party in good faith and for value (Bona Fide Purchaser).
    

---

### II. Cancellation of Instruments (Sections 39-41)

1. Definition and Object

Cancellation is a remedy where the court declares a written document to be void or voidable and orders it to be "delivered up and cancelled" (physically nullified). Its object is Quia Timet ("because he fears")—to prevent future injury.

2. When Can Cancellation be Ordered? (Section 39)

Any person against whom a written instrument is void or voidable may sue to have it adjudged void or voidable if:

1. **Void or Voidable:** The document is legally invalid (e.g., a forged deed, a contract signed under coercion).
    
2. **Reasonable Apprehension of Injury:** The plaintiff must show that if the instrument is left outstanding, it may cause him **serious injury** in the future.
    
    - _Example:_ A forged deed showing someone else owns your house. Even if they aren't evicting you _now_, if you leave it alone, 20 years later your evidence might be lost, and the deed could be used to take your home.
        

3. Partial Cancellation (Section 40)

Where an instrument is evidence of different rights or obligations, the court may cancel it in part and let the rest stand.

- _Example:_ A forged will gives Property X to A and Property Y to B. If only the gift to A is proven forged, the court can cancel that part and leave B's gift valid.
    

4. Power to Require Compensation (Section 41)

This section embodies the maxim: "He who seeks equity must do equity."

- **The Rule:** On adjudging the cancellation of an instrument, the court may require the plaintiff to make any **compensation** to the other party which justice may require.
    
- _Example:_ A minor (under 18) fraudulently mortgages his house to borrow 10 Lakh Taka. He later sues to cancel the mortgage deed because "minors cannot sign contracts." The court will cancel the deed (because the law says a minor's contract is void), but under **Section 41**, it will order the minor to **refund the 10 Lakh Taka** to the lender.
    

---

### III. Distinction: Rectification vs. Cancellation

This comparison is vital for exam clarity.

|**Feature**|**Rectification (Sec 31)**|**Cancellation (Sec 39)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Objective**|To _correct_ a mistake and enforce the true intent.|To _destroy_ a dangerous/invalid document.|
|**Status of Document**|The agreement is valid, but the _writing_ is wrong.|The document itself is _void_ or _voidable_.|
|**Grounds**|Fraud or Mutual Mistake.|Void/Voidable + Fear of Serious Injury.|
|**Result**|The document survives in a corrected form.|The document is declared dead/ineffective.|

---

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Section 41 (Compensation):** This is the most practical exam tool. If a problem involves a **Minor** cancelling a contract, you _must_ cite Section 41. It is the only way to make a minor pay back money they received unjustly.
    
- **Mutual Mistake:** In Rectification questions, verify if the mistake was **mutual**.
    
    - _Scenario:_ A intends to sell Plot X. B intends to buy Plot Y. They sign a contract for Plot Y.
        
    - _Analysis:_ This is _not_ mutual mistake (they didn't agree on the same thing). There is no "true agreement" to rectify back to. The remedy here is Rescission (Cancellation) because there was no consensus _ad idem_.
        
- **Presumption of Validity:** Courts presume written documents are correct. Rectification is hard to get. You need strong evidence (like an earlier draft contract) to prove the error.
