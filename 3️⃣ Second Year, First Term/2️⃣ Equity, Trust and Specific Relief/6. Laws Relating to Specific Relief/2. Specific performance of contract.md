
# 6.2 Specific Performance of Contract ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** This is the core of the Specific Relief Act. It defines _when_ the state will force a person to keep their word. It requires balancing the sanctity of contract with practical limits (e.g., you can't force someone to paint a picture against their will).
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5):** This is arguably the most important section for problem-based questions. You will frequently be asked to advise a client on whether they can force a sale of land or goods under Section 12, or why their claim might fail under Section 21.
    

### Introduction

**Specific Performance** is an equitable remedy where the Court orders a party to perform their contractual obligations _in specie_ (in the specific form agreed upon). In Bangladesh, this is governed by **Chapter II (Sections 12–30)** of the **Specific Relief Act (SRA), 1877**. Unlike the Contract Act which focuses on compensation (damages), these sections focus on execution.

---

### I. When Specific Performance is Granted (Section 12)

Section 12 is the "Gateway" to specific performance. The Court may enforce the specific performance of a contract in four specific scenarios:

1. **Trust:** When the act agreed to be done is in the performance wholly or partly of a trust.
    
2. **No Standard for Damage:** When there exists no standard for ascertaining the actual damage caused by the non-performance.
    
    - _Example:_ A agrees to buy a rare antique vase. If the seller refuses, how much money is "equal" to the vase? There is no market standard.
        
3. **Inadequate Damages:** When the act agreed to be done is such that pecuniary compensation (money) for its non-performance would **not afford adequate relief**.
    
4. **Unlikely to Recover:** When it is probable that pecuniary compensation cannot be got for the non-performance (e.g., the defendant is insolvent).
    

The Statutory Presumptions (Explanation to Sec 12):

To make the judge's life easier, the Act creates two legal presumptions:

- **Immovable Property (Land/Buildings):** The law presumes that money is **NOT** an adequate remedy. Land is unique. Therefore, specific performance is the _rule_ for land contracts.
    
- **Movable Property (Goods):** The law presumes that money **IS** an adequate remedy. Therefore, specific performance is the _exception_ (granted only for unique goods).
    

---

### II. Contracts Which Cannot be Specifically Enforced (Section 21)

Even if a contract is valid, the Court will **refuse** specific performance if it falls under one of the 8 categories in **Section 21**. This is the "Defense" section.

1. **Monetary Compensation Adequate:** If money is enough to fix the loss (e.g., a contract to sell 100 tons of ordinary rice), specific performance is denied.
    
2. **Personal Services:** Contracts which run into such minute or numerous details, or which are so dependent on the **personal qualifications** or volition of the parties.
    
    - _Example:_ A contract to write a book, paint a portrait, or sing at a concert. The court cannot stand over an artist and say "Paint better!"
        
3. **Uncertain Terms:** A contract whose terms the Court cannot find with reasonable certainty. (e.g., "I will sell you my land for a good price").
    
4. **Revocable Nature:** A contract which is in its nature revocable. (e.g., A partnership at will. If the court forces them to partner today, they can legally dissolve it tomorrow, making the order futile).
    
5. **Breach of Trust:** A contract made by trustees in excess of their powers or in breach of their trust.
    
6. **Ultra Vires:** A contract made by a corporation in excess of its powers.
    
7. **Continuous Duty:** A contract involving the performance of a continuous duty extending over a longer period than three years (e.g., a long-term maintenance contract).
    
8. **Non-Existent Subject Matter:** If a material part of the subject matter has ceased to exist before the contract (e.g., the house burned down before the sale).
    

---

### III. Discretion of the Court (Section 22)

One of the most vital principles of the SRA is that **"The jurisdiction to decree specific performance is discretionary."**

- **Not a Right:** The court is not bound to grant specific performance merely because it is lawful to do so.
    
- **Judicial Discretion:** The discretion must be sound and reasonable, guided by judicial principles, not arbitrary.
    

**When Discretion is exercised to REFUSE relief:**

1. **Unfair Advantage:** Where the plaintiff has an unfair advantage over the defendant (even if there is no fraud).
    
2. **Hardship:** Where performing the contract would involve some hardship on the defendant which he did not foresee, whereas its non-performance would involve no such hardship on the plaintiff.
    

**When Discretion is exercised to GRANT relief:**

- Where the plaintiff has done substantial acts or suffered losses in consequence of a contract capable of specific performance.
    

---

### IV. Who Can Obtain Specific Performance? (Section 23)

Generally, only the parties to the contract can sue. However, Section 23 expands this to:

- Any party to the contract.
    
- The **Representative in interest** (heirs/assignees) of any party (unless the contract is personal, like painting).
    
- The **Beneficiary**, in contracts of settlement on marriage or compromise of doubtful rights.
    
- A **Remainderman**, in certain land settlements.
    

---

### V. Who Cannot Obtain Specific Performance? (Section 24)

Specific performance of a contract cannot be enforced in favor of a person:

1. **Who could not recover compensation:** If they breached the contract themselves.
    
2. **Who acts negligently:** Who has become incapable of performing their part or violates an essential term.
    
3. **Who has already settled:** Who has chosen to accept satisfaction (money) for the breach already.
    

---

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Section 12 vs. Section 21:** This is the most common essay structure. " Discuss the circumstances under which specific performance is granted (Sec 12) and refused (Sec 21)." Memorize at least 4 points from Section 21.
    
- **Personal Bar (Sec 24):** This is crucial for "Clean Hands." If the plaintiff is the one who failed to pay the money on time (violating an essential term), they cannot ask the court to force the other party to sell the land.
    
- **Delay (Laches):** Always mention that because Specific Performance is an _equitable_ remedy, delay defeats equity. If you wait 2 years to sue for land without a good reason, the court may refuse under its discretionary power (Sec 22), even if the limitation period (1 year under Article 113) hasn't technically passed.
    
- **Mutuality:** Historically, "Mutuality" was required (if I can sue you, you must be able to sue me). While SRA modifies this, the principle generally remains that specific performance shouldn't be one-sided.
