# 1. Concept of Equity ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** This is the foundational bedrock of the entire course. Without grasping the historical conflict between Common Law and Equity, you cannot understand why specific relief or trusts exist.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4):** This topic frequently appears in exams, particularly as essay questions regarding the history, the nature of equitable jurisdiction, or the application of equity in the Bangladeshi legal system.
    

---

In a jurisprudential sense, "Equity" refers to a body of rules and principles that developed in England to mitigate the rigidity and harshness of the Common Law. It is not synonymous with general fairness or natural justice in the abstract, but rather a specific system of legal rules administered by the Court of Chancery. Equity does not destroy the law; rather, it supplements it. As the great legal historian Frederic William Maitland famously noted, "Equity was a gloss on the common law," filling the gaps where the law was too formalistic to provide a just remedy.

---

### I. The Genesis: Common Law and the Rise of Equity

_(Covering Subtopics: 1.1 Common law, 1.2 Origin and growth of equity)_

To understand Equity, one must first understand the system it was born to correct: the **Common Law**.

#### 1. The Rigidity of Common Law 
Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, a unified system of law—Common Law—emerged in England. While efficient, it became increasingly ossified by the 13th and 14th centuries. It suffered from three major defects:

- **The Writ System:** A plaintiff could only bring a case if their complaint fit into a specific, existing "writ" (a royal order). If a wrong did not fit a standard writ, the Common Law courts offered no remedy.
    
- **Inadequate Remedies:** The Common Law generally only offered "damages" (monetary compensation). It could not force a defendant to perform a contract or stop a harmful act.
    
- **Strict Formalism:** Cases were often lost due to minor technical errors in drafting or procedure, regardless of the merits of the case.
    

#### 2. Origin and Growth of Equity 
Aggrieved citizens who could not get justice in Common Law courts began petitioning the King, who was considered the "Fountain of Justice." The King delegated these petitions to his **Lord Chancellor**, a high-ranking official often referred to as the "Keeper of the King’s Conscience."

- **The Early Stage (Medieval Period):** The Chancellor, usually a clergyman, decided cases not based on binding legal precedent but on "conscience" and "Christian morality." He looked at the intent rather than the form.
    
- **The Middle Stage (Systematization):** As lawyers replaced clergymen as Chancellors (notably Sir Thomas More and Lord Nottingham), Equity began to develop its own precedents and rules. It transformed from an arbitrary decision-making process into a structured legal system.
    
- **The Conflict:** The growth of Equity caused friction with Common Law judges. This culminated in the _Earl of Oxford’s Case_ (1615), where King James I ruled that **where Law and Equity conflict, Equity shall prevail.**

### II. Nature and Definition

_(Covering Subtopic: 1.3 Nature and definition)_

#### 1. Definition 

Defining Equity is complex because it is historical in origin.

- **Aristotle’s Definition:** In _Nicomachean Ethics_, Aristotle defined "Epikeia" (Equity) as a correction of legal justice. The law speaks in general terms, but human affairs are variable; Equity steps in to correct the law where it is defective due to its universality.
    
- **Maitland’s Definition:** Equity is "that body of rules which is administered by our courts of justice which, were it not for the operation of the Judicature Acts, would be administered only by those courts which would be known as Courts of Equity."
    

#### 2. Nature of Equity

- **Discretionary:** Unlike Common Law, which is often mandatory ("if X happens, Y is the penalty"), equitable remedies (like injunctions) are discretionary. The court grants them only if it is just to do so.
    
- **In Personam:** Common Law acts _in rem_ (against property/things), but Equity acts _in personam_ (against the person). Equity compels the individual's conscience to do right.
    
- **Supplemental:** Equity does not claim to replace the law but to fulfill it.

### III. Institutional Framework: Courts of Equity

_(Covering Subtopic: 1.4 Courts of Equity: Composition, powers and functions)_

Historically, the administration of Equity was distinct from the Common Law courts (like the King's Bench).

#### 1. Composition 
The primary Court of Equity was the **Court of Chancery**.

- **The Chancellor:** The presiding judge and head of the court.
    
- **Master of the Rolls:** Originally the keeper of records, later became a deputy judge to assist the Chancellor.
    
- **Vice-Chancellors:** Appointed later as the caseload grew to assist with the administration of justice.
    

#### 2. Powers and Functions 
The Court of Chancery had exclusive and concurrent jurisdiction:

- **Exclusive Jurisdiction:** Matters where Common Law offered _no_ relief (e.g., Trusts, where the legal owner is different from the beneficial owner).
    
- **Concurrent Jurisdiction:** Matters where Common Law offered relief (damages), but it was inadequate (e.g., Specific Performance for breach of contract involving land).
    
- **Auxiliary Jurisdiction:** Helping Common Law courts by providing procedural aid, such as "Discovery of Documents" (forcing a party to reveal evidence), which Common Law courts could not originally do.
    

_Note: The separate Courts of Equity were abolished in England by the **Judicature Acts of 1873-1875**, which created a "fused" system where all courts could administer both Law and Equity._

### IV. Application of Equity and Jurisdiction in Bangladesh

_(Covering Subtopics: 1.5 Application of equity, 1.6 Jurisdiction in Bangladesh)_

**1. Application of Equity (General Principles)** Equity applies where the strict letter of the law results in an unconscionable outcome. It applies through specific "Maxims of Equity" (which will be covered in Topic 2) and through specific remedies. It is dynamic but now bound by precedent; judges cannot invent new equity based on personal whims ("Equity is not the length of the Chancellor's foot").

**2. Jurisdiction in Bangladesh** Bangladesh does not have separate "Courts of Equity" or a "Chancery Division." The application of Equity in Bangladesh is unique and codified.

- **Codified Equity:** Many principles of English Equity have been formally written into Bangladeshi statutes. For example:
    
    - **The Trusts Act, 1882:** Codifies the equitable concept of Trust.
        
    - **The Specific Relief Act, 1877:** Codifies equitable remedies like injunctions and specific performance.
        
    - **The Contract Act, 1872:** Incorporates equitable principles regarding undue influence and mistake.
        
    - **The Transfer of Property Act, 1882:** Incorporates the equitable doctrine of part performance.
        
- **Residual Equity (Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience):** Unlike England, Bangladeshi courts are courts of both Law and Equity simultaneously. In situations where there is no specific statutory law covering a dispute, Section 6 of the _Civil Courts Act, 1887_ allows courts to decide cases according to **"Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience."** This clause is the gateway through which English equitable principles enter Bangladeshi law, provided they suit local conditions.

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

>**The Earl of Oxford’s Case (1615):** Memorize this case name. It established the supremacy of Equity over Common Law. "Equity is a goddess, and the law is a creeping thing."
    
>**Fusion:** Remember that in Bangladesh, we never had a "fusion" conflict because our legal system (introduced by the British) combined Law and Equity from the very beginning. We do not have separate courts for them.
    
>**Key Distinction:** Always remember: **Common Law = Right** (I have a right to damages). **Equity = Discretion** (I ask the court to grant me a remedy because damages are not enough).
    
>**Terminology:** When writing exams, use the phrase _"Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience"_ when referring to the source of equitable jurisdiction in Bangladesh.
