# 1.2 Origin and Growth of Equity ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

### I. The Three Stages of Growth

The history of Equity is generally divided into three distinct periods:

#### 1. The Mediaeval Period (Growth of the Chancery)

As discussed in Subtopic 1.1, the Common Law had become rigid and defective.

- **Petitions to the King:** Disappointed litigants who couldn't get justice in the Common Law courts (due to technicalities or the defendant's power) began sending petitions to the King. They appealed to his "Grace" and "Charity" as the "Fountain of Justice."
    
- **Delegation to the Chancellor:** The King was too busy to hear every petition, so he delegated this duty to his **Lord Chancellor**.
    
    - _The Chancellor's Profile:_ At this time, the Chancellor was almost always a Bishop or Archbishop (a churchman). He was known as the "Keeper of the King's Conscience."
        
- **The Court of Chancery:** By the 14th century, the Chancellor began hearing these cases independently in his own court—the Court of Chancery.
    
- **Basis of Decisions:** The Chancellor did not follow previous legal precedents. He decided cases based on **"Conscience,"** **"Christian Morality,"** and **"Natural Justice."** If a Common Law rule was technically correct but morally wrong (e.g., kicking a widow out of her home because she lost the deed), the Chancellor would step in to stop it.
    

#### 2. The Renaissance(নবজাগরণ) and The Conflict (1500s – 1615)

As the Court of Chancery grew more powerful, it began to clash with the Common Law judges.

- **The Common Injunction:** The main weapon of the Chancellor was the "Common Injunction." He would not say the Common Law judgment was wrong; instead, he would order the successful plaintiff _not to enforce_ the judgment. If they disobeyed, they were imprisoned for contempt.
    
- **Coke vs. Ellesmere:** The conflict reached its peak between **Sir Edward Coke** (Chief Justice of the King’s Bench, champion of Common Law) and **Lord Ellesmere** (Lord Chancellor). Coke was furious that the Chancellor was effectively overruling his court.
    
- **The Earl of Oxford’s Case (1615):** The Climax.
    
    - _Facts:_ A judgment was obtained by fraud in a Common Law court. The Chancellor (Ellesmere) issued an injunction to prevent its enforcement. Coke declared this illegal.
        
    - _Resolution:_ The dispute was referred to King James I. On the advice of Francis Bacon, the King ruled in favor of the Chancellor.
        
    - _The Principle:_ **"Where Law and Equity conflict, Equity shall prevail."**
        

#### 3. The Period of Systematization (1660 – 1873)

After the conflict was settled, Equity had to mature. It could not remain a system based on the personal whim of one man.

- **Selden’s Criticism:** The jurist John Selden famously mocked early Equity, saying: _"Equity is a roguish thing. For law we have a measure... equity is according to the conscience of him that is Chancellor, and as that is larger or narrower, so is equity. 'Tis all one as if they should make the standard for the measure we call a foot, a Chancellor's foot."_
    
- **The Transformation:** To fix this uncertainty, later Chancellors (who were now lawyers, not clergymen) began to follow their own precedents.
    
    - **Lord Nottingham (The Father of Modern Equity):** He began systematizing the rules, declaring that _"The conscience of this court is not natural and private conscience, but a civil and political one (tied to precedent)."_
        
    - **Lord Eldon:** By the 19th century, under Lord Eldon, Equity became as rigid and technical as the Common Law itself. It was no longer vague "fairness"; it was a strict body of rules.
        

---

### II. The Judicature Acts (1873-1875)

The final step in the growth of Equity was the administrative "fusion" of the courts.

- **The Problem:** By the 19th century, the separation was causing delays. A person might have to sue in the Common Law court for damages and then go to the Chancery court for an injunction—two expensive lawsuits for one dispute.
    
- **The Solution:** The **Judicature Acts of 1873 and 1875** abolished the separate courts. They created a single **High Court of Justice** that could administer _both_ Law and Equity.
    
- **Section 25:** The Act statutorily reaffirmed the rule from the _Earl of Oxford’s Case_: in any conflict between the rules of Law and the rules of Equity, the rules of Equity shall prevail.

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Key Names to Drop in Essays:**
    
    - **Maitland:** "Equity came not to destroy the law, but to fulfill it."
        
    - **Lord Nottingham:** Credited with systematizing Equity.
        
    - **Sir Edward Coke:** The defender of Common Law who lost the battle in 1615.
        
- **The "Gloss" Metaphor:** Always describe Equity as a "gloss" or "supplement" to the Common Law. It assumes the Common Law exists and adds to it. It is not a standalone system (e.g., Equity doesn't have rules for murder or theft; it relies on Common Law for that).
    
- **The "Chancellor's Foot":** Memorize Selden’s quote. It is the perfect critique of early, uncodified Equity and explains _why_ the system had to evolve into fixed rules.
