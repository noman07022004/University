## 1.6 Jurisdiction in Bangladesh ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** This is the most practical part of the first section. It explains how the abstract English history you just learned actually functions in a Bangladeshi courtroom today.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5):** Examiners frequently ask questions like, "Discuss the application of Equity in Bangladesh" or "How is the principle of 'Justice, Equity and Good Conscience' applied?"

---

### I. The Nature of Jurisdiction: No Separate Courts

Unlike the historical development in England (where Common Law and Equity courts fought for centuries), the legal history of Bangladesh (as part of British India) is different.

**1. Fusion from the Beginning** When the British established the legal system in this region, they did not import the separate courts. From the very beginning, the courts established in British India were empowered to administer **both** Law and Equity.

- **Unified System:** A judge in a Bangladeshi Civil Court has always had the power to grant a decree for damages (Common Law) and an injunction (Equity) in the same suit. We never needed a "Judicature Act" to fuse the systems because they were never separated.
    

---

### II. Codified Equity (Statutory Jurisdiction)

The primary jurisdiction of Equity in Bangladesh is **statutory**. The British colonial legislature took the vague, discretionary principles of English Equity and wrote them down into rigid Acts of Parliament. This transformed "soft" equity into "hard" law.

**Key Statutes Incorporating Equitable Principles:**

1. **The Specific Relief Act, 1877:** This is purely equitable. It codifies the rules for Specific Performance, Injunctions, Rectification, and Rescission.
    
2. **The Trusts Act, 1882:** This codified the exclusive equitable jurisdiction over Trusts, defining the rights and duties of trustees and beneficiaries.
    
3. **The Contract Act, 1872:** Sections dealing with "Voidable Contracts" (e.g., coercion, undue influence, fraud) are based on equitable principles of fairness.
    
4. **The Transfer of Property Act, 1882:** Contains equitable doctrines like the "Doctrine of Part Performance" (Section 53A) and rules regarding redemption of mortgages.
    
5. **The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890:** Based on the Chancellor's equitable jurisdiction over infants and minors.
    

In these areas, Bangladeshi courts do not look to English cases for "discretion"; they follow the **written sections of the Act**.

---

### III. Residual Equity: "Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience"

What happens if a dispute arises in Bangladesh that is _not_ covered by any specific statute or personal law (Hindu/Muslim law)?

**1. The Source of Power** The Civil Courts are empowered to fill these legal gaps. The authority comes from historical statutes like the **Civil Courts Act, 1887**.

- **Section 37 (or equivalent in other acts):** It mandates that in cases regarding succession, marriage, caste, etc., the Personal Law (Muslim/Hindu) applies.
    
- **The Residual Clause:** In cases _not_ covered by those specific laws, the court shall act according to **"Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience."**
    

**2. Interpretation** Historically, the Privy Council interpreted "Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience" to mean **the rules of English Law and Equity**, so far as they are applicable to local conditions.

- _Meaning:_ This clause is the "gateway" through which English equitable principles (that are not codified in our Acts) enter Bangladeshi law. If there is no Bangladeshi law on a topic, the judge looks at how English Equity handles it and applies that rule if it suits our society.
    

---

### IV. Limitations of Equity in Bangladesh

The jurisdiction of Equity in Bangladesh is subject to a massive limitation: **Statute Law prevails.**

**1. No Override Power** In England (historically), Equity could restrain the enforcement of the Law. In Bangladesh, **Equity cannot override a written Statute.**

- _Example:_ The _Limitation Act_ says a suit must be filed within 3 years. A plaintiff cannot argue, "But Equity says Laches (delay) is flexible!" The Judge will say, "The Statute sets a hard limit. Equity cannot ignore the Parliament."
    

**2. Supporting, Not Superseding** Equity in Bangladesh acts only to:

- **Interpret** the law (to avoid harshness).
    
- **Fill gaps** in the law (where the statute is silent).
    
- It _never_ acts against the specific command of a statute.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **The "Gateway" Phrase:** Memorize the phrase **"Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience."** This is the magic phrase that gives Bangladeshi judges equitable power in uncodified areas.
    
- **Comparison Table:** A strong exam answer often contrasts the two sources:
    
    - **Codified Equity:** Specific Relief Act, Trusts Act (Mandatory).
        
    - **Uncodified Equity:** Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience (Discretionary, Gap-filling).
        
- **Case Law:** In the famous case of _Abul Fata Mahomed Ishak v. Russomoy Dhur (1894)_, the Privy Council famously remarked that "Justice, equity and good conscience" generally meant the rules of English Equity. Using this case name adds significant weight to your answer.
