

## 1.4 Courts of Equity: Composition, Powers, and Functions ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4):** Understanding _who_ ran these courts and _what_ powers they claimed helps clarify why certain remedies (like Trusts) were exclusive to them, while others (like Specific Performance) were shared with Common Law.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3):** You are less likely to get a full essay on the "Composition of the Chancery," but short notes on "Exclusive vs. Concurrent Jurisdiction" are common exam questions.
    

---

### I. Composition of the Court of Chancery

The "Court of Equity" was formally known as the **Court of Chancery**. It evolved from a one-man operation into a structured judicial body.

**1. The Lord Chancellor**

- **Role:** The presiding judge and head of the court. Historically, he was the "Keeper of the King's Conscience" and the most powerful minister in the government.
    
- **Evolution:** Originally a clergyman (Bishop/Archbishop) who decided cases based on Canon Law and morality. After the Reformation (16th Century), lawyers began to hold the post, turning it into a legal rather than religious office.
    

**2. The Master of the Rolls**

- **Role:** Originally the keeper of the Chancery records (the "rolls" of parchment).
    
- **Evolution:** As the Chancellor's workload became unmanageable, the Master of the Rolls was empowered to hear cases as a deputy judge. By the 19th century, he became a primary judge of the court, though his decisions could be appealed to the Chancellor.
    

**3. The Vice-Chancellors**

- **Role:** Additional judges appointed to assist the Chancellor.
    
- **Evolution:** Created by statute in the early 19th century (1813) to clear the massive backlog of cases. (At one point, it took decades for a case to finish in Chancery—a problem famously satirized by Charles Dickens in _Bleak House_).
    

---

### II. Powers and Functions (The Three Jurisdictions)

The most critical academic categorization of Equity is how its powers interacted with the Common Law. This is divided into three "Jurisdictions".

#### 1. Exclusive Jurisdiction

- **Definition:** Matters where the Common Law courts offered **no relief** and recognized **no rights** at all. If you went to a Common Law court, they would dismiss your case. Only Equity provided a remedy.
    
- **Key Example:** **Trusts**.
    
    - _Scenario:_ A transfers land to B to hold for C.
        
    - _Common Law View:_ B is the owner. C has no rights.
        
    - _Equity View:_ B is the legal owner, but C is the beneficial owner. Equity enforces C's rights.
        
- **Other Examples:** Administration of estates of the deceased, foreclosure of mortgages.
    

#### 2. Concurrent Jurisdiction

- **Definition:** Matters where the Common Law recognized the right and offered a remedy (Damages), but Equity offered a **different, more adequate remedy**. Both courts could hear the case, but the plaintiff chose Equity for a better result.
    
- **Key Example:** **Breach of Contract**.
    
    - _Common Law Remedy:_ Damages (Money).
        
    - _Equity Remedy:_ **Specific Performance** (Force the party to perform) or **Injunction** (Stop the breach).
        
- **Note:** This jurisdiction depended on the "Adequacy of Damages" test. If money was enough, Equity would not interfere.
    

#### 3. Auxiliary Jurisdiction

- **Definition:** Matters where Equity did not decide the case itself but **helped** the Common Law court to do justice by providing procedural tools that Common Law lacked.
    
- **Key Example:** **Discovery of Documents**.
    
    - _Scenario:_ In a Common Law trial, the plaintiff suspects the defendant has a secret letter proving the fraud. Common Law had no power to force him to show it.
        
    - _Equity's Aid:_ The plaintiff would file a "Bill of Discovery" in Chancery. The Chancellor would force the defendant (on oath) to reveal the document, which was then used in the Common Law trial.
        

---

### III. The End of Separation

It is important to note that these distinct "jurisdictions" merged administratively in England with the **Judicature Acts (1873-1875)**.

- Today, a single court in England (and effectively in Bangladesh) exercises all three powers. A judge can grant damages (Common Law), enforce a Trust (Exclusive Equity), and order Specific Performance (Concurrent Equity) all in the same lawsuit.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Jurisdictional Classifications:** This is the most "exam-relevant" part of this subtopic. If asked to "Discuss the jurisdiction of Equity," structure your answer strictly under these three heads: **Exclusive**, **Concurrent**, and **Auxiliary**.
    
- **The "Bleak House" Reference:** In an essay about the growth (and problems) of the Chancery courts, mentioning Charles Dickens' novel _Bleak House_ (case of _Jarndyce and Jarndyce_) adds distinct academic flair. It highlights why the Vice-Chancellors were needed—the system had become slow and expensive.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** Remember, in Bangladesh, we never had this split. Our courts have always possessed all three jurisdictions simultaneously since the British established the legal system here.
