## 1.1 Common Law ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** You absolutely cannot understand Equity without first understanding Common Law. Equity was literally created to fix the defects of the Common Law. If Common Law is the "Thesis," Equity is the "Antithesis."
>- **Exam (⭐ 3):** While you might not get a full essay question solely on "What is Common Law?" in an Equity exam, you will frequently be asked to _distinguish_ between Law and Equity or explain the conflict between them.

---

### I. Definition and Historical Context

**Common Law** refers to the body of customary law, based upon judicial decisions and embodied in reports of decided cases, that has been administered by the common-law courts of England since the Middle Ages. It is distinct from statute law (laws made by Parliament) and from Equity (laws made by the Chancellor).

It is called "Common" because, after the Norman Conquest of 1066, King William the Conqueror sought to establish a unified legal system "common" to the entire realm of England, replacing the fragmented local customs of the Anglo-Saxons.

### II. Key Characteristics of Common Law

To understand why Equity was needed, we must look at how the Common Law functioned:

**1. The Writ System (The Rigid Framework)** The Common Law was procedural. To start a lawsuit, a plaintiff had to purchase a "Writ" (a sealed royal order) from the King's Chancery.

- **No Writ, No Remedy:** Each wrong had a specific writ (e.g., _Writ of Trespass_, _Writ of Debt_). If a plaintiff's problem didn't fit into one of these pre-existing "pigeonholes," the court would throw the case out.
    
- **Formalism:** The system was hyper-technical. A minor spelling error or choosing the wrong writ (even if the injustice was obvious) resulted in the case being dismissed.
    

**2. Stare Decisis (Doctrine of Precedent)** Common Law relies heavily on the principle of _stare decisis_ ("let the decision stand").

- Once a point of law was decided by a superior court, it became binding on all future cases.
    
- **The Problem:** While this created certainty, it also created rigidity. Judges felt bound by bad precedents and could not change the law to fit new, complex situations.
    

**3. Trial by Jury** Common Law cases were typically decided by a jury, which determined the facts. (Equity courts, by contrast, did not use juries; the Chancellor was the sole judge).

### III. The Defects that Birthed Equity

By the 13th and 14th centuries, the Common Law had become ossified (hardened). Three major defects made it inadequate for justice, paving the way for Equity:

1. **Rigidity:** As mentioned, the refusal to recognize new types of wrongs left many victims without help.
    
2. **Inadequacy of Remedy:** The Common Law usually only offered **Damages** (money).
    
    - _Scenario:_ If your neighbor was cutting down your ancient trees, the Common Law would say, "Let him finish, then sue him for the value of the timber."
        
    - _The Gap:_ It could not stop him (Injunction) or force him to replant (Specific Performance). Money was often a poor substitute for the actual right.
        
3. **Power Imbalance:** Powerful local lords could often intimidate juries or sheriffs in the Common Law courts. The King (and his Chancellor), however, were powerful enough to compel even the mightiest nobles to do justice.

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **"Common Law" has three meanings:** Be careful how you use the term, as it changes based on context:
    
    1. **Common Law vs. Equity:** (This course) The strict law of the King's Bench vs. the discretionary justice of the Chancellor.
        
    2. **Common Law vs. Civil Law:** The English legal system (precedent-based) vs. the Roman/European system (code-based).
        
    3. **Common Law vs. Statute Law:** Judge-made law vs. Parliamentary Acts.
        
- **Visualizing the timeline:** Remember that Common Law came _first_. Equity developed _later_ as a "gloss" or patch to fix it.
    
- **Bangladesh Context:** Bangladesh is a Common Law country. This means our legal system is based on the English model of precedent, adversarial trials, and the distinction (now fused in administration) between legal and equitable principles.
