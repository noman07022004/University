# 4. Trust ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** This is the heart of the second section of your course. You cannot understand the duties of a trustee or the rights of a beneficiary without mastering the fundamental concept of a "Trust."
>- **Exam (⭐ 5):** This is an inevitable exam topic. Questions frequently cover the "Three Certainties" required to create a trust, the classification of trusts, or the "Doctrine of Cy-près."
    

### Introduction

A **Trust** is the most distinct achievement of English Equity. It is a relationship where one person (the **Trustee**) holds property for the benefit of another (the **Beneficiary**). It creates a split in ownership: the Trustee holds the _legal_ title (on paper), while the Beneficiary holds the _equitable_ title (the real benefit). In Bangladesh, the law regarding trusts is codified in **The Trusts Act, 1882**.

---

### I. History and Growth of Trust

_(Covering Subtopic: 4.1 History and growth of trust)_

**1. The "Use" (The Medieval Ancestor)** The concept began in medieval England as the "Use" (from Latin _opus_, meaning "on behalf of").

- **Reason:** Feudal lords had to pay heavy taxes to the King when they died and passed land to their heirs. To avoid this, a landowner (Feoffor) would transfer the land to a group of friends (Feoffees) "to the use of" himself and his family.
    
- **The Problem:** Common Law courts did not recognize the rights of the family (the beneficiaries). If the friends stole the land, Common Law said, "They are the legal owners, there is nothing we can do."
    
- **Equity Steps In:** The Chancellor interfered, stating that while the friends were the _legal_ owners, their _conscience_ required them to hold the land for the family. This was the birth of the Trust.
    

**2. The Statute of Uses (1535) and the Modern Trust** King Henry VIII tried to ban this practice with the _Statute of Uses_, which forced the legal title to move to the beneficiary, enabling him to collect taxes again. However, lawyers found a loophole (the "Use upon a Use"), and by the 17th century, the modern "Trust" was fully established.

---

### II. Nature and Definition

_(Covering Subtopic: 4.2 Nature and definition)_

**1. Statutory Definition (Section 3, Trusts Act 1882)** A "Trust" is an obligation annexed to the ownership of property, and arising out of a confidence reposed in and accepted by the owner, or declared and accepted by him, for the benefit of another, or of another and the owner.

**2. Key Elements (The Parties)**

- **Author of the Trust (Settlor):** The person who reposes the confidence and transfers the property.
    
- **Trustee:** The person who accepts the confidence and holds the property.
    
- **Beneficiary (Cestui que trust):** The person for whose benefit the confidence is accepted.
    
- **Trust Property:** The subject matter of the trust (money, land, etc.).
    

---

### III. Classification of Trusts

_(Covering Subtopic: 4.3 Classification)_

Trusts are classified based on how they are created and their purpose.

**1. Based on Creation**

- **Express Trust:** Created intentionally by the author, either by words or in writing (e.g., "I give $10,000 to X to hold for Y").
    
- **Implied Trust:** Not created by express words but presumed by the court based on the conduct of parties.
    
    - **Resulting Trust:** When the beneficial interest "results" (jumps back) to the creator. (e.g., A transfers land to B for a trust, but the trust fails. B holds the land on a "Resulting Trust" for A).
        
    - **Constructive Trust:** Imposed by the court _regardless_ of intention to prevent fraud or unjust enrichment. (e.g., If a trustee makes a secret profit from trust property, he holds that profit on a Constructive Trust for the beneficiary).
        

**2. Based on Purpose**

- **Private Trust:** For the benefit of specific individuals (e.g., "for my children").
    
- **Public (Charitable) Trust:** For the benefit of the public at large (e.g., "for the education of the poor in Dhaka"). These are often permanent and enjoy tax exemptions.
    

---

### IV. Creation and Extinction of Trust

_(Covering Subtopic: 4.4 Creation and extinction of trust)_

**1. The Three Certainties (Creation)** For a valid trust to be created (especially under English law and followed in Bangladesh), three things must be clear, known as the rule in **Knight v Knight (1840)**:

- **Certainty of Intention:** The language must show a clear intention to create a binding obligation, not just a hope or wish. (e.g., "I _hope_ he uses the money for the kids" creates no trust. "I _direct_ him to use..." creates a trust).
    
- **Certainty of Subject Matter:** The property must be clearly identified. (e.g., "The bulk of my estate" is too vague; "My house at 10 Main St" is valid).
    
- **Certainty of Objects:** The beneficiaries must be identifiable. (e.g., "For my friends" is too vague; "For my employees" is valid).
    

**2. Who can create a Trust?** Any person competent to contract (major, sound mind) can create a trust.

**3. Extinction of Trust (Termination)** A trust is extinguished when:

- Its purpose is completely fulfilled.
    
- Its purpose becomes unlawful.
    
- The trust property is destroyed.
    
- **Revocation:** The author can revoke the trust if they reserved the power to do so in the trust deed (Section 78, Trusts Act 1882).
    

---

### V. Doctrine of Cypress (Cy-près)

_(Covering Subtopic: 4.6 Doctrine of cypress)_

**1. Meaning** "Cy-près" (pronounced _see-pray_) is a Norman-French phrase meaning **"as near as possible."**

**2. Application** This doctrine applies **only to Public/Charitable Trusts**. If a charitable trust is created but the original purpose becomes impossible or impossible to carry out, the court does _not_ let the money go back to the donor's heirs. Instead, the court directs the property to be applied to another charitable purpose that is **"as near as possible"** to the original intention.

- **Example:** A man leaves money in 1900 to "fight leprosy in Village X." By 2024, leprosy is eradicated in that village. Under _Cy-près_, the court will not cancel the trust but might order the money to be used to "fight skin diseases in Village X" or "fight leprosy in the neighboring village."
    

---

### VI. Remedies for Breach of Trust

_(Covering Subtopic: 4.5 Remedies for breach of trust)_

When a trustee violates their duty (e.g., steals money, invests recklessly), the beneficiary has several remedies:

**1. Personal Remedy (Against the Trustee)** The beneficiary can sue the trustee personally to compensate for the loss. The trustee is liable to restore the property or pay its value.

**2. Proprietary Remedy (Tracing)** If the trustee used trust money to buy an asset (e.g., a car or house), the beneficiary can "trace" the money into that asset and claim the asset itself.

- _Example:_ Trustee steals 10 Lakh BDT from the trust and buys a plot of land. The land value rises to 50 Lakh BDT. The beneficiary can claim the _land_ (worth 50 Lakh), not just the original 10 Lakh.

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Section 3 of Trusts Act 1882:** Memorize this definition. It is the standard starting point for any exam answer on Trusts in Bangladesh.
    
- **Knight v Knight (1840):** You _must_ cite this case when discussing the creation of a trust. If any of the "Three Certainties" is missing, the trust fails.
    
- **Cy-près Limit:** Remember, Cy-près **never** applies to private trusts. If a private trust fails (e.g., "for my son," and the son dies before the trust starts), the money goes back to the creator (Resulting Trust).
    
- **Comparison:** A common short question compares "Trust vs. Bailment" or "Trust vs. Agency."
    
    - _Trust:_ Trustee has _legal ownership_.
        
    - _Bailment/Agency:_ Bailee/Agent only has _possession_, not ownership.
