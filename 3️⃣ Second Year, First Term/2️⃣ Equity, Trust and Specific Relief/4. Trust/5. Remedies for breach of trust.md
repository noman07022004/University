

# 4.5 Remedies for Breach of Trust ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5):** This is the "enforcement" mechanism of the Trust. A trust is useless if there is no consequence for breaking it. Understanding the difference between suing the trustee personally (Personal) and chasing the stolen asset (Proprietary) is crucial for legal practice.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5):** Problem questions often end with the trustee running away or going bankrupt. You will be asked: "What can the beneficiary do?" You must be able to apply the rules of **Tracing**.
    

---

### I. What is a Breach of Trust?

A breach of trust occurs when a trustee fails to perform any of the duties imposed upon them by the trust instrument or by the general law (The Trusts Act, 1882).

- **Active Breach:** Doing something forbidden (e.g., investing in a gambling company, selling land without permission).
    
- **Passive Breach:** Failing to do something required (e.g., leaving money in a drawer instead of investing it, failing to collect rent).
    

**Section 23** of the _Trusts Act, 1882_ states the general rule: Where the trustee commits a breach of trust, he is liable to make good the loss which the trust property or the beneficiary has thereby sustained.

---

### II. Personal Remedies (Remedy _In Personam_)

The primary remedy is to sue the trustee personally for the loss. This is an action against the trustee's own pocket.

1. Measure of Liability

The trustee must put the trust estate back in the same position as if the breach had not been committed.

- **Restitution:** If they stole money, they must replace it.
    
- **Compensation:** If their negligence caused the value of shares to drop, they must pay the difference.
    

2. Liability for Interest

Generally, a trustee is liable to pay interest on the money lost.

- **Standard Rate:** Usually simple interest (e.g., 6%).
    
- **Penal Rate:** If the trustee used the money for their own business or committed fraud, the court may charge **compound interest** at a higher rate to strip them of any profit they made.
    

3. Joint and Several Liability (Section 27)

If there are co-trustees, they are "jointly and severally" liable.

- _Meaning:_ If Trustee A and Trustee B together lose 1 Lakh Taka, the beneficiary can sue Trustee A for the _entire_ 1 Lakh. Trustee A then has to chase Trustee B for his share (Contribution).
    
- _Passive Trustee:_ Even a trustee who did nothing (sat by while the other stole) can be liable for failing to watch his colleague.
    

---

### III. Proprietary Remedies: The Doctrine of Tracing

This is the most powerful remedy in Equity. If the trustee is bankrupt (has no money), suing them personally is useless. **Tracing** allows the beneficiary to identify and claim the trust property itself, even if it has changed form.

**1. Following vs. Tracing**

- **Following:** Pursuing the _same_ asset as it moves from hand to hand (e.g., Trustee gives the Trust's gold watch to X; Beneficiary demands the watch from X).
    
- **Tracing:** Identifying the value of the trust property in a _new_ asset (e.g., Trustee sells the gold watch and buys a car; Beneficiary claims the car).
    

2. Tracing into a Mixed Fund (The Rule in Re Hallett’s Estate)

What happens if the Trustee mixes Trust Money ($1,000) with his Own Money ($1,000) in a bank account, and then spends some of it?

- **The Problem:** Money has no earmark. Whose money did he spend?
    
- **The Presumption:** Equity presumes the trustee acts honestly. Therefore, he is presumed to spend **his own money first**.
    
    - _Example:_ Trustee has $2,000 total (mixed). He spends $1,000 on a holiday. The court says, "That was his own $1,000." The remaining $1,000 in the bank belongs to the Trust.
        
- **The Oatway Exception:** If the trustee buys a profitable asset (e.g., a lottery ticket) with the mixed money, the beneficiary can choose to claim that asset. (He cannot say "I spent my own money on the winning ticket and kept the trust money in the bank").
    

3. Limits to Tracing

Tracing ends when:

- The property is destroyed/consumed (e.g., spent on a dinner).
    
- The property reaches a **Bona Fide Purchaser for Value Without Notice** (Equity's Darling).
    

---

### IV. Impounding Beneficial Interest (Section 62)

Sometimes, a beneficiary instigates the breach.

- **Scenario:** Beneficiary X tells Trustee Y, "Please invest in this risky startup, I authorize it." The money is lost. Beneficiary X then sues Trustee Y for breach.
    
- **Remedy:** The court may **impound** (seize) X's share of the trust interest to indemnify the trustee. You cannot consent to a breach and then sue for it.
    

---

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Bankruptcy Strategy:** In exams, always check if the trustee is solvent.
    
    - _Trustee Rich?_ Sue for **Personal Remedy** (easier).
        
    - _Trustee Bankrupt?_ Use **Tracing** to claim the asset directly (so you don't have to stand in line with other creditors).
        
- **Re Hallett's Estate (1880):** This is the leading case for tracing. You must cite it when discussing mixed funds. It established that a trustee cannot say "I spent the trust money and kept mine."
    
- **Criminal Breach of Trust:** Note that breach of trust is also a crime under the **Penal Code (Section 405)** in Bangladesh. Mentioning this adds a "multi-disciplinary" layer to your answer.
