

# 4.6 Doctrine of Cypress (Cy-près) ⭐ ⭐ ⭐ ⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4):** This doctrine highlights the unique privilege accorded to "Charity" in Equity. It explains why a gift to a charity never fails, unlike a gift to a person.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4):** This is a favorite "Short Note" topic. Examiners often ask: _"Discuss the doctrine of Cy-près"_ or _"What happens when a charitable purpose becomes impossible?"_
    

---

### I. Meaning and Origin

The term **"Cy-près"** (pronounced _see-pray_) is an old Norman-French phrase meaning **"as near as possible"** or "so near to it."

- **The Principle:** If a person intends to give property to a charity, but the specific mode of execution becomes impossible or impractical, the court will not let the trust fail. Instead, it will apply the property to _another_ charitable purpose that falls **"as near as possible"** to the original intention of the donor.
    
- **Objective:** Equity favors charity. It tries to save a charitable gift from failing and reverting to the donor's heirs.
    

---

### II. Conditions for Application

For the Cy-près doctrine to apply, two main conditions must be satisfied:

**1. General Charitable Intention** The court must find that the donor had a general intention to benefit charity, not just a specific, narrow wish.

- **Scenario A (Specific Intention):** "I give $10,000 to the 'Dhaka Cat Shelter'." If that specific shelter closes down before the testator dies, the gift **fails**. The money goes back to the heirs (Resulting Trust) because the donor only wanted to help _that specific shelter_.
    
- **Scenario B (General Intention):** "I give $10,000 to the 'Dhaka Cat Shelter' _to help animals_." Even if the shelter closes, the court sees a _general_ intent to help animals. The court will apply Cy-près and give the money to the "Chittagong Dog Shelter" or similar.
    

**2. Impossibility or Impracticality** The original purpose must be impossible or impracticable to carry out. This can happen:

- **Initial Failure:** The charity named in the will never existed or ceased to exist before the testator died.
    
- **Subsequent Failure:** The charity existed but later closed down, or the purpose was fulfilled (e.g., a trust to "abolish slavery" after slavery was already abolished).
    
- **Surplus Funds:** The trust has too much money. (e.g., A disaster relief fund raises 10 Crore Taka for a flood, but only 5 Crore is needed. The surplus is applied Cy-près to other disaster relief, rather than returned to millions of donors).
    

---

### III. Limitation: Only for Public Trusts

It is crucial to understand that Cy-près applies **exclusively** to Public (Charitable) Trusts.

- **Private Trusts:** If a Private Trust fails (e.g., "Trust for my son," and the son dies), the doctrine of Cy-près does **not** apply. The property returns to the Settlor via a **Resulting Trust**.
    
- **Rationale:** Courts have the power to protect public welfare (Parens Patriae jurisdiction), but they cannot rewrite a private individual's gift to their family.
    

---

### IV. Application in Bangladesh

In Bangladesh, this equitable principle is recognized under the general law of Trusts and specifically in statutes regarding public endowments (like Waqf or Hindu Religious Endowments).

- **Section 83 of the Trusts Act, 1882:** This section deals with trust failure. While it generally mandates a Resulting Trust, the principles of English Equity allow the application of Cy-près for charitable trusts under the "Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience" jurisdiction.
    

---

### 📝 Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Spelling:** The syllabus spells it "Cypress," but the correct legal spelling is **"Cy-près"**. Use the correct spelling in your exam to show sophistication, but acknowledge the syllabus term.
    
- **The "Paramount" Intention:** In problem questions, look for the donor's "paramount" intention. Did they love _Charity_ generally, or just _One Specific Institution_?
    
    - _Hint:_ If the will says "for the purpose of..." (describing a goal), it's usually General Intention. If it just names a specific obscure club, it might be Specific Intention.
        
- **Example Case:** _Re Lysaght (1966)_. A testatrix gave money to the Royal College of Surgeons for scholarships but added a racist condition (no Jews/Catholics). The College refused the condition. The court used Cy-près to **remove the condition** and let the trust proceed, arguing her "general intention" was education, not discrimination.
