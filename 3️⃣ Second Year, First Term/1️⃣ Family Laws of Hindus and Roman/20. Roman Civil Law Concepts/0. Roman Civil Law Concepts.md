## 20. Roman Civil Law Concepts ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This section constitutes the bulk of "Private Law." Modern concepts like "Possession is 9/10ths of the law," "Contracts require consent," and "Universal Succession" all originate here.

### Introduction

Roman Civil Law (_Jus Civile_) is the body of law applicable to Roman citizens. It is characterized by strict formalism in the early period and increasing flexibility (equity) in the later period. The Roman jurist Gaius divided all law into three parts: Persons (_Personae_), Things (_Res_), and Actions (_Actiones_). This topic covers the vast area of **"Things"** (Property, Obligations, and Succession).

---

### I. Property and Ownership

**Syllabus Covered:** _20.1 Property and Ownership_

Roman Law made a sharp distinction between the physical thing (_Res_) and the legal rights over it.

**1. Classification of Things (_Res_)**

- **_Res Mancipi:_** Important agricultural assets essential to early Roman life (land in Italy, slaves, beasts of burden like horses/oxen).
    
    - **Transfer:** Could _only_ be transferred by a highly formal ritual called **Mancipatio**. Simple delivery was not enough.
        
- **_Res Nec Mancipi:_** All other things (money, clothes, tools, provincial land).
    
    - **Transfer:** Could be transferred by simple delivery (**Traditio**).
        

**2. Ownership (_Dominium_) vs. Possession (_Possessio_)** This is a critical conceptual distinction in Roman Law:

- **Dominium (Ownership):** The ultimate legal title. The owner has the right to use, enjoy, and destroy the property.
    
- **Possessio (Possession):** The factual physical control over a thing, combined with the _intention_ to hold it as one's own (_Animus Possidendi_).
    
    - _Note:_ A thief has possession, but not ownership. A landlord has ownership, but the tenant has physical detention (custody). Roman law protected possession legally (via Interdicts) to prevent violence, even if the possessor wasn't the true owner.
        

**3. Modes of Acquiring Ownership**

- **Mancipatio:** A formal ceremony with a balance holder and five witnesses. The transferee held the thing and recited a set formula. Essential for _Res Mancipi_.
    
- **Traditio:** Simple delivery of the item with the intent to transfer ownership. Used for _Res Nec Mancipi_ (and later for everything under Justinian).
    
- **Usucapio:** Acquisition of ownership by long-term possession (prescription). If you bought something in good faith from a non-owner and held it for 1 year (movables) or 2 years (land), you became the legal owner.

### II. Contracts and Torts

**Syllabus Covered:** _20.2 Contracts and Torts_

In Roman Law, both Contracts and Torts are sources of **"Obligations"**—a legal bond (_Vinculum Juris_) compelling one person to do something for another.

**1. Contracts (_Contractus_)** Romans did not have a general theory that "any agreement is a contract." An agreement became a contract only if it fit into one of four specific categories:

- **Verbal Contracts:** Formed by spoken words using a strict formula.
    
    - _Example:_ **Stipulatio** (Question: "Do you promise to pay 100?" Answer: "I promise").
        
- **Literal Contracts:** Formed by writing an entry in the household ledger/account book.
    
- **Real Contracts:** Formed by the delivery of a thing (_Res_). The obligation arises because you received the item.
    
    - _Mutuum:_ Loan for consumption (e.g., lending money or grain).
        
    - _Commodatum:_ Loan for use (e.g., lending a horse for a journey).
        
    - _Depositum:_ Handing something over for safekeeping.
        
- **Consensual Contracts:** Formed merely by the **consent** of the parties. No form or delivery required. These were the most modern and commercially important.
    
    - _Emptio Venditio:_ Sale of goods.
        
    - _Locatio Conductio:_ Hire/Lease.
        
    - _Societas:_ Partnership.
        
    - _Mandatum:_ Agency (doing a favor for free).
        

**2. Torts (_Delicts_)** A _Delict_ was a wrongful act against an individual (a civil wrong), as opposed to a _Crime_ (offense against the State). The penalty was usually a monetary fine paid to the victim.

- **Furtum (Theft):** Taking someone's property with intent to gain.
    
- **Rapina (Robbery):** Theft with violence.
    
- **Damnum Iniuria Datum:** Wrongful damage to property (governed by the _Lex Aquilia_).
    
- **Iniuria:** Insult or injury to a person's dignity or body (assault, libel).

### III. Marriage

**Syllabus Covered:** _20.3 Marriage (Part 1)_

Roman marriage was meant for the procreation of legitimate children who would fall under the father's power.

**1. Forms of Marriage**

- **Cum Manu (With Power):** The wife legally passed from her father's family into her husband's family. She became like a "daughter" to her husband regarding property rights.
    
    - _Confarreatio:_ Religious ceremony (for Patricians).
        
    - _Coemptio:_ A symbolic "sale" of the wife to the husband.
        
    - _Usus:_ Cohabitation for one year without interruption.
        
- **Sine Manu (Without Power):** The "Free Marriage." The wife remained legally part of her father's family. Her property remained hers (separate from the husband). This became the dominant form in the late Republic and Empire.
    

**2. Divorce** Roman law was very liberal regarding divorce. Since marriage was based on the "intent to be married" (_Affectio Maritalis_), if that intent ceased, the marriage ended. Either party could divorce the other without needing state approval (though Christianity later restricted this).

### IV. Wills and Intestacy

**Syllabus Covered:** _20.3 Marriage and Inheritance (Part 2), 20.4 Wills and Intestacy_

Roman succession is based on the concept of **Universal Succession**. The heir (_Heres_) steps into the shoes of the deceased, acquiring _all_ their assets and _all_ their liabilities/debts as a single unit.

**1. Wills (_Testamentum_)**

- **Importance:** To die without a will was considered a misfortune in Rome.
    
- **Requirements:** A valid will required:
    
    - _Testamentifactio:_ The legal capacity to make a will.
        
    - _Institution of an Heir:_ You _must_ name an heir ("Let Titius be my heir"). If this fails, the whole will fails.
        
    - _Witnesses:_ Usually required 5 or 7 witnesses and seals.
        
- **Inofficious Will:** If a testator unfairly disinherited his close children without cause, they could challenge the will under the _Querela Inofficiosi Testamenti_ (Complaint of the Unduteous Will) to get their fair share.
    

**2. Intestacy (No Will)** If a person died Intestate (without a will), the law decided the heirs based on the **Twelve Tables** (strict agnatic order) and later **Justinian's Reforms** (blood relationship).

- **Justinian’s Order (Novels 118 & 127):** This is the system that resembles modern law.
    
    1. **Descendants:** Sons/Daughters (equally).
        
    2. **Ascendants & Siblings:** Parents and brothers/sisters.
        
    3. **Half-siblings:** Consanguineous (same father) or Uterine (same mother).
        
    4. **Collaterals:** Other relatives.

### V. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Nemo dat quod non habet:** "No one gives what he does not have." This maxim comes from Roman property law—a thief cannot transfer ownership to a buyer because the thief doesn't have it.
    
- **Stipulatio:** This is the most famous Roman contract. In exams, if asked about "Verbal Contracts," describe the Q&A format ("Spondesne? Spondeo."). It was used for almost anything—loans, marriage settlements, guarantees.
    
- **Res Mancipi Trick:** Examiners love asking: _"Titius sold a horse to Seius by simple delivery (Traditio). Who owns the horse?"_
    
    - _Answer:_ Under early law, Titius still owns it! A horse is _Res Mancipi_ and requires _Mancipatio_. Seius only has _Possessio_ (until he holds it long enough for _Usucapio_).
        
- **Patria Potestas:** Remember that in Roman inheritance, if a son is under his father's power, he cannot make a will because he technically owns nothing—everything belongs to the father.
