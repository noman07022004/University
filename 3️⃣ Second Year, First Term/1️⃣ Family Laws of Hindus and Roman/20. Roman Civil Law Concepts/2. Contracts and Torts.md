
### 20.2 Contracts and Torts (_Contractus et Delicta_) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This subtopic explains the origin of all legal duties. Roman Law gave the world the fundamental theory of "Obligations"—the legal bond that ties two people together.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** A massive area for exams. You will frequently face questions like "Classify Roman Contracts" or "Discuss the principles of liability under the Lex Aquilia."
    

---

#### I. Introduction: The Concept of Obligation

In Roman Law, both Contracts and Torts are sources of **Obligations**.

- **Definition:** An Obligation (_Obligatio_) is a "legal bond" (_Vinculum Juris_) whereby one person (Debtor) is constrained by the law to do something for another person (Creditor).
    
- **Sources:** Justinian classified obligations into four sources, but the two primary ones are:
    
    1. **Contracts:** Obligations arising from lawful agreements.
        
    2. **Delicts (Torts):** Obligations arising from wrongful acts.
        

---

#### II. Contracts (_Contractus_)

Unlike modern law, where "any agreement" can be a contract, Roman Law followed a **Closed List System**. An agreement was not enforceable unless it fit into one of four specific forms. A mere agreement outside these forms was a _Nudum Pactum_ ("Naked Pact") and produced no action.

**The Four Classes of Contracts:**

**1. Verbal Contracts (_Verbis_)**

- **Form:** Created by the utterance of solemn words.
    
- **The Stipulatio:** This was the most important Roman contract. It consisted of a formal Question and Answer.
    
    - _Creditor:_ "Do you promise to pay 100 sestertii?" (_Spondesne?_)
        
    - _Debtor:_ "I promise." (_Spondeo._)
        
- **Feature:** It was unilateral (only the debtor was bound) and stricti juris (strictly interpreted). It could be used to make _any_ promise binding.
    

**2. Literal Contracts (_Litteris_)**

- **Form:** Created by a written entry in the creditor's household account book (_Codex_).
    
- **Usage:** It was used to transform an existing debt into a strict written debt. (Rarely used in the later Empire).
    

**3. Real Contracts (_Re_)**

- **Form:** Created by the **delivery of a thing** (_Res_). The obligation arises not from the promise, but because you actually received the item.
    
- **Types:**
    
    - **_Mutuum_** **(Loan for Consumption):** Lending money, grain, or wine. The borrower must return an _equivalent amount_ (not the exact same coins/grain). This is a strict loan.
        
    - **_Commodatum_** **(Loan for Use):** Lending a specific item (e.g., a horse, a book) to be returned. The borrower must return the _exact same thing_.
        
    - **_Depositum_** **(Deposit):** Handing an item to someone for safekeeping (free of charge).
        
    - **_Pignus_** **(Pledge):** Handing over an item as security for a debt.
        

**4. Consensual Contracts (_Consensu_)**

- **Form:** Created merely by the **consent** of the parties. No formalities or delivery required. These were the most modern and commercially vital contracts.
    
- **Types:**
    
    - **_Emptio Venditio_** **(Sale):** Agreement to exchange goods for a money price. (Note: Ownership didn't pass until delivery, but the contract was binding upon agreement).
        
    - **_Locatio Conductio_** **(Hire):** Leasing land, hiring a servant, or hiring a contractor.
        
    - **_Societas_** **(Partnership):** Agreement to share profits and losses in a joint venture.
        
    - **_Mandatum_** **(Mandate/Agency):** Gratuitous agency—doing a favor for another at their request.

#### III. Torts (_Delicts_)

A **Delict** in Roman Law was a wrongful act against an individual (a civil wrong).

- **Nature:** It was treated as a private matter, not a state crime. The victim sued the wrongdoer for a **Penal Action** (a monetary fine/penalty), not for compensation only.
    
- **The Four Main Delicts:**
    

**1. _Furtum_ (Theft)**

- **Definition:** Fraudulent interference with a thing (handling it) with intent to gain.
    
- **Scope:** It was broader than modern theft. It included embezzlement and even "usage theft" (using a borrowed horse for a longer journey than agreed).
    
- **Penalty:** Fines ranged from double the value (for non-manifest theft) to quadruple the value (if caught in the act).
    

**2. _Rapina_ (Robbery)**

- **Definition:** Theft committed with **violence**.
    
- **Origin:** Introduced by the Praetor to deal with mob violence.
    
- **Penalty:** Triple or quadruple damages.
    

**3. _Damnum Iniuria Datum_ (Property Damage)**

- **Statute:** Governed by the famous **_Lex Aquilia_** (c. 287 BC).
    
- **Definition:** Wrongful loss caused to another's property (slaves or cattle in Ch 1; other things in Ch 3).
    
- **The Concept of Fault:** This law introduced the concept that liability arises from _dolus_ (intent) or **_culpa_** (negligence). Even slight negligence created liability.
    
- **Legacy:** This is the ancestor of the modern Law of Torts/Negligence.
    

**4. _Iniuria_ (Personal Injury/Insult)**

- **Definition:** Aggression against a person’s physical integrity or dignity.
    
- **Scope:** Included assault, battery, defamation, and public insults.
    
- **Penalty:** Originally "Talio" (eye for an eye) in Twelve Tables; later replaced by "Estimated Damages" where the victim claimed a sum and the judge decided if it was fair.
    

---

#### IV. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Nudum Pactum:** Remember the maxim _"Ex nudo pacto non oritur actio"_ (From a naked promise, no action arises). Unless an agreement fell into one of the 4 categories above, it was unenforceable.
    
- **Lex Aquilia:** You **must** cite the _Lex Aquilia_ when discussing property damage. It is the single most important statute in Roman tort law.
    
- **Distinction:**
    
    - _Mutuum:_ Borrower owns the grain (consumes it) and repays equivalent.
        
    - _Commodatum:_ Borrower merely possesses the horse (uses it) and returns the specific horse.
