

### 20.1 Property and Ownership ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the foundation of the Roman Law of Things (_Jus Rerum_). The distinction between _Res Mancipi_ (valuable agricultural assets) and _Res Nec Mancipi_ explains the rigid formalism of early Roman law versus the flexibility of later law.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Problem questions constantly test the "Modes of Acquisition." A classic scenario involves a person selling a slave by simple delivery (_Traditio_) instead of ritual transfer (_Mancipatio_) and asking who owns the slave.
    

---

#### I. Classification of Things (_Res_)

Roman Law did not treat all property equally. The most fundamental division was based on the economic importance of the item to an early agricultural society.

**1. _Res Mancipi_**

- **Definition:** Things that were essential to the early Roman household and economy.
    
- **List:**
    
    - Land in Italy (_Fundus Italicus_).
        
    - Slaves (_Servi_).
        
    - Beasts of burden (Horses, oxen, mules, donkeys—but not elephants or camels).
        
    - Rustic servitudes (Rights of way over land).
        
- **Transfer:** These could **only** be transferred by a highly formal ritual called **_Mancipatio_** (or _In Jure Cessio_). Simple delivery was insufficient to pass ownership.
    

**2. _Res Nec Mancipi_**

- **Definition:** All other things (money, clothes, tools, land outside Italy, minor animals).
    
- **Transfer:** These could be transferred by simple delivery (**Traditio**) with the intent to pass title.
    

> **Evolution:** Justinian eventually abolished the distinction between _Res Mancipi_ and _Res Nec Mancipi_, allowing _Traditio_ for all property. But for exam purposes, the distinction is vital for the Classical period.

---

#### II. Ownership (_Dominium_) vs. Possession (_Possessio_)

Roman Law made a sharp conceptual distinction between the legal title and factual control.

**1. _Dominium_ (Ownership)**

- **Definition:** The ultimate legal title to a thing, giving the holder the right to use, enjoy, and destroy it.
    
- **Types:**
    
    - **_Dominium ex Jure Quiritium:_** Formal civil law ownership available only to Roman Citizens.
        
    - **_Bonitary Ownership (In Bonis Habere):_** Equitable ownership protected by the Praetor. This occurred when a person bought a _Res Mancipi_ without the proper ritual (e.g., just by delivery). The buyer wasn't the "Quiritary" owner yet, but the Praetor protected him against the seller.
        

**2. _Possessio_ (Possession)**

- **Definition:** The factual physical control over a thing.
    
- **Elements:** To have legal possession, two elements must coexist:
    
    - **_Corpus:_** Physical control or detention of the object.
        
    - **_Animus Possidendi:_** The mental intention to hold the object as one's own (excluding others).
        
- **Detention (_Detentio_):** If you have _Corpus_ but no _Animus_ (e.g., a borrower or tenant), you do not have Possession; you only have Custody.
    

---

#### III. Modes of Acquiring Ownership

How does one become an owner? Romans divided modes into **Derivative** (getting it from someone else) and **Original** (creating a new title).

**1. _Mancipatio_ (Derivative - Formal)**

- **Required for:** _Res Mancipi_.
    
- **The Ritual:** A solemn ceremony involving the buyer, the seller, a "Libripens" (scale-holder), and **5 witnesses** (all Roman citizens).
    
- **Action:** The buyer holds the thing (e.g., the slave) and taps the scales with a piece of bronze, reciting a set formula: _"I declare this man to be mine..."_
    
- **Effect:** Immediate transfer of Quiritary Ownership.
    

**2. _Traditio_ (Derivative - Informal)**

- **Required for:** _Res Nec Mancipi_ (and later everything).
    
- **The Act:** Simple physical delivery of the thing.
    
- **Requirement:** There must be a "Just Cause" (_Iusta Causa_)—an intention to transfer ownership (e.g., a sale or gift), not just a loan.
    

**3. _Usucapio_ (Original - Prescription)**

- **Definition:** Acquiring ownership by continuous possession for a certain period of time. This "cured" defective titles (e.g., if you bought a slave by _Traditio_ instead of _Mancipatio_).
    
- **Conditions:**
    
    - **Possession:** Must have actual control.
        
    - **Time:** **1 year** for movables; **2 years** for land (under Twelve Tables).
        
    - **Good Faith (_Bona Fides_):** You must believe you are the rightful owner.
        
    - **Just Title:** You must have a valid reason for possessing (e.g., you bought it).
        
    - **Not Stolen:** You cannot usucapt stolen goods (_Res Furtivae_).
        

---

#### IV. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Nemo dat quod non habet:** _"No one gives what he does not have."_ This famous maxim applies to Derivative modes. A thief cannot transfer ownership to a buyer because the thief has no ownership to give. The buyer can only become an owner through _Usucapio_ (if the goods weren't stolen, which is a catch).
    
- **The "Bonitary" Trap:** If an exam question says: _"A sells a horse to B by handing it over. Who owns the horse?"_
    
    - **Answer:** Under strict Civil Law, **A still owns it** (because a horse is _Res Mancipi_ and needs _Mancipatio_). However, B has "Bonitary Ownership" and will become full owner after 1 year via _Usucapio_.
        
- **Praetorian Protection:** Mention the **Actio Publiciana**. This was the legal remedy the Praetor gave to a "Bonitary Owner" to recover property if he lost it before the _Usucapio_ period was over.
