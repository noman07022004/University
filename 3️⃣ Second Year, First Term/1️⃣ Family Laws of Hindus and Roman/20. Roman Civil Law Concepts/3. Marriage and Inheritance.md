
### 20.3 Marriage and Inheritance ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Roman society was built on the family (_Familia_). Understanding Marriage (_Nuptiae_) is crucial because only a valid marriage produced children who were under the father's power (_Patria Potestas_) and thus eligible to inherit as "proper heirs."
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Examiners frequently compare the ancient _Cum Manu_ marriage with the later _Sine Manu_ marriage to illustrate the changing status of women.
    

---

#### I. Roman Marriage (_Justae Nuptiae_)

In Roman Law, marriage was not just a contract; it was a fact of social life with legal consequences. Modestinus defined it as _"The union of a male and a female, a partnership in the whole of life, a sharing of divine and human rights."_

**1. The Two Forms of Marriage** The legal position of the wife depended entirely on which form of marriage was adopted.

- **Marriage _Cum Manu_ (With Power):**
    
    - **Concept:** The wife legally passed out of her father's family and into her husband's family. She fell under the **Manus** (Hand/Power) of her husband (or his father).
        
    - **Legal Status:** She became legally like a "daughter" to her husband (_filiae loco_).
        
    - **Inheritance:** She inherited from her husband as an agnatic heir (like a child), but lost all rights of inheritance from her birth family.
        
    - **Creation:** Established by strict rituals:
        
        - _Confarreatio:_ A religious ceremony involving a cake of spelt (for Patricians).
            
        - _Coemptio:_ A symbolic "sale" of the wife to the husband.
            
        - _Usus:_ Acquisition by prescription (living together for one year). If she stayed away for 3 nights (_Trinoctium_), she avoided the _Manus_.
            
- **Marriage _Sine Manu_ (Without Power):**
    
    - **Concept:** The "Free Marriage." The wife remained legally a member of her **father's family** (under his _Patria Potestas_). She did _not_ pass under the husband's power.
        
    - **Legal Status:** She was legally a stranger to her husband. Her property remained hers (separate property).
        
    - **Inheritance:** She did not automatically inherit from her husband (since she wasn't in his agnatic family), nor he from her.
        
    - **Dominance:** By the late Republic and Empire, this became the **standard form** of marriage, giving Roman women significant financial independence.
        

**2. Essentials of a Valid Marriage** For a marriage to be _Justae Nuptiae_ (Civil Law Marriage), three conditions were needed:

- **_Connubium:_** The legal capacity to marry each other. Both had to be citizens (or have specific grant).
    
- **_Puberty:_** The parties must be of age (14 for males, 12 for females).
    
- **_Consent (Affectio Maritalis):_** This was the most important element. Marriage depended on the _continuing_ consent of the parties to live as husband and wife. No formal ceremony was strictly required legally, only the intent (_Affectio_) and the act of taking the wife to the husband's house (_Deductio_).
    

---

#### II. Divorce (_Divortium_)

Because Roman marriage was based on "continuing consent," it could be dissolved relatively easily compared to modern or Canon law.

- **By Mutual Consent:** If both parties agreed to end the union.
    
- **By Repudiation (_Repudium_):** One party could unilaterally end the marriage by withdrawing consent. In the classical period, no state approval was needed. The party simply sent a messenger or a letter of divorce.
    
- **Financial Consequence:** The main legal complication was the **Dowry** (_Dos_). Upon divorce, the husband generally had to return the Dowry to the wife (or her father), which acted as a check against frivolous divorces.
    

---

#### III. The Link to Inheritance (Succession)

In Roman Law, Marriage and Inheritance are inseparable because inheritance was originally based on **Agnatic** ties (legal kinship through males), which only a valid marriage could create.

**1. The Concept of _Heres_ (Heir)** The Roman concept of "Heir" is different from the English concept.

- **Universal Succession:** The heir succeeds to the **entire legal personality** of the deceased.
    
- **Assets and Liabilities:** The heir gets all the property but also becomes personally liable for all the **debts** of the deceased. If the debts exceeded the assets, the heir had to pay out of his own pocket (until Justinian introduced the "Benefit of Inventory").
    

**2. Classes of Heirs (Introduction)** Before discussing Wills (Topic 20.4), one must understand _who_ the natural heirs were in the family:

- **_Sui Heredes_** **(Proper Heirs):** Persons under the _Patria Potestas_ of the deceased who became independent (_Sui Juris_) upon his death.
    
    - _Includes:_ Sons, daughters (unmarried), wife (in _Manu_ marriage).
        
    - _Feature:_ They were "necessary heirs"—they could not refuse the inheritance (originally).
        
- **_Extranei Heredes_** **(External Heirs):** Any heirs outside the immediate family (e.g., brothers, uncles, friends). They had to voluntarily accept the inheritance.
    

---

#### IV. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Affectio Maritalis:** This is the key keyword for Roman Marriage. It means the "intention to be married." If this intention stopped, the marriage stopped.
    
- **The Dowry (_Dos_):** Always mention the _Dos_. Even in "Free Marriage" (_Sine Manu_), the wife's family gave a Dowry to the husband to help with household expenses. It was the husband's property during marriage, but he had to account for it if the marriage ended.
    
- **Status of Children:**
    
    - If _Justae Nuptiae_ (Valid Roman Marriage): Children are in Father's power.
        
    - If no _Connubium_ (e.g., Roman + Foreigner): Children follow the status of the **Mother** and are _Sui Juris_ (not under Father's power).
