### 20.4 Wills and Intestacy (_Testamentum et Ab Intestato_) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** The Roman horror of dying "Intestate" (without a will) drove the development of a sophisticated succession law. The concept of "Universal Succession"—where the heir steps completely into the shoes of the deceased—is the foundation of modern European inheritance law.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** A critical exam topic. You are frequently asked to "Discuss the essentials of a valid Roman Will" or "Compare the order of intestate succession under the Twelve Tables and Justinian."
    

---

#### I. The Concept of Universal Succession

Before discussing the rules, one must grasp the underlying theory. In Roman Law, death did not dissolve the legal personality of the deceased.

- **The Heir (_Heres_):** The heir was not just a recipient of property; he was the **continuation** of the deceased person.
    
- **Universal Succession:** The heir succeeded to the _Universitas Juris_ (the totality of rights and duties). This meant he acquired all assets but was also personally liable for all **debts**, even if they exceeded the value of the estate (unless he used the _Benefit of Inventory_ introduced later by Justinian).
    

---

#### II. Testamentary Succession (Wills)

The making of a Will (_Testamentum_) was a central feature of Roman life. It was defined by Modestinus as _"The legal declaration of our intentions which we wish to be performed after our death."_

1. Essentials of a Valid Will

For a will to be valid, it had to meet strict requirements:

- **_Testamentifactio_ (Capacity):**
    
    - The Testator must have the right to make a will (must be a Roman Citizen, _Sui Juris_, and of sound mind).
        
    - The Heir must have the right to receive (Slaves could be heirs if simultaneously freed).
        
- **Institution of an Heir (_Institutio Heredis_):** This was the "head and foundation" (_caput et fundamentum_) of the will.
    
    - The will **must** expressly name an heir ("Let Titius be my heir").
        
    - If this clause was invalid or missing, the **entire will collapsed**, even the legacies within it.
        
- **Formalities:**
    
    - _Early Law:_ Wills were made orally before the assembly (_Comitia Calata_) or by a ritual sale of the estate (_Per aes et libram_).
        
    - _Later Law (Praetorian/Imperial):_ A written document sealed by **7 witnesses** became the standard.
        

**2. Inofficious Will (_Querela Inofficiosi Testamenti_)**

- **Concept:** Romans believed a father had a moral duty to provide for his close family. If he disinherited his children without good cause, the will was considered "Unduteous" or "Inofficious" (essentially implying he was insane).
    
- **Remedy:** The disinherited children could sue to have the will set aside and inherit as if he died intestate. This is the ancestor of the modern "forced share" or "legitim" in Civil Law systems.
    

---

#### III. Intestate Succession (_Ab Intestato_)

If a Roman died without a valid will (or the will failed), the law determined the heirs. The rules evolved drastically over 1,000 years.

1. The Twelve Tables (Early Law - Strict Agnatic)

The Twelve Tables based inheritance purely on Agnatic kinship (power/male line), ignoring blood ties through females.

- **Order of Succession:**
    
    1. **_Sui Heredes_ (Own Heirs):** Children under the father's power at the time of death. (Includes adopted children and wife _in manu_).
        
    2. **_Proximus Agnatus_ (Nearest Agnate):** If no _Sui_, it went to the nearest male relative through the male line (e.g., brother, uncle).
        
    3. **_Gentiles_ (Clansmen):** If no agnates, it went to the wider clan (Gens).
        

> **Critique:** This system was harsh. An emancipated son got nothing. A mother got nothing from her son (unless in _manu_).

2. Justinian's Reforms (Final Law - Blood Relationship)

By the time of Justinian (Novels 118 & 127), the law was modernized to base inheritance on Cognatic kinship (blood relationship), treating males and females mostly equally.

- **Order of Succession (Novels 118):**
    
    1. **Descendants:** Sons and daughters inherit equally. (Grandchildren represent deceased parents).
        
    2. **Ascendants & Germana Siblings:** Parents and full brothers/sisters inherit together.
        
    3. **Half-Siblings:** Consanguineous (same father) or Uterine (same mother).
        
    4. **Collaterals:** Other relatives (cousins, etc.).
        

---

#### IV. Legacies (_Legata_)

A **Legacy** is different from an **Inheritance**.

- **Inheritance:** The Heir takes the _whole_ estate (universal succession) and debts.
    
- **Legacy:** A specific gift (e.g., "I give my gold ring to Marcus") carved out of the estate. The Legatee receives the gift but is **not** liable for the debts. The Heir must pay the debts first, then give the legacy.
    

---

#### V. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **"The Will speaks from death":** A will is "ambulatory"—it can be changed at any time until the testator dies. The last valid will revokes all previous ones.
    
- **The "Necessary Heir":** A slave appointed as an heir was called a "Necessary Heir" because he could not refuse the inheritance. This was often done by bankrupt Romans to pass the shame of insolvency (_Infamia_) onto the slave rather than the family name.
    
- **Exam Strategy:** If asked about "Succession," always structure your answer chronologically: _Twelve Tables_ (Rigid/Agnatic) $\rightarrow$ _Praetorian Law_ (Correction/Bonorum Possessio) $\rightarrow$ _Justinian_ (Modern/Cognatic).
