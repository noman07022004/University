## 16. Doctrines (Inheritance) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** These doctrines are the mathematical and logical rules used to calculate exactly _how much_ share an heir gets. Without them, you might know _who_ inherits, but not _what proportion_ they receive.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** These concepts are frequently tested in short notes (e.g., "Write a short note on _Per Stirpes_") or as the underlying mechanism to solve problem-based questions involving multiple generations of heirs.
    

---

### Introduction

In Hindu Law, simply identifying the heirs is not enough. Several specific doctrines govern the _quantum_ (amount) of the share and the _nature_ of the right the heir possesses. These doctrines help resolve conflicts when heirs of different degrees (e.g., a son and a grandson of a predeceased son) claim property simultaneously, or when heirs attempt to transfer their future rights before the owner dies.

---

### I. Doctrine of Representation

**Syllabus Covered:** _16.1 Doctrine of representation_

The **Doctrine of Representation** allows a descendant to step into the shoes of their deceased ancestor and inherit the share that the ancestor would have taken if they were alive.

1. **The Rule:** If a male Hindu dies leaving behind a son, and a grandson (from a _predeceased_ son), the grandson is not excluded by the living son. instead, the grandson "represents" his dead father and takes the share his father would have received.
    
2. **Scope:** This doctrine extends to three degrees of male descendants in the male line:
    
    - Son
        
    - Grandson (Son of a predeceased son)
        
    - Great-grandson (Son of a predeceased grandson)
        
3. **Application in Schools:**
    
    - **Mitakshara:** It is a fundamental part of the coparcenary system. A son, grandson, and great-grandson all have a birthright.
        
    - **Dayabhaga:** Even though the Dayabhaga school does not recognize birthrights, it **does** recognize the Doctrine of Representation for succession. This is because a grandson offers the same spiritual benefit (_Pinda_) to the grandfather that his deceased father would have offered.
        

> **Example:** $A$ has two sons, $S1$ and $S2$. $S1$ dies before $A$, leaving a son $GS$.
> 
> - When $A$ dies, the property is divided into two shares (one for $S1$'s branch, one for $S2$).
>     
> - $S2$ takes 50%.
>     
> - $GS$ takes the other 50% (representing his dead father $S1$).
>

### II. Succession _Per Stirpes_ and _Per Capita_

**Syllabus Covered:** _16.4 Succession per stirpes and succession per capita_

These are the two methods of dividing property among heirs.

**1. Succession _Per Stirpes_ (By Roots/Branches)**

- **Definition:** Distribution according to the stock or branch. This method is linked directly to the Doctrine of Representation.
    
- **Application:** It applies mainly to the male descendants (Son, Grandson, Great-grandson).
    
- **How it works:** The estate is divided by the number of original branches (sons), regardless of how many people are currently alive in that branch.
    
- **Example:** A man has two sons. Son A has 1 child. Son B has 5 children. Both Son A and Son B die. The grandfather dies.
    
    - The property is divided into **2 halves** (one for A's branch, one for B's branch).
        
    - A's 1 child gets **50%**.
        
    - B's 5 children share the other 50% (getting **10% each**).
        

**2. Succession _Per Capita_ (By Heads)**

- **Definition:** Distribution per individual. Every claimant gets an equal share, regardless of which branch they belong to.
    
- **Application:** This usually applies to heirs in the same degree of relationship who do not rely on the doctrine of representation (e.g., when daughters inherit, or collaterals in some contexts).
    
- **How it works:** Total Estate ÷ Total Number of Heirs.
    
- **Example:** If the rule was _Per Capita_ in the scenario above (2 branches, but 6 total grandchildren), the estate would be divided by 6. Everyone would get **16.6%**.
    
    - _Note:_ In classical Hindu law, Strangers/Collaterals often take per capita.

### III. Doctrine of _Spes Successionis_

**Syllabus Covered:** _16.2 Doctrine of spes successionis_

This is a Latin term meaning **"Expectation of Succession."**

1. **Concept:** It refers to the interest of a probable heir who hopes to succeed to property _in the future_, but whose right has not yet vested (become fixed).
    
2. **The Reversioner's Right:** While a Hindu widow holds the "Widow's Estate" (Limited Estate), the husband's male relatives (Reversioners) have no actual ownership. They only have a _Spes Successionis_—a hope that they will survive the widow and inherit the property.
    
3. **Transferability:**
    
    - Under Section 6(a) of the _Transfer of Property Act, 1882_, a _Spes Successionis_ **cannot be transferred**.
        
    - You cannot sell or mortgage a "possibility." If a reversioner sells his "future share" while the widow is still alive, that sale is **void ab initio** (invalid from the start).

### IV. Co-heirs

**Syllabus Covered:** _16.3 Co-heirs_

When multiple people inherit property together, they are called co-heirs. The way they hold this property differs by school.

1. **Tenancy-in-Common (Dayabhaga Rule):**
    
    - When heirs (even sons) succeed to property under Dayabhaga law, they hold it as **Tenants-in-Common**.
        
    - **Feature:** Each heir has a defined, specific share (e.g., "I own 1/3rd"). Even if they haven't physically divided the land, their share is legally distinct. If one dies, his share goes to his own heirs.
        
2. **Joint Tenancy (Mitakshara Rule):**
    
    - When coparceners inherit/hold ancestral property, they hold it as **Joint Tenants**.
        
    - **Feature:** No one has a defined numerical share. They have a fluctuating interest.
        
    - **Survivorship:** If one dies, his interest merges with the others (Doctrine of Survivorship), rather than passing to his heirs (subject to statutory exceptions like the 1937 Act).
        
3. **Daughters as Co-heirs:**
    
    - Under both schools, if two daughters inherit their father's property, they take as **Joint Tenants with Right of Survivorship**.
        
    - If one daughter dies, the other takes the whole estate. It does _not_ go to the deceased daughter's heirs until the last daughter dies.

### V. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Distinguish Representation vs. Survivorship:**
    
    - _Representation_ means "I take my dead father's place to get his share."
        
    - _Survivorship_ means "My dead brother's share disappears and merges into mine."
        
- **Problem Question Tip:** If you see a problem where a Hindu man dies leaving a son ($S1$) and two grandsons ($G1, G2$) from a predeceased son ($S2$):
    
    - Apply **Per Stirpes**.
        
    - Divide the property by 2 (for the two original sons).
        
    - $S1$ gets 1/2.
        
    - $G1$ and $G2$ together get the other 1/2 (share equally, so 1/4 each).
        
- **The "Transfer" Trap:** Examiners often ask: _"X, a reversioner, sells his chance of inheriting Y's estate to Z. Is the sale valid?"_
    
    - **Answer:** No. Citing the _Doctrine of Spes Successionis_ and Section 6(a) of the Transfer of Property Act is mandatory here.
