### 16.1 Doctrine of Representation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the mathematical engine of Hindu inheritance. Without this doctrine, inheritance calculations would fail whenever a generation is missing (e.g., a predeceased son). It explains _why_ a grandson inherits alongside an uncle.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Problem questions inevitably involve a mix of living sons and grandsons of deceased sons. You must apply this doctrine to calculate their shares correctly.
    

---

#### I. Definition and Concept

The **Doctrine of Representation** is a fundamental rule of succession that allows a descendant to step into the shoes of their deceased ancestor and inherit the share that the ancestor would have taken if they were alive.

- **The Principle:** The son of a predeceased son represents his father. He is not excluded by his uncles; rather, he takes the place of his father in the line of inheritance.
    
- **Scope:** This right extends to **three degrees** of male descendants in the male line:
    
    1. Son
        
    2. Grandson (Son of a predeceased Son)
        
    3. Great-grandson (Son of a predeceased Grandson)
        

#### II. Application in the Two Schools

While the outcome is often the same, the theoretical justification differs between the schools.

**1. Mitakshara School**

- **Basis:** The doctrine is a natural consequence of the **Coparcenary** system. Since the son, grandson, and great-grandson all acquire a **Right by Birth** (_Janmasvatvavada_) in the ancestral property, their interest exists independently.
    
- **Operation:** Even if the father dies before the grandfather, the son's birthright remains. He represents his father's branch and claims the share that belonged to that branch.
    

**2. Dayabhaga School**

- **Basis:** The Dayabhaga school does not recognize the right by birth. However, it **does** strictly apply the Doctrine of Representation based on **Spiritual Benefit**.
    
- **Reasoning:** A grandson offers the _Pinda_ (funeral cake) to his grandfather. This Pinda is spiritually efficacious because it represents the Pinda his deceased father would have offered. Therefore, the grandson has the same spiritual capacity as the son and deserves the same share.
    

---

#### III. How it Works (Calculation Rule)

The Doctrine of Representation results in a division of property **Per Stirpes** (according to roots/branches), not _Per Capita_ (according to heads).

Illustrative Example:

Let us assume a Hindu male ($P$) has three sons: $A$, $B$, and $C$.

- $A$ is alive.
    
- $B$ is dead, leaving two sons ($GS1$, $GS2$).
    
- $C$ is dead, leaving three sons ($GS3$, $GS4$, $GS5$).
    

**Distribution when $P$ dies:**

1. The estate is divided into **3 equal shares** (one for each original son/branch).
    
2. **Son A** takes his **1/3** share directly.
    
3. **B's Branch:** The 1/3 share meant for $B$ goes to his sons ($GS1$ & $GS2$). They divide it equally.
    
    - $GS1$ gets 1/6.
        
    - $GS2$ gets 1/6.
        
4. **C's Branch:** The 1/3 share meant for $C$ goes to his sons ($GS3$, $GS4$, $GS5$). They divide it equally.
    
    - Each gets 1/9.
        

> **Key Takeaway:** $GS1$ (a grandson) gets a larger share (1/6) than $GS3$ (also a grandson, who gets 1/9) because $GS1$ belongs to a smaller branch. They do _not_ all share equally as individuals.

---

#### IV. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **Does it apply to Daughters?**
    
    - In classical Hindu law (applicable in Bangladesh), the Doctrine of Representation applies strictly to **male descendants**.
        
    - If a daughter dies before her father, her children generally do _not_ represent her to claim her share (unless they qualify independently as Daughter's Sons, who are separate heirs).
        
- **Limit of 3 Degrees:** The doctrine stops at the Great-grandson. A Great-great-grandson cannot claim representation if the intermediate links are broken.
    
- **Exam Phrase:** Use the phrase **"The son represents the predeceased father"** in your answers. It is the standard legal phrasing.
