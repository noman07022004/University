## 11.1 Categories of Endowment ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Identifying the category of an endowment is the first step in any legal dispute. The rights of the public, the powers of the manager, and the intervention of the court all depend on whether the endowment is Public or Private, Real or Illusory.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** A critical exam topic. Questions often ask to "Distinguish between Public and Private Endowments" or "Explain the tests to determine if a Debuttar is real or sham."
    

---

### Introduction

Hindu Law classifies endowments (_Debuttar_) into several distinct categories based on their object, scope, and validity. The most legally significant classification is between **Public** and **Private** endowments, as this determines the right of the public to worship and the power of the State to intervene.

The main categories are:

1. **Public and Private** (Based on Beneficiaries)
    
2. **Real and Illusory/Sham** (Based on Validity)
    
3. **Absolute and Partial** (Based on Extent of Dedication)
    
4. **Religious and Charitable** (Based on Purpose)
    

---

### 1. Public vs. Private Endowment

This is the most important distinction in Hindu Law.

#### **A. Public Endowment (_Sarvajanin Debuttar_)**

- **Definition:** An endowment is public when the dedication is for the use and benefit of the **public** at large or a specified section of it.
    
- **Beneficiaries:** The general public. They have a legal **Right of Worship**. They can enter the temple as a matter of right, not permission.
    
- **Management:** Often regulated by statutes (like the Religious Endowments Act) or a large body of trustees.
    
- **Intervention:** In cases of mismanagement, any two persons can sue the Shebait under **Section 92 of the Civil Procedure Code (CPC)**.
    
- **Example:** The Dhakeshwari Temple or Ramna Kali Mandir in Dhaka.
    

#### **B. Private Endowment (_Pariwarik Debuttar_)**

- **Definition:** An endowment is private when the dedication is for the worship of a family deity (_Ishta Devata_) by the members of the donor's family **only**.
    
- **Beneficiaries:** Only the family members of the founder. The public has **no right** of entry. If outsiders are allowed, it is by "leave and license" (permission) which can be revoked.
    
- **Consensus:** The family members (if all agree) can theoretically convert the property or move the Idol (though strictly religious law forbids it, civil law allows it by consensus).
    
- **Intervention:** Section 92 of the CPC does **not** apply. The public cannot sue.
    

#### **C. Tests to Determine the Nature**

Since deeds are often silent, Courts use these tests to decide if a temple is Public or Private:

1. **Intention:** Did the founder intend access for the public?
    
2. **User:** Has the public worshipped there freely for a long time without asking permission? (Long user suggests Public).
    
3. **Structure:** Is the temple built inside the residential house (Private) or separate with access to the main road (Public)?
    
4. **Funding:** Is it maintained by family funds (Private) or public subscriptions/donations (Public)?

### 2. Real vs. Illusory (Sham) Endowment

This classification deals with the **genuineness** of the dedication.

#### **A. Real Endowment**

- **Definition:** A genuine transfer where the donor completely divests himself of ownership and vests the property in the Idol.
    
- **Conduct:** The income from the property is actually used for the _Puja_, repairs, and festivals.
    

#### **B. Illusory (Sham) Endowment**

- **Definition:** A deed of _Debuttar_ created merely to defraud creditors or escape taxes, while the donor continues to enjoy the property himself.
    
- **Legal Status:** Void. The property is treated as the secular property of the donor and can be attached by creditors.
    
- **Test of Deception:** The Court looks at the accounts. If the deed says "All income to the Idol" but the "Shebait" spends 90% of it on his own luxury car and only 10% on flowers for the Idol, the endowment is likely Illusory.
    

---

### 3. Absolute vs. Partial Dedication

This relates to **how much** of the property interest is given.

#### **A. Absolute Dedication**

- **Definition:** The entire property (both the corpus and the income) is given to the Idol.
    
- **Effect:** The property belongs wholly to the Deity. The heirs of the donor have no claim over it.
    

#### **B. Partial Dedication (Charge)**

- **Definition:** The property remains with the heirs, but a **Charge** is created on it for religious purposes.
    
- **Example:** "My son shall inherit this land, but he must spend 5000 Taka per year for the Durga Puja."
    
- **Effect:** The son becomes the owner. He can sell the land, but the buyer takes it subject to the liability of paying 5000 Taka for the Puja. The land is alienable (unlike absolute Debuttar).
    

---

### 4. Religious vs. Charitable (_Ishta_ vs. _Purta_)

Hindu Law distinguishes between acts that lead to heaven (_Ishta_) and acts that help humanity (_Purta_).

- **Religious (_Ishta_):** Dedications for the worship of gods, construction of temples, and performance of sacrifices.
    
- **Charitable (_Purta_):** Dedications for the benefit of the public, such as digging wells, building hospitals (_Dharamshalas_), schools, or feeding the poor.
    
- **Legal Difference:** While both are valid "Endowments," traditionally _Purta_ works were always considered Public, whereas _Ishta_ works could be Private (Family Idol).
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Partial Dedication" Trick:** If an exam problem says: _"X dedicates his house to the Idol but says 'My family shall reside in the surplus rooms'."_
> 
> - **Analysis:** This is usually a **Partial Dedication**. The property descends to heirs subject to the charge of worship. The heirs can sell the house, provided the worship is maintained.
>     

> **Section 92 CPC:** Always mention this section when discussing Public Endowments. It is the primary statutory remedy for mismanagement of public trusts.

> **Family Concensus:** In a Private Endowment, if all family members agree to abolish the endowment, courts sometimes allow it. In a Public Endowment, this is impossible—once Public, always Public.
