## 11.9 Alienation of Debutter Property ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the most practical aspect of endowment law. Temple lands are often valuable, and corrupt Shebaits often try to sell them. Understanding when such a sale is valid (and when it is void) is critical for protecting religious property.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is a certain exam topic. Problem questions almost always involve a Shebait selling or leasing temple land to pay for repairs or festivals. You must apply the "Legal Necessity" test.
    

---

### Introduction

The general rule of Hindu Law is that **Debutter property is inalienable**. Since the property belongs to the Deity (who is a perpetual minor), the Shebait (manager) has no title to it and therefore cannot sell, mortgage, or gift it away. The corpus of the endowment is intended to be perpetual.

However, this rule is not absolute. The Privy Council recognized that in emergencies, the preservation of the estate is more important than the rule against alienation. Therefore, a Shebait can alienate the property in exceptional circumstances.

---

### 1. The Power of Alienation

The power of a Shebait to alienate (sell/mortgage) Debutter property is exactly the same as that of a **Natural Guardian** managing a minor's estate. It is governed by the principles laid down in **_Hanoomanpersaud v. Mussumat Babooee_** (1856).

A Shebait can alienate property **ONLY** in two cases:

1. **Legal Necessity (_Apatkale_):** Urgent needs to preserve the estate or the religious service.
    
2. **Benefit of the Estate:** Transactions that are clearly profitable and prudent for the endowment.
    

### 2. What Constitutes "Legal Necessity"?

The necessity must be actual, immediate, and unavoidable.

- **Valid Necessities:**
    
    - Repairing the temple building to prevent it from collapsing.
        
    - Defending the Idol's title in a lawsuit (litigation expenses).
        
    - Paying government revenue/taxes to save the land from being auctioned.
        
    - Conducting **essential** daily worship (_Nitya Seva_) or mandatory festivals if income has failed (e.g., due to drought).
        
- **Invalid Grounds:**
    
    - Purchasing luxury items for the Shebait.
        
    - Expanding the temple merely for prestige without funds.
        
    - Spending on non-essential, grand festivals when there is no money.
        

### 3. Burden of Proof

- **The Rule:** The burden of proof lies entirely on the **Alienee (Buyer/Lender)**.
    
- **What must he prove?**
    
    1. That there was an **actual necessity** for the loan/sale.
        
    2. OR, that he made **bona fide inquiries** into the existence of the necessity and honestly believed it existed (even if it didn't).
        
- **Risk:** If the buyer fails to inquire, and the Shebait wastes the money, the sale will be set aside, and the buyer loses both the land and the money.
    

### 4. Permanent Leases

- **The Rule:** A Shebait generally **cannot grant a permanent lease** (lease for 99 years or perpetuity) of Debutter property with a fixed rent.
    
- **Reason:** Fixed rent ignores future inflation, which permanently damages the financial health of the Deity.
    
- **Exception:** A permanent lease may be valid only if it is justified by **"imperative necessity"** (e.g., the land is a swamp and no one else will take it to cultivate unless given a permanent lease).
    

### 5. Remedies for Unauthorized Alienation

If a Shebait sells property without legal necessity, what happens?

1. **Voidable:** The transaction is generally voidable, not void ab initio (in some views). It can be set aside.
    
2. **Recovery Suit:** The succeeding Shebait (or the Idol through a next friend/devotee) can file a suit to recover the property.
    
3. **Limitation:** Under the **Limitation Act**, the period to recover possession of immovable property sold by a previous manager is **12 years** from the death, resignation, or removal of the transferor (Shebait).

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Hanoomanpersaud’s Case:** You **MUST** cite this case. It is the foundation for the "Legal Necessity" doctrine for both Guardians and Shebaits.
> 
> - _Principle:_ "The power of the manager for an infant heir to charge an estate not his own is, under the Hindu law, a limited and qualified power. It can only be exercised rightly in a case of need, or for the benefit of the estate."
>     

> **Sale of the Idol Itself:**
> 
> - _Question:_ Can a Shebait sell the Idol (the statue)?
>     
> - _Answer:_ **NEVER.** It is _Res Extra Commercium_ (things outside commerce). Trafficking in deities is strictly prohibited and void.
>     

> **Doctrine of Factum Valet:** Does not apply here. A sale without necessity is invalid, no matter how formal the deed is.
