## 11.4 Capacity of the Settlor ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Just like a Will or a Gift, an Endowment is a transfer of property. Therefore, the person creating it (the Settlor/Founder) must be legally competent. Understanding the specific restrictions on a "Hindu Widow" or a "Mitakshara Karta" is vital for determining if a temple dedication is valid or voidable.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** Questions often appear as problem scenarios: _"A Hindu widow dedicates her entire husband's estate to a temple. Is this valid?"_ (Answer: No, only a small portion is allowed).
    

---

### Introduction

The "Settlor" (also called the Founder or Donor) is the person who establishes the endowment and dedicates the property to the Deity. Since an endowment involves the permanent alienation of property, the law requires that the settlor must have the legal **capacity** to transfer ownership.

Generally, any Hindu who is of **sound mind** and has attained **majority** can dedicate their property for religious or charitable purposes, provided they have a valid title to the property.

---

### 1. General Qualifications

The basic qualifications for a settlor are the same as for any transferor under the Transfer of Property Act or Contract Act:

1. **Sound Mind:** The settlor must be capable of understanding the nature and effect of the act. A dedication made by a lunatic or a person under the influence of intoxication is **Void**.
    
2. **Majority:** The settlor must have attained the age of **18 years** (under the _Majority Act, 1875_). A minor cannot create a valid endowment, even through a guardian (unless the guardian does so with Court permission for the minor's benefit).
    
3. **Legal Title:** A person cannot dedicate what they do not own. A trespasser or a lessee (with a short lease) cannot dedicate the land permanently to an Idol.
    

### 2. Capacity under Dayabhaga Law (Bangladesh)

Since Bangladesh is primarily governed by the Dayabhaga school, the rules here are liberal regarding the father.

- **The Father:** Under Dayabhaga law, a Hindu father is the **absolute owner** of all his property (whether ancestral or self-acquired). Therefore, he has the **absolute capacity** to dedicate any portion (or even the whole) of his property to a religious endowment without the consent of his sons.
    
- **The Coparcener:** A Dayabhaga coparcener can validly dedicate his own defined share of the joint family property.
    

### 3. Capacity under Mitakshara Law

For those governed by Mitakshara law in Bangladesh (migrant communities), the rules are stricter due to the joint family system.

- **The Karta (Manager):** A Karta generally cannot alienate joint family property. However, he has a special power to make a gift of a **small portion** of family property for religious purposes (_Gifts of Affection_ or pious acts). He cannot dedicate the _entire_ estate.
    
- **The Coparcener:** An individual coparcener cannot dedicate his undivided interest without the consent of others (unless he separates first).
    

### 4. Capacity of a Hindu Widow

This is a specific and frequent exam point. A widow holding a "Widow's Estate" (limited interest) has restricted powers.

- **For Husband's Spiritual Benefit:** A widow **CAN** dedicate a portion of her husband's property for religious acts that conduce to the spiritual welfare of her deceased husband (e.g., _Sraddha_, pilgrimage to Gaya).
    
- **Limit (Reasonable Portion):** She cannot alienate the **whole** or a substantial part of the estate for this purpose. It must be a "reasonable portion" (usually interpreted as a small fraction).
    
- **For Her Own Benefit:** She **CANNOT** dedicate her husband's property for her _own_ spiritual salvation (e.g., building a temple for her own merit). She can only do that with her _Stridhana_ (absolute property).
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Widow's Limit" Trap:** A common problem question: _"A widow dedicates the entire ancestral house to a Deity for her husband's salvation."_
> 
> - **Verdict:** **Invalid.**
>     
> - **Reason:** She can only dedicate a _reasonable small portion_. Dedicating the whole estate disinherits the reversioners (husband's heirs) and is not permitted.
>     

> **Dayabhaga Father:** Remember that in Bangladesh, a father can theoretically dedicate **100%** of his property to a temple, leaving his sons with nothing. The sons cannot challenge this on the ground of "ancestral property" because they have no right by birth under Dayabhaga.
