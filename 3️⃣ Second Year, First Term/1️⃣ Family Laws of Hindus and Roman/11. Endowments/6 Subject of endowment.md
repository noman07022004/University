## 11.6 Subject of Endowment ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** You cannot create a trust with "nothing" or "vague promises." Understanding that the subject matter must be specific property is crucial for drafting valid deeds.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** Problem questions sometimes feature vague dedications like "I give a portion of my income to the temple." You must determine if this is specific enough to be valid.
    

---

### Introduction

The "Subject of Endowment" refers to the **property** that is dedicated to the religious or charitable purpose. Just as there must be a donor (Settlor) and a beneficiary (Object), there must be a specific thing (Subject) that is transferred.

In Hindu Law, the rule is simple: **Any property** capable of being alienated (transferred) can be the subject of an endowment, provided it is **specific** and **identifiable**.

---

### 1. Requirement of Specificity

- **The Rule:** The property dedicated must be specified with reasonable certainty.
    
- **Vague Dedications are Void:** A declaration like _"I dedicate some of my land to the Temple"_ is **Void for Uncertainty**. The court must be able to identify exactly which plot of land belongs to the Idol.
    
- **Description:** The deed should ideally specify boundaries, plot numbers, or specific assets (e.g., "My gold necklace weighing 10 grams").
    

### 2. Types of Property

Almost any transferable asset can be dedicated.

#### **A. Immovable Property**

- **Land and Buildings:** The most common form of endowment. A donor can dedicate a house, a garden, or agricultural land.
    
- **Benefits arising out of land:** A donor can dedicate the **rent** or **profits** of a specific property without transferring the property itself (creating a Charge).
    

#### **B. Movable Property**

- **Money and Jewelry:** Dedicating cash (e.g., a fixed deposit) or ornaments for the Idol is valid.
    
- **Chattels:** Furniture, utensils for _Puja_, or even livestock (like dedicating a cow for milk for the deity) are valid subjects.
    

### 3. Future Property

- **General Rule:** A dedication of property that _does not exist_ at the time (future property) is generally **Void**.
    
- **Exception:** If a valid endowment is already created with existing property, the donor can direct that _future_ accumulations (e.g., future rents) be added to the endowment.
    

### 4. Part of Property (Partial Dedication)

- **The Concept:** A donor does not have to dedicate the _entirety_ of a property.
    
- **Fractional Share:** He can dedicate "one-half share" of a specific house. This is valid because the share is specific, even if the property is undivided.
    
- **Charge:** He can dedicate a specific sum of money to be paid annually from the income of a specific estate. This creates a "Charge" on the estate, while the estate itself remains with the heirs.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Uncertainty" Trap:**
> 
> - _Problem:_ "X executes a deed saying 'I dedicate enough money from my estate to build a small temple'."
>     
> - _Analysis:_ This might be challenged for uncertainty ("enough money" is vague). However, courts sometimes uphold it by calculating a "reasonable amount" based on the donor's status. It's safer to specify the amount.
>     

> **Debt as Subject:** Can a person dedicate a "debt" owed to him? Yes, an **Actionable Claim** can be dedicated. The Idol then becomes the creditor and can sue to recover the money.
