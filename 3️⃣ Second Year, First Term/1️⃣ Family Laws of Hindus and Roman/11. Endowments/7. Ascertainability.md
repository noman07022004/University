## 11.7 Ascertainability ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** A trust or endowment cannot exist in a vacuum. The law demands to know _who_ will benefit. If the beneficiaries are so vague that the court cannot identify them (e.g., "for the benefit of my friends"), the endowment fails.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** This concept often appears in objective questions or as a sub-issue in problem questions regarding the validity of a deed.
    

---

### Introduction

The principle of **Ascertainability** refers to the requirement that the beneficiaries of an endowment must be identifiable. For a trust or endowment to be valid, the court must be able to know _who_ has the right to enforce it.

However, the application of this rule differs significantly between **Private** and **Public** endowments in Hindu Law.

### 1. Ascertainability in Private Endowments

In a Private Endowment (_Pariwarik Debuttar_), the beneficiaries are specific individuals—usually the members of the founder's family.

- **The Rule:** The beneficiaries must be **ascertained** or **capable of being ascertained**.
    
- **Application:** A trust for "the worship of the family deity by my descendants" is valid because, at any given time, the list of family members (descendants) can be ascertained.
    
- **Limitation:** If a private trust is created for a vague group (e.g., "for my friends"), it may fail for uncertainty because "friends" is not a legally ascertainable class.
    

### 2. Ascertainability in Public Endowments

In a Public Endowment (_Sarvajanin Debuttar_), the beneficiaries are the general public.

- **The Rule:** The beneficiaries constitute a body which is **incapable of ascertainment**. They are an uncertain and fluctuating body (e.g., "all Hindus" or "residents of Dhaka").
    
- **Validity:** Despite the beneficiaries being unascertainable individuals, the endowment is **Valid** provided the **Object/Purpose** is certain.
    
    - _Logic:_ In a public trust, it is not necessary to name every individual Hindu who will worship. It is enough to dedicate it "for the public."
        

### 3. Certainty of Object (The "Dharma" Rule)

While the _beneficiaries_ of a public trust can be fluctuating, the _purpose_ must be certain.

- **Specific Objects (Valid):**
    
    - "For feeding the poor" (Valid).
        
    - "For the worship of Goddess Kali" (Valid).
        
    - "For the education of Brahmins" (Valid).
        
- **Vague Objects (Void):**
    
    - **"For Dharma" (Religion):** A bequest simply for "Dharma" or "Punya-karye" (pious acts) is generally held to be **Void for Uncertainty**.
        
    - _Reason:_ The term "Dharma" is too broad. It could mean anything from feeding ants to building temples. The court cannot supervise such a vague trust because it doesn't know what specific act the founder intended.
        

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Fluctuating Body" Concept:** Use this phrase. It describes the public. Even though the _individuals_ change (people are born and die), the _body_ (the public) remains constant enough to be a beneficiary.

> **Runchordas v. Parvatibai:** This is the landmark Privy Council case which established that a bequest for "Dharma" is void for uncertainty. Citing this adds significant weight to your answer.
