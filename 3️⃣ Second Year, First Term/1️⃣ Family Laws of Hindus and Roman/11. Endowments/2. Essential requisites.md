## 11.2 Essential Requisites of Endowment ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Knowing the "ingredients" of a valid endowment is the first step in drafting a deed or challenging one in court. You must understand that a mere wish to build a temple is not enough; there must be specific legal elements present.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** A standard exam question asks: "What are the essential requisites for a valid Hindu religious endowment?" You need to list the specific requirements discussed below.
    

---

### Introduction

For a Hindu religious endowment (_Debuttar_) to be legally valid and binding, it is not sufficient to merely have a pious intention. The law requires the presence of certain specific elements. If any of these are missing, the endowment is void, and the property remains with the heirs of the donor.

Broadly, the essential requisites can be summarized as: **(1) Competency of the Settlor**, **(2) Valid Object**, **(3) Definite Property**, and **(4) Valid Dedication (Ceremony/Act)**.

---

### 1. The Four Pillars of Validity

According to the leading cases and Shastric principles, a valid endowment must satisfy these four conditions:

1. **Capacity of the Settlor:** The person creating the endowment (donor) must have the legal capacity to do so (Sound mind, Major, and Legal Title to the property). _(Detailed in 11.4)_.
    
2. **Valid Object:** The endowment must be for a purpose recognized by Hindu Law as religious or charitable (e.g., Idol worship, feeding the poor). The beneficiary (Idol/Math) must be ascertainable. _(Detailed in 11.5)_.
    
3. **Definite Property:** The subject matter of the endowment must be specific and identifiable property (e.g., "Plot No. 101," not "some of my land"). _(Detailed in 11.6)_.
    
4. **Dedication (The Act):** There must be a clear and unequivocal act of dedication, divesting the donor of ownership and vesting it in the Deity.
    

---

### 2. Formalities of Dedication (Ceremonies)

Unlike a secular Gift (which requires a registered deed) or a Trust (which requires a written instrument under the Trusts Act), the creation of a Hindu religious endowment is relatively informal in strict law, though highly ceremonial in practice.

#### **A. No Writing Necessary (The General Rule)**

- **Inter Vivos:** A Hindu can create an endowment _inter vivos_ (during his life) **orally** without any writing. The _Indian Trusts Act, 1882_ (and its BD equivalent) does not apply to Hindu religious endowments.
    
- **Exception:** If the endowment is created by a **Will**, it must be in writing and attested (as per the Succession Act).
    
- **Practice:** In modern times, a "Deed of Dedication" (_Arpannama_) is almost always executed and registered to avoid future disputes and meet the requirements of the Transfer of Property Act regarding immovable property.
    

#### **B. The Ceremonies: _Sankalpa_ and _Utsarga_**

The ancient law prescribed two essential religious rites to complete the dedication:

1. **Sankalpa (Resolution):**
    
    - This is the mental resolve or intention. The donor recites mantras stating his intention to dedicate the property for a specific religious purpose.
        
2. **Utsarga (Renunciation):**
    
    - This is the formal act of renunciation. The donor performs a ritual (usually with water/Ganges water) formally giving up his ownership over the property.
        
    - **Significance:** Once _Utsarga_ is performed, the property becomes _Debuttar_ (belonging to the God). The donor can no longer sell or reclaim it.
        

#### **C. _Prana Pratistha_ (Consecration of Idol)**

- If the endowment is for a temple, the dedication is not complete until the Idol is installed with the ceremony of **Prana Pratistha** (breathing life into the image).
    
- Before this ceremony, the image is just a statue; after it, it becomes a Deity (_Juristic Person_).
    

---

### 3. Absolute vs. Partial Dedication

A crucial requisite is determining the _extent_ of the dedication.

- **Absolute Dedication:** The donor gives the **entire** beneficial interest (corpus + income) to the Idol. The heirs take nothing.
    
- **Partial Dedication (Charge):** The donor retains the property (title remains with heirs), but creates a **Charge** on it for religious expenses.
    
    - _Example:_ "My son gets the house, but he must pay 10,000 Taka/year for the Puja."
        
    - _Result:_ The property is alienable (can be sold), but the buyer must continue paying for the Puja.
        

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Writing" Myth:** A common objective question: _"Is a written deed mandatory for a Hindu endowment?"_
> 
> - **Answer:** **No** (unless by Will). However, mention that in _practice_, courts look for a deed to prove the "clear intention."
>     

> **Ceremonies:** While _Sankalpa_ and _Utsarga_ are the traditional essentials, modern courts have held that if the **intention** is clear and the **conduct** (using income for the deity) is proved, the absence of specific religious ceremonies will not invalidate the endowment.

> **Trusts Act Exclusion:** Remember to mention that the **Trusts Act, 1882** explicitly exempts Hindu religious endowments. This is why the strict "writing and registration" rules of secular trusts don't apply automatically.
