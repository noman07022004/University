## 2.2 Modern Sources of Hindu Law ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** While ancient sources provide the _content_ of the law, modern sources provide the _authority_. You cannot understand how Hindu Law works in a modern courtroom (especially in Bangladesh) without understanding the dominance of statutes and precedents.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Questions often ask for a "Critical analysis of modern sources" or specifically about the doctrine of "Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience."

---

The Modern Sources of Hindu Law emerged during the British colonial administration and continue to function in the independent legal system of Bangladesh. Unlike the ancient sources (Sruti/Smriti) which were religious and claimed divine origin, modern sources are secular, state-enforced, and dynamic. They transformed Hindu Law from a "religious code of duties" into a "positive law of rights."

These sources are:

1. **Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience**
    
2. **Judicial Precedents (Case Law)**
    
3. **Legislation (Statutes)**
    

---

### 1. Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience

This is considered the most significant indirect source of modern Hindu Law. It served as a bridge between the ancient texts and the modern English legal system.

- **Origin:** In the 18th century, British administrators established a rule: "In cases where no specific rule exists in the Sruti, Smriti, or Custom, the decision shall be made according to _Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience_."
    
- **The "Gap-Filler":** The ancient Smritis did not cover every possible modern dispute. When the texts were silent, judges had to invent a solution.
    
- **Importing English Law:** In practice, British judges interpreted "Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience" to mean **English Common Law**.
    
    - _Result:_ They applied English rules of trust, specific performance, and equity to Hindu disputes, thereby modifying the law.
        
    - _Example:_ The Hindu law of "Wills" was almost entirely created through this principle, as ancient Hindus did not have a concept of a Will (testamentary succession).
        

### 2. Judicial Precedents (Case Law)

This refers to the body of law created by the decisions of the superior courts. It is based on the English doctrine of **Stare Decisis** (let the decision stand), meaning a decision by a higher court is binding on all lower courts.

- **Transformation:** Before the British, Hindu Law was interpreted by Pundits who could vary their opinions. The British courts replaced Pundits with binding written judgments, making the law **certain** and **uniform**.
    
- **Hierarchy of Authority in Bangladesh:**
    
    1. **The Privy Council (London):** From 1726 to 1947, this was the highest court of appeal for India. Its decisions (e.g., defining the rights of a Hindu widow) are still binding in Bangladesh unless overruled by the Supreme Court.
        
    2. **The Supreme Court of Bangladesh:** Decisions of the Appellate Division are the law of the land (Article 111 of the Constitution).
        
    3. **The High Court Division:** Its decisions are binding on subordinate courts.
        
- **Impact:** Today, if you want to know the law on "Hindu Joint Family Property," you do not read the _Manusmriti_; you read the leading Privy Council and Bangladeshi court judgments.
    

### 3. Legislation (Statutes)

Legislation is the supreme source of law. An Act of Parliament has the power to abrogate(বাতিল করা) (cancel), amend(সংশোধন করা), or modify(পরিবর্তন করা) any rule of Hindu Law derived from Sruti, Smriti, or Custom.

- **The Power of Parliament:** While ancient texts were "unchangeable divine words," modern statutes can change any part of the law to suit changing social needs.
    
- **Categories of Legislation:**
    
    1. **Remedial Acts:** Passed to correct specific social evils or restore rights.
        
        - _The Caste Disabilities Removal Act, 1850:_ Protected converts from losing inheritance.
            
        - _The Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856:_ Legalized remarriage.
            
    2. **Amending Acts:** Passed to alter specific property rights.
        
        - _The Hindu Women’s Rights to Property Act, 1937:_ Gave widows a share in property.
            
    3. **Administrative Acts:** Passed for better management.
        
        - _The Hindu Marriage Registration Act, 2012 (Bangladesh):_ Introduced optional registration.
            

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> Current Hierarchy of All Sources:
> 
> In a modern problem question, always apply sources in this order:
> 
> 1. **Statute** (The Act of Parliament overrides everything).
>     
> 2. **Precedent** (Supreme Court/Privy Council decisions).
>     
> 3. **Custom** (If proved valid).
>     
> 4. **Personal Law Text** (The Smritis/Dayabhaga).
>     

> **Key Term - "Stare Decisis":** Use this Latin term in your exam when discussing Precedents. It shows academic rigor. It means "to stand by things decided."

> **Wills:** Remember that the concept of a **Will** (giving property away after death) is **not** found in the Vedas or Smritis. It is entirely a creation of "Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience" and Case Law (specifically the _Tagore v. Tagore_ case).
