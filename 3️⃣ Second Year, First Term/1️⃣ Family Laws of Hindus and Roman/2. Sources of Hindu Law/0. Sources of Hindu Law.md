## 2. Sources of Hindu Law ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** You cannot interpret any legal principle without knowing its hierarchy. For example, knowing that "Custom overrides written text" or "Statutes override Custom" is fundamental to solving problems.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is one of the most frequent exam questions. Questions like "Discuss the sources of Hindu Law" or "Can custom override the Smritis?" are standard.


---

### Introduction

The "Sources of Hindu Law" refer to the origins from which the rules of human conduct were derived. Unlike modern law, which comes primarily from a Parliament (Legislature), Hindu Law has evolved over thousands of years through various channels.

Scholars categorize these sources into two broad historical groups:

1. **Ancient Sources (Traditional/Shastric):** The original religious and customary roots.
    
2. **Modern Sources (Anglo-Hindu):** The legal mechanisms introduced during and after the British rule.
    

---

### 2.1 Ancient Sources

These are the sources that developed the law before the arrival of the British. They are religiously grounded and evolved organically within the Hindu community.

#### 1. Sruti (The Vedas)

- **Meaning:** "What is heard." It is believed to be the direct language of God (divine revelation) heard by the ancient Sages.
    
- **Significance:** It is the primary and paramount(সর্বাপেক্ষা গুরুত্বপূর্ণ) source of authority. Theoretically, all law flows from the Sruti.
    
- **Content:** The four Vedas (Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva).
    
- **Legal Limitation:** While they are the ultimate authority, they contain very little positive law (rules for daily life). They focus mostly on religious rites and sacrifices. Thus, they are the source of _Dharma_ (duty), but not often the source of specific legal rules.
    

#### 2. Smriti (The Dharmashastras)

- **Meaning:** "What is remembered." Sages remembered the divine revelations and compiled them into written codes.
    
- **Significance:** These are the actual text-books of Hindu Law. Where the Sruti is silent, the Smriti speaks.
    
- **Classification:**
    
    - **Dharmasutras (Prose):** The earlier texts (e.g., Gautama, Baudhayana).
        
    - **Dharmashastras (Verse):** The later, more systematic codes.
        
        - _Manusmriti:_ The most ancient and authoritative (Orthodox).
            
        - _Yajnavalkya Smriti:_ More liberal and logical (basis of Mitakshara).
            
        - _Narada Smriti:_ Purely legal code (distinguished law from religion).
            

#### 3. Commentaries and Digests (Nibandhas)

- **Origin:** As society changed, the old Smritis became unclear or contradictory. Learned scholars wrote commentaries to interpret them.
    
- **Function:** Under the guise of "interpretation," these scholars actually modified the law to suit local customs and changing times.
    
- **Authority:** In the event of a conflict between an ancient Smriti and a later Commentary, the **Commentary prevails(প্রাধান্য লাভ করা)** because it represents the accepted law of that time and region.
    
    - _Example:_ The **Dayabhaga** (by Jimutavahana) is a digest that prevails in Bangladesh, while the **Mitakshara** (by Vijnaneshwara) prevails in most of India.
        

#### 4. Custom (Sadachara)

- **Meaning:** Habitual practices observed by a community for a long time.
    
- **Position:** Hindu Law is unique because it values Custom highly. The Privy Council famously stated: **"Clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law."**
    
- **Essentials of Valid Custom:** For a custom to be law, it must be:
    
    1. **Ancient:** Practiced for a long time (time immemorial).
        
    2. **Continuous:** Without interruption.
        
    3. **Certain:** Definite and clear.
        
    4. **Reasonable:** Not against public policy or morality.
        
    5. **Not opposed to Statute:** A custom cannot override a specific Act of Parliament.

### 2.2 Modern Sources

These sources emerged during the British Colonial period and continue to function today. They transformed Hindu Law from a religious code into a state-enforced legal system.

#### 1. Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience

- **Origin:** Introduced by the British. When a dispute arose that was not covered by any Smriti or existing Custom, the Judges were required to decide based on "Justice, Equity, and Good Conscience."
    
- **Impact:** In practice, this meant applying principles of **English Common Law** to Hindu disputes. This source modernized Hindu Law, filling gaps where the ancient texts were silent.
    

#### 2. Judicial Precedents (Case Law)

- **Concept:** The doctrine of _Stare Decisis_ (binding precedent).
    
- **Hierarchy:**
    
    - Decisions of the **Privy Council** (pre-1947) are binding on Bangladeshi courts (unless overruled).
        
    - Decisions of the **Supreme Court of Bangladesh** (Appellate Division) are binding on all lower courts.
        
- **Significance:** Most practical Hindu Law today is "Judge-made law." For example, the rules regarding a "Widow's Estate" or "Legal Necessity" for selling land are defined primarily by case law, not the Vedas.
    

#### 3. Legislation (Statutes)

- **Authority:** This is the supreme source of modern law. An Act of Parliament can override all previous sources—Sruti, Smriti, Custom, or Case Law.
    
- **In Bangladesh:** While less frequent than in India, legislation is still the ultimate authority.
    
    - _Example:_ The _Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856_ overrode the custom/text that forbade widows from marrying.
        
    - _Example:_ The _Hindu Marriage Registration Act, 2012_ introduced state rules for recording marriages.

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Custom" Doctrine:** The most vital principle for exams is the relationship between Text and Custom.
> 
> - _Formula:_ **Custom > Smriti (Written Text).**
>     
> - _Logic:_ If a text says X, but the community has validly practiced Y for centuries, the Court will enforce Y. This allows the law to be flexible.
>     

> **The "Hierarchy" of Sources:** If asked to arrange sources by binding force today:
> 
> 1. **Legislation** (Top - overrides everything).
>     
> 2. **Precedent** (Interpretations binding on lower courts).
>     
> 3. **Commentaries** (The authority in specific schools, e.g., Dayabhaga).
>     
> 4. **Smriti / Custom** (The foundational layer).
>     

> **Conflict Rule:**
> 
> - Conflict between Sruti and Smriti? $\rightarrow$ **Sruti prevails.**
>     
> - Conflict between Smriti and Commentary? $\rightarrow$ **Commentary prevails.** (Because the Commentary explains how the Smriti is to be understood in that region).
>
