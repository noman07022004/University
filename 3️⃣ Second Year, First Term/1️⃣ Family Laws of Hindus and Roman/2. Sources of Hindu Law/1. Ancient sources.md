## 2.1 Ancient Sources of Hindu Law ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the "genealogy" of the law. To understand why a rule exists in the Dayabhaga school, you often have to trace it back to how a Commentary interpreted a Smriti.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** Short notes on "Manusmriti," "Narada Smriti," or "Essentials of a Valid Custom" are very common.

---

The ancient sources of Hindu Law are the reservoirs from which the law flowed before the intervention of the British legal system. These sources are not statutory codes passed by a parliament but are a mixture of **divine revelation**, **recalled wisdom**, **scholarly interpretation**, and **community practice**.

They are hierarchically arranged: technically, the Vedas are supreme, but practically, the Commentaries and Customs determine the actual law applicable today.

---

### 1. Sruti (The Vedas)

The word _Sruti_ derives from the root _Shru_ (to hear). It signifies "that which was heard" directly by the ancient Sages (_Rishis_) from the Divine.

- **Nature:** They are considered _Apaurusheya_ (not of human origin) and are the paramount source of authority. No human law can technically contradict the Sruti.
    
- **The Four Vedas:**
    
    1. **Rig Veda:** The oldest; contains hymns praising gods.
        
    2. **Yajur Veda:** Contains rituals and sacrificial formulas.
        
    3. **Sama Veda:** The Veda of chants and melodies.
        
    4. **Atharva Veda:** Contains spells, incantations, and some references to customs.
        
- **Legal Relevance:** While they are the ultimate root of _Dharma_ (righteousness), they contain very little "positive law" (rules for inheritance, debt, etc.). They provided the _ethical foundation_ rather than the _legal code_.
    

### 2. Smriti (The Institutes/Codes)

The word _Smriti_ means "what is remembered." It is the recollection of the wisdom of the Sruti by the Sages, compiled into systematic codes. When the Sruti is silent, the Smriti speaks.

The Smritis are divided into two chronological phases:

#### A. Dharmasutras (Prose, 800–200 BC)

These were the earliest manuals, written in prose. They were brief aphorisms meant to be memorized by students.

- _Key Authors:_ Gautama, Baudhayana, Apastamba, Vasistha.
    
- _Focus:_ Primarily on the duties of students and householders, with less emphasis on civil law.
    

#### B. Dharmashastras (Verse, 200 BC – 200 AD)

These are the metrical codes (written in poetry/shlokas) that form the backbone of strict Hindu Law. The three "Great Smritikars" (Law-Givers) are:

1. **Manusmriti (Code of Manu):**
    
    - **Status:** The most ancient and supreme authority.
        
    - **Character:** Highly orthodox and rigid. It established the strict Varna (caste) system and placed Brahmins at the top.
        
    - **Content:** It deals comprehensively with the 18 titles of law (_Vyavahara_), but often mixes law with religious penance.
        
2. **Yajnavalkya Smriti:**
    
    - **Status:** The most coherent and systematic code.
        
    - **Character:** More liberal than Manu. It is shorter, clearer, and more logical.
        
    - **Crucial Link:** The **Mitakshara School** (dominant in India) is simply a commentary on this specific Smriti.
        
3. **Narada Smriti:**
    
    - **Status:** The "Jurist's Code."
        
    - **Character:** Progressive. Narada was the first to separate **Civil Law** from **Religious Precepts**.
        
    - **Content:** He focused entirely on legal procedure, pleadings, evidence, and courts, ignoring religious rituals. He allowed widow remarriage and gambling (under state control), which Manu strictly forbade.
        

### 3. Commentaries and Digests (Nibandhas)

As centuries passed, the language of the Smritis became archaic, and society evolved. To bridge the gap between the rigid ancient texts and the changing social needs, learned scholars wrote **Commentaries**.

- **The Interpretative Fiction:** These scholars claimed they were merely "interpreting" the Smritis. In reality, they modified, expanded, and even altered the laws to fit local customs.
    
- **Birth of Schools:** The difference in interpretation of the _same_ Smriti texts led to the emergence of different schools:
    
    - **Mitakshara:** Written by _Vijnaneshwara_ (Commentary on Yajnavalkya). Accepted everywhere except Bengal.
        
    - **Dayabhaga:** Written by _Jimutavahana_ (A Digest of all codes). Accepted in **Bengal (Bangladesh)**.
        
- **Legal Rule:** In case of conflict, the **Commentary overrides the Smriti** because the courts enforce the interpretation accepted by the local people.
    

### 4. Custom (Sadachara/Walat)

In Hindu Law, Custom is not just a source; it is a transcendent force. The Smritis themselves state: _"Custom is powerful; it overrides the Sacred Law."_

#### Types of Custom:

1. **Local Custom (_Desha-achara_):** A custom observed in a specific geographical region (e.g., Dayabhaga in Bengal).
    
2. **Class/Caste Custom:** Rules observed by a specific caste or trade (e.g., customs of the commercial community or Sudras).
    
3. **Family Custom (_Kula-achara_):** Usages specific to a single family (e.g., succession rules in royal families).
    

#### Essentials of a Valid Custom:

For a custom to be legally enforceable in court today, it must satisfy specific tests:

1. **Ancient:** It must be of "immemorial antiquity" (English law requires 1189 AD; in India/Bangladesh, proof of 100+ years usually suffices).
    
2. **Continuous:** It must have been practiced without interruption.
    
3. **Certain:** It must be definite, not vague.
    
4. **Reasonable:** It must not be opposed to reason or public policy.
    
5. **Not Immoral:** A custom promoting promiscuity or crime is void (e.g., temple prostitution customs were struck down).
    
6. **Not Opposed to Statute:** A custom cannot violate a clear Act of Parliament (e.g., Sati was a custom, but the _Sati Regulation Act_ abolished it).
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The Hierarchy of Authority:** Memorize this sequence for conflict resolution in Ancient Law:
> 
> 1. **Custom** (Top - if valid)
>     
> 2. **Sruti** (Theoretical Top, but practically rare)
>     
> 3. Smriti (The Text)
>     
>     However, in the modern courtroom, the hierarchy is: Statute > Custom > Precedent > Commentary > Smriti.
>     

> **Exam Question:** _"Discuss the role of Narada in the development of Hindu Law."_
> 
> - **Answer Focus:** Highlight that he was the first to **secularize** law, separating legal procedure from religious ritual. He is considered the father of strict legal pleading in Hindu jurisprudence.
>     

> **Jimutavahana:** Since you are studying in Bangladesh, **Jimutavahana** is the most important commentator for you. His work, _Dayabhaga_, is the source of the specific inheritance laws (like the widow's share) applied in Bangladeshi courts.
