### 13.3 Burden of Proof ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** In any legal dispute involving a Widow's Estate, knowing _who_ must prove the facts is often more decisive than the facts themselves.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is the "application" part of the law. Exam problems often describe a sale made 20 years ago and ask if it can be challenged. You must apply the rules of burden of proof and "bona fide inquiry" to answer.
    

---

#### I. The General Rule: Onus on the Alienee

When a Hindu widow (or limited owner) alienates property and the reversioners challenge the transaction (either during her life or after her death), the burden of proof (_Onus Probandi_) lies squarely on the **Alienee** (the purchaser, mortgagee, or creditor).

It is **not** for the reversioners to prove that there was _no_ necessity. It is for the purchaser to prove that the transaction was valid.

**Why?** Because the purchaser knows they are dealing with a person who has **limited powers** of alienation. They are expected to know the law: a widow cannot sell the corpus except for specific needs. Therefore, they proceed at their own risk.

---

#### II. What the Alienee Must Prove

To uphold the alienation and protect their title, the alienee must prove **one** of the following two things:

1. **Actual Necessity:** That there was, in fact, a legal necessity (e.g., a pending debt, a daughter's marriage) or a benefit to the estate at the time of the sale; **OR**
    
2. **Bona Fide Inquiry:** That they acted honestly and made proper and reasonable inquiries into the existence of the necessity, and were satisfied that the necessity existed.
    

> **The Principle of Protection:** If the buyer made an honest inquiry and was satisfied that the widow needed money for a valid purpose, the sale is valid **even if** it turns out later that there was no actual necessity, or if the widow misspent the money afterwards. The law does not expect the lender/buyer to supervise how the widow spends the cash.

---

#### III. The Landmark Authority

**Case Name:** **_Hunoomanpersaud Panday v. Mussumat Babooee (1856)_**

This Privy Council judgment is the _locus classicus_ (the standard authority) for this entire topic. The principles laid down here apply to alienations by all limited owners (widows, guardians, managers).

**Key Principles from the Case:**

- The power of a limited owner is limited and qualified.
    
- The burden of proof is on the lender/purchaser.
    
- The lender is bound to inquire into the necessities of the loan.
    
- If the lender acts in **good faith** and makes **sufficient inquiry**, they are not bound to see to the application of the money.
    

---

#### IV. Role of Recitals in the Deed

A "Recital" is the part of the sale deed that explains the reason for the sale (e.g., _"I, Widow X, am selling this land to pay off my husband's debt"_).

- **Evidentiary Value:** Recitals are **evidence** of the representation made to the purchaser, but they are **not conclusive proof** of the necessity itself.
    
- **Ancient Alienations:** If a sale took place a very long time ago (e.g., 50 years) and the original witnesses and parties are dead, the court treats recitals with more weight. The court may presume that the transaction was lawful because it is impossible to bring fresh evidence after such a delay.
    

---

#### V. Consent of Reversioners

If the widow alienates the property with the **consent** of the nearest reversioners (the husband's heirs who would take the property if she died at that moment):

- It raises a **strong presumption** that the alienation was for a valid legal necessity.
    
- The burden of proof shifts to the person challenging the sale to prove that there was _no_ necessity.
    
- However, the consent must be of the **whole body** of nearest reversioners, not just one of them.
    

---

#### VI. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **The "Application of Money" Rule:** A common exam trick is: _"The widow sold land to A for her daughter's marriage. A paid the money. The widow then used the money to buy jewelry for herself and did not pay for the marriage. Is the sale valid?"_
    
    - **Answer:** **Yes.** Under the rule in _Hunoomanpersaud_, if 'A' made a bona fide inquiry and paid the money in good faith, he is not responsible for how the widow actually spent it. He keeps the land.
        
- **Specific Performance:** If a widow enters into a contract to sell land but refuses to complete it, the buyer cannot sue for "Specific Performance" unless they can prove legal necessity.
    
- **Citation:** Always cite _Hunoomanpersaud Panday_ (1856) when discussing the burden of proof. It adds significant academic weight to your answer.
