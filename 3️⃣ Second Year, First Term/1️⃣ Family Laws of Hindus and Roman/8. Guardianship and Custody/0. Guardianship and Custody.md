## 8. Guardianship and Custody ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** A minor (person under 18) cannot legally manage property or enter into contracts. Therefore, understanding _who_ acts on their behalf is crucial for any property dispute involving a minor. The distinction between "guardianship" (legal authority) and "custody" (physical care) is a fundamental legal concept.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Problem questions often involve a guardian selling a minor's land to pay off debts. You must determine if the sale is valid based on the type of guardian and the doctrine of "Legal Necessity."
    

---

### Introduction

In Hindu Law, the State is considered the ultimate protector of all minors (_Parens Patriae_). However, for practical management, the law recognizes the right of guardianship. Guardianship is the right to control the **person** and **property** of a minor.

While the ancient Shastric law provided the basic framework, the law in Bangladesh is now heavily supplemented by the **Guardians and Wards Act, 1890**. This Act applies to all communities, but the personal law (Hindu Law) is still consulted to determine who has the preferential right to be appointed.

---

### 8.1 Concept of Guardianship

A "Minor" in Hindu Law (and generally in Bangladesh under the _Majority Act, 1875_) is a person who has not completed the age of **18 years**.

- **Role:** A guardian is a person who has the care of the person of the minor or of his property, or of both.
    
- **Objective:** The primary objective is the **Welfare of the Minor**. In ancient times, the father's right was absolute (_Patria Potestas_), but in modern law, the welfare of the child is the paramount consideration, overriding the rights of the father if necessary.
    

---

### 8.2 Distinction between Guardianship and Custody

Though often used interchangeably, these terms have distinct legal meanings.

- **Guardianship (Legal Authority):**
    
    - Refers to the **legal right** to make decisions for the minor (e.g., selling land, managing money, consenting to medical treatment).
        
    - It implies legal ownership/management of the minor's estate.
        
- **Custody (Physical Care):**
    
    - Refers to the actual **physical possession** and care of the minor (e.g., feeding, bathing, keeping the child in the house).
        
    - _Key Distinction:_ A person can have custody without being the legal guardian.
        
    - _Example:_ In Hindu Law, the Father is the natural **Guardian**, but if the child is very young (under 5 years), the Mother typically has the right to **Custody** (_Hizanat_ equivalent). The father retains the legal title, but the mother keeps the child.
        

---

### 8.3 Classification of Guardianship

Hindu Law classifies guardians into four specific categories based on the source of their authority.

#### A. Natural Guardians

These are persons who have a right to be guardians by the very fact of their relationship to the minor. They do not need a court order.

1. **The Father:** He is the natural guardian of the person and separate property of his minor children. His right is superior to all others.
    
2. **The Mother:** She is the natural guardian **after the father**.
    
    - _Exception:_ If the father is alive but renounces the world (Sanyasi) or is unfit, the mother can act as natural guardian.
        
3. **The Husband:** For a married minor girl, the husband is the lawful natural guardian.
    

#### B. Testamentary Guardians

- **Appointment:** A guardian appointed by a **Will**.
    
- **Who can appoint?** Only a Hindu **Father** has the power to appoint a testamentary guardian for his minor children. He can nominate someone to manage the child's property after his death.
    
- **Mother's Right:** Under traditional law (Dayabhaga), a mother **cannot** appoint a testamentary guardian, even if the father is dead (though this is debated in modern equity).
    

#### C. Guardians Appointed by the Court (Certified Guardian)

- **Authority:** Where there is no natural or testamentary guardian, or where the natural guardian is unfit, the District Court can appoint a guardian under the _Guardians and Wards Act, 1890_.
    
- **Effect:** Once a court appoints a guardian, the age of majority for that ward extends to **21 years** (instead of 18).
    

#### D. De Facto Guardians

- **Concept:** A person who has no legal title (not a parent, not appointed by court) but effectively manages the minor's estate out of love or necessity (e.g., a grandmother, uncle, or elder brother).
    
- **Legal Status:** They are "guardians in fact" but not "guardians in law." Their powers are severely restricted.
    

---

### 8.4 Powers of Different Types of Guardians

The most critical exam question is: _Can a guardian sell the minor's property?_ The answer depends on the type of guardian.

#### 1. Powers of Natural Guardian

The father (or mother) has wide powers but is not an absolute owner.

- **Power of Alienation (Sale/Mortgage):** A natural guardian can sell or mortgage the minor's immovable property **ONLY** in case of:
    
    1. **Legal Necessity** (_Apatkale_): E.g., for the minor's food, education, medical treatment, or government tax.
        
    2. **Benefit of the Estate:** E.g., selling a crumbling house to buy fertile land.
        
- **Burden of Proof:** The buyer must prove that he inquired and found that the "Legal Necessity" existed. If he fails, the minor can cancel the sale upon reaching 18.
    
- **Limitation:** The natural guardian cannot bind the minor by a personal covenant (cannot sign a contract that makes the minor personally liable for debt).
    

#### 2. Powers of Testamentary Guardian

- Their powers are defined by the **Will**.
    
- If the Will is silent, they generally have the same powers as a natural guardian.
    

#### 3. Powers of Certified Guardian (Court Appointed)

- **Strict Control:** They are public officers. They **cannot** sell or mortgage immovable property without the **previous permission of the Court**.
    
- **Effect:** Any sale made without court permission is **Voidable** at the instance of the minor.
    

#### 4. Powers of De Facto Guardian

- **Strict Restriction:** A de facto guardian has **NO power** to alienate (sell/mortgage) the minor's immovable property.
    
- **Effect:** An alienation by a de facto guardian is **Void Ab Initio** (Void from the beginning). It cannot be ratified.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Hanoomanpersaud" Case:** This is the most famous case in Hindu Law (_Hanoomanpersaud v. Mussumat Babooee_). It established the doctrine of **"Legal Necessity."**
> 
> - _Exam Tip:_ Whenever you discuss a guardian selling property, you **MUST** cite this case. It states that the power of the manager can only be exercised in a case of need or for the benefit of the estate.
>     

> **Mother's Position:** A trick question: _"Can a mother be a natural guardian if the father is alive?"_
> 
> - **Answer:** Generally **No**. The father is the primary guardian. The mother comes _after_ him. However, for custody (physical care), she is preferred for children of tender years.
>     

> **Void vs. Voidable:**
> 
> - Sale by **Natural Guardian** without necessity = **Voidable** (Valid until minor cancels it).
>     
> - Sale by **De Facto Guardian** = **Void** (Invalid legally).
