## 8.1 Concept of Guardianship ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Before diving into the powers of a guardian, you must understand _who_ requires a guardian and the legal philosophy behind it. This section defines the "Minor" and the fundamental shift from "Parental Rights" to "Child's Welfare."
    
- **Exam (⭐ 2/5):** While essential for understanding, direct exam questions on the abstract "concept" are rare. However, the definition of a "Minor" (age limits) is frequently tested in objective questions.
    

---

### Introduction

In any legal system, certain individuals are deemed incapable of managing their own affairs due to immaturity or lack of understanding. The law calls them "Minors" and provides them with a protector called a "Guardian." In Hindu Law, the concept of guardianship is based on the principle that the State is the ultimate protector (_Parens Patriae_) of all children, but it delegates this authority to parents or relatives for practical care.

Unlike the ancient Roman concept of _Patria Potestas_ (where the father had absolute, life-and-death power over the child), modern Hindu Law views guardianship not as a "right" of the parent, but as a "duty" or "trust" reposed in them for the benefit of the child.

---

### 1. Definition of a Minor

Who needs a guardian? In Hindu Law, a guardian is required only for a "Minor." The age of majority is determined not by the ancient texts (which used puberty or age 15/16 as the benchmark) but by the general secular law of Bangladesh.

- **General Rule:** Under the **Majority Act, 1875**, a person is deemed to have attained majority when they complete the age of **18 years**.
    
- **Exception (The 21-Year Rule):** If a guardian has been appointed by a Court (under the _Guardians and Wards Act, 1890_) for a minor before they turn 18, the age of majority for that specific child is extended to **21 years**.
    
- **Legal Consequence:** Until this age is reached, the person is legally incompetent to enter into contracts, sell property, or manage their estate. Any contract signed by a minor is _Void ab initio_ (void from the start).
    

### 2. The Scope of Guardianship

The concept of guardianship in Hindu Law covers two distinct domains:

1. **Guardianship of the Person:** This refers to the care, custody, upbringing, education, and health of the minor. It involves physical possession and day-to-day parenting.
    
2. **Guardianship of the Property:** This refers to the management, preservation, and dealing with the minor's estate (land, money, assets). A person might be a guardian of the person but not the property, and vice versa.
    

### 3. The Guiding Principle: "Welfare of the Minor"

The most significant conceptual shift in Hindu Law has been regarding the objective of guardianship.

- **Ancient View:** The father had a supreme, natural right to the custody of his children, almost like a property right.
    
- **Modern View:** The **Welfare of the Minor** is the paramount consideration.
    
    - If a father is unfit (e.g., immoral, abusive, or creates a hostile environment), the court can strip him of his "natural right" and appoint someone else.
        
    - The "Right of the Father" is now subordinate to the "Best Interest of the Child."

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Age Calculation:** In exam problems, pay close attention to the age.
> 
> - _Scenario:_ "X, aged 17, sells his land." $\rightarrow$ **Void**.
>     
> - _Scenario:_ "X, aged 19 (whose guardian was appointed by court at age 10), sells his land." $\rightarrow$ **Void** (because his majority age is 21).
>     

> **Statutory Overlay:** Remember that while you are studying "Hindu Law," the definitions often come from the _Majority Act, 1875_ and the _Guardians and Wards Act, 1890_. These secular acts apply to Hindus in Bangladesh.

> **Marriage vs. Majority:** Do not confuse the age of marriage (21 for boys, 18 for girls) with the age of majority (18 for all). A Hindu boy of 19 is a "Major" (can sign contracts) but cannot legally marry under the Restraint Act.
