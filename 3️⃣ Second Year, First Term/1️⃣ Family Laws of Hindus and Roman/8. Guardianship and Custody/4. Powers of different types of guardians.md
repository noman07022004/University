## 8.4 Powers of Different Types of Guardians ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the practical core of guardianship law. Merely knowing "who" is a guardian is useless unless you know "what" they can do. Specifically, understanding who can sell a minor's land and under what conditions (Legal Necessity) is vital for property law practice.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is a favorite area for problem questions. A classic scenario involves a guardian selling a minor's property to pay off debts or for a wedding. You must apply the "Legal Necessity" test and determine if the sale is Valid, Voidable, or Void.
    

---

### Introduction

The powers of a guardian are not absolute. They are in the nature of a trust held for the benefit of the minor. The law strikes a balance: it grants guardians enough power to manage the estate effectively but imposes restrictions to prevent them from squandering the minor's inheritance.

The extent of this power depends entirely on the **category of guardian**. A Natural Guardian has wider powers under the uncodified law, while a Court-Appointed Guardian is strictly regulated by the _Guardians and Wards Act, 1890_.

---

### 1. Powers of Natural Guardian (Father/Mother)

In Bangladesh (under uncodified Hindu Law), the powers of a natural guardian are governed by the principles laid down by the Privy Council in the landmark case of **_Hanoomanpersaud v. Mussumat Babooee_** (1856).

- **Power of Alienation:** A natural guardian has the power to sell or mortgage the minor's immovable property, but this power is **limited and qualified**. It can be exercised **ONLY** in two specific cases:
    
    1. **Legal Necessity (_Apatkale_):** Immediate needs for the preservation of the minor or the estate.
        
        - _Examples:_ Paying government revenue (tax) to save land from auction, paying for the minor's essential education, medical treatment, or performing mandatory religious rites (like the father's _Sraddha_).
            
    2. **Benefit of the Estate:** Transactions that are clearly profitable or protective for the property.
        
        - _Example:_ Selling a small, scattered plot of land to buy a larger, more productive piece of land nearby.
            
- **Burden of Proof:** The burden lies on the **Alienee (Buyer)** to prove that:
    
    - There was an actual legal necessity.
        
    - Or, that he made a bona fide inquiry into the necessity before buying.
        
- **Legal Effect of Unauthorized Sale:** If a natural guardian sells property _without_ legal necessity:
    
    - The transaction is **Voidable** (not Void).
        
    - It remains valid until the minor challenges it. The minor can set it aside upon attaining majority (within 3 years of turning 18).
        

### 2. Powers of Testamentary Guardian

- **Source of Power:** Their powers are defined by the **Will** that appointed them.
    
- **Extent:**
    
    - If the Will is silent, they generally exercise the same powers as a natural guardian (subject to legal necessity).
        
    - However, the Will can impose **restrictions** (e.g., "The guardian cannot sell the ancestral house"). If the guardian ignores this restriction and sells, the sale is voidable.
        

### 3. Powers of Certified Guardian (Court Appointed)

These guardians are public officers appointed under the **Guardians and Wards Act, 1890**. Their powers are strictly statutory.

- **Section 29 (Restriction on Alienation):** A certified guardian **CANNOT**, without the previous permission of the Court:
    
    1. Mortgage, charge, sell, gift, or exchange any immovable property of the minor.
        
    2. Lease any part of the property for a term exceeding 5 years (or extending more than 1 year past the minor's majority).
        
- **Legal Effect (Section 30):** Any disposal of immovable property by a certified guardian strictly in contravention of Section 29 (i.e., without court permission) is **Voidable** at the instance of any other person affected thereby (usually the minor). It is not void ab initio, but it is easily overturned.
    

### 4. Powers of De Facto Guardian

This is the area of greatest confusion and divergence between India and Bangladesh.

- **In India (1956 Act):** A de facto guardian is strictly prohibited from dealing with the minor's property. Any sale is **Void**.
    
- **In Bangladesh (Uncodified Law):** Since the 1956 Act does not apply, Bangladesh follows the old Privy Council rule from _Hanoomanpersaud’s case_.
    
    - **The Rule:** A de facto guardian (like a mother acting when the father is absent, or an uncle) has the **same power** as a natural guardian to alienate property **in case of necessity or benefit to the estate**.
        
    - **Risk:** However, courts are very cautious. While legally _possible_ under old law, modern courts in Bangladesh closely scrutinize such sales. If there is no strict necessity, the sale is treated as **Void Ab Initio** (unlike the Natural Guardian's "Voidable" sale), because a de facto guardian has no legal title to transfer ownership.

### Comparative Table: Power to Alienate Immovable Property

|**Type of Guardian**|**Authority to Sell/Mortgage**|**Condition**|**Legal Effect of Violation**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Natural**|**Yes** (Qualified Power).|Only for **Legal Necessity** or Benefit of Estate.|**Voidable** (Valid until minor cancels).|
|**Testamentary**|**Yes** (Subject to Will).|Must follow restrictions in the Will.|**Voidable**.|
|**Certified**|**No** (Generally).|Must obtain **Court Permission** (Sec 29).|**Voidable** (Sec 30).|
|**De Facto**|**Yes** (Historically in BD).|Only for urgent **Legal Necessity**.|**Void / Voidable** (Courts are hostile; usually Void).|

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Landmark Case:** You **MUST** cite **_Hanoomanpersaud v. Mussumat Babooee (1856)_**.
> 
> - It established the **"Legal Necessity"** doctrine.
>     
> - It laid down that the lender/buyer must inquire into the necessity.
>     

> **Crucial Distinction for Bangladesh:** Be careful with **De Facto Guardians**.
> 
> - If you read Indian textbooks, they say "Void."
>     
> - In **Bangladesh**, under pure Dayabhaga/Uncodified law, they _technically_ have the power under emergency/necessity (based on _Hanoomanpersaud_), but it is a weak title. In exams, it is safer to state: _"Under uncodified law, they have limited power for necessity, but modern courts view unauthorized alienation as void."_
>     

> **Problem Solving:**
> 
> - _Scenario:_ "Court-appointed guardian sells land to pay for minor's surgery but forgets to ask the Judge."
>     
> - _Result:_ The sale is **Voidable** under Section 30 of the Guardians and Wards Act, even though the "necessity" (surgery) existed. **Statutory procedure overrides necessity.**
