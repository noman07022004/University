## 9.6 Transfer of Interest ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This subtopic defines the _quantum_ (amount) of rights passed. It distinguishes between giving full ownership (Absolute Estate) and giving only a right to enjoy property for a lifetime (Life Estate). This is crucial for interpreting Wills and Gifts.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** Questions often ask: "Can a Hindu create a life estate by gift?" or "Is a gift of future property valid?" You need to know the rules of _Tagore v. Tagore_.
    

---

### Introduction

In a Gift (_Dan_), the "Transfer of Interest" refers to the passing of legal rights from the donor to the donee. The fundamental rule is that the donor must completely divest himself of the interest he intends to give. He cannot give the property and yet keep the title deeds or control in his own hands.

However, a complex legal question arises: _What kind of interest can be transferred?_ Can a Hindu give away property forever (Absolute Interest), or can he give it for a limited time (Limited Interest)?

### 1. Transfer of Absolute Interest (_Sampurna Swatwa_)

- **General Rule:** Ordinarily, when a Hindu makes a gift, the presumption is that he transfers the **Absolute Interest** (Full Ownership).
    
- **Effect:** The donee becomes the full owner. He can sell, mortgage, or destroy the property. The donor retains no rights.
    
- **Interpretation:** If a deed says "I give this land to my son," without any restrictions, the court presumes the son gets absolute ownership, heritable by his heirs.
    

### 2. Transfer of Limited Interest (Life Estate)

Can a Hindu transfer an interest that is _less_ than ownership? Yes.

- **Life Estate:** A donor can transfer property to a person (e.g., his widowed daughter) for her **life only**.
    
    - _Effect:_ She can enjoy the income and reside there, but she cannot sell the property. Upon her death, the property does not go to her heirs; it reverts to the donor or goes to a specified second person (Remainderman).
        
- **Validity:** Unlike Muslim Law (where the validity of life estates in gifts was historically debated), **Hindu Law recognizes the creation of a Life Estate**. A Hindu can legally create a "Life Interest" in favor of A, and then an "Absolute Interest" in favor of B.
    

### 3. Transfer of Future Interest (Void)

- **The Rule:** A gift must be of **existing** property.
    
- **Prohibition:** A transfer of an interest in "Future Property" is **Void Ab Initio**.
    
    - _Example:_ "I gift you the mangoes that will grow in my garden next year" or "I gift you the land I hope to inherit from my uncle."
        
- **Doctrine of _Spes Successionis_:** The "chance" of an heir succeeding to an estate cannot be transferred. It is a mere hope, not a legal interest.
    

### 4. The Rule in _Tagore v. Tagore_ (1872)

This is the most famous Privy Council judgment regarding the transfer of interest in Hindu Law.

- **The Principle:** A Hindu cannot create an estate unknown to Hindu Law.
    
- **Key Holding:** A gift to a person who is **not in existence** (unborn) at the date of the gift is **Void**.
    
    - _Modern Modification:_ Under statutory law (Section 13 of Transfer of Property Act), a transfer to an unborn person is valid _only if_ the absolute interest is transferred to them (no life estate to unborn).

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Problem Scenario:** _"A father gifts his house to his daughter for her life, and after her death to his nephew."_
> 
> - **Analysis:** This is a valid transfer of interest.
>     
>     - Daughter gets a **Life Estate**.
>         
>     - Nephew gets a **Vested Remainder** (he owns the future right now).
>         

> **Crucial Distinction:**
> 
> - **Absolute Gift:** "To X forever." (X owns it fully).
>     
> - **Limited Gift:** "To X for life." (X owns it until death).
>     
> - Hindu Law allows both.
>     

> **Spes Successionis:** Memorize this Latin term ("Hope of Succession"). A gift of _Spes Successionis_ is invalid because there is no "Interest" to transfer yet.
