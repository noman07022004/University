## 9.9 _Donatio mortis causa_ ⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This concept is a unique hybrid in jurisprudence—it sits halfway between a Gift (_Inter Vivos_) and a Will (_Testament_). Understanding it helps you grasp how the law handles emergency transfers made on a deathbed.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While less frequent than "Registration of Gift," it appears as a specific short note or an objective question. A common trick question involves the validity of a deathbed gift of _land_ (which is invalid).
    

---

### Introduction

The Latin term **_Donatio mortis causa_** translates to "Gift in contemplation of death." In Hindu Law, as applied in Bangladesh, this refers to a gift made by a person who is ill and expects to die shortly of his illness.

It is a peculiar legal transaction because it possesses the nature of both a gift and a legacy:

- Like a **Gift**, it involves the delivery of property during the lifetime of the donor.
    
- Like a **Legacy (Will)**, it is revocable and only takes full effect upon the death of the donor.
    

While ancient Hindu texts recognized deathbed gifts (_Mumurshu Dan_), the modern law governing this in Bangladesh is **Section 191 of the Succession Act, 1925** (which applies to Hindus).

---

### 1. Essential Requirements

For a _Donatio mortis causa_ to be valid, it must satisfy specific statutory conditions laid down in Section 191 of the Succession Act, 1925.

#### **A. Movable Property Only**

- **The Rule:** This type of gift is valid **ONLY for movable property** (e.g., cash, jewelry, watches, bank fixed deposit receipts).
    
- **Immovable Property:** A gift of land or a house made on a deathbed is **VOID** unless it is effected by a registered deed. One cannot transfer land simply by handing over title deeds while dying.
    

#### **B. Contemplation of Death**

- The donor must be ill and must entertain a reasonable apprehension that he will die from that illness.
    
- If a healthy person makes a gift saying "take this in case I die someday," it is not a _Donatio mortis causa_; it is an invalid attempt at a Will.
    

#### **C. Delivery of Possession**

- There must be actual or constructive delivery of the property to the donee.
    
- _Example:_ Handing over the keys to a safe containing jewelry constitutes valid constructive delivery. Mere verbal declaration ("I give you my watch") without handing it over is insufficient.
    

#### **D. Conditional on Death**

- The gift takes effect only if the donor dies of that specific illness.
    
- It is inherently **conditional**: "This is yours _if_ I die; but if I recover, I take it back."
    

---

### 2. Revocability (Termination)

Unlike a normal gift (_Inter vivos_) which is irrevocable once accepted, a _Donatio mortis causa_ is inherently fragile and revocable.

1. **Recovery of Donor:** If the donor recovers from the illness, the gift is **automatically revoked** without any act from the donor. The property reverts to him.
    
2. **Resumption:** The donor can change his mind and take the property back at any time before he dies.
    
3. **Death of Donee:** If the donee dies before the donor, the gift fails, and the property remains with the donor.
    

---

### 3. Comparison: _Donatio mortis causa_ vs. Gift _Inter vivos_

|**Feature**|**Gift Inter vivos (Normal Gift)**|**Donatio mortis causa**|
|---|---|---|
|**Timing**|Made when donor is healthy/normal.|Made when donor is dying/ill.|
|**Revocability**|Generally **Irrevocable** (except for fraud).|**Revocable** (on recovery/will).|
|**Property**|Can be Movable or Immovable.|**Movable ONLY**.|
|**Effect**|Title passes immediately and absolutely.|Title passes conditionally (subject to death).|
|**Registration**|Mandatory for Immovable.|Not required (Delivery suffices).|

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Land" Trap:** This is the most critical exam point.
> 
> - _Scenario:_ "A father, on his deathbed, hands over the deed of his house to his son saying 'Take this house'."
>     
> - _Analysis:_ This is **NOT** a valid _Donatio mortis causa_. Why? Because it is **Immovable Property**. The transfer fails. The house will go to all heirs via inheritance.
>     

> **Marz-ul-Maut:** If you study Muslim Law, you will find a similar concept called _Marz-ul-Maut_ (Deathbed Gift).
> 
> - _Distinction:_ Under Muslim Law, a deathbed gift is limited to **1/3rd** of the estate. Under Hindu Law (via Succession Act), a _Donatio mortis causa_ can cover the **whole** movable property; there is no 1/3rd limit.
>     

> **Suicide:** A gift made in contemplation of suicide is generally considered **invalid** as "suicide" is not considered a natural illness or legal cause for such a gift in older interpretations, though modern views vary. Stick to "illness" for exams.
