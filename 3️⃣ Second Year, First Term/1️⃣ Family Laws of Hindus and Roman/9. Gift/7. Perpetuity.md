## 9.7 Perpetuity ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** The "Rule Against Perpetuity" is the legal mechanism that prevents families from hoarding property forever. It ensures that land remains in commerce. Understanding the calculation (Life + 18 years) is essential for drafting Wills and Deeds.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** This is a classic "problem question" topic. You will often see a scenario like: _"A gifts property to his son for life, then to his son's son for life, then to his son's son's son..."_ You must determine where this chain becomes void.
    

---

### Introduction

The term "Perpetuity" refers to an arrangement where property is tied up in a family forever, making it inalienable (unsellable) for generations. The general policy of law is against such hoarding. The law believes in the **Free Circulation of Property** for the benefit of society and commerce.

In Hindu Law, the ancient texts did not explicitly forbid perpetuities because the concept of a "Will" was unknown. However, with the introduction of Wills, wealthy Hindus tried to create "family estates" that could never be sold. The Privy Council in _Tagore v. Tagore_ and subsequently the legislature stepped in to stop this. Today, in Bangladesh, the rule is governed by **Section 14 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882**.

---

### 1. The Rule Against Perpetuity (Section 14, TPA)

This statutory rule applies to Hindus in Bangladesh. It limits how far into the future a donor/testator can control their property.

- **The Rule:** No transfer of property can operate to create an interest which is to take effect after the **lifetime** of one or more persons living at the date of such transfer, and the **minority** of some person who shall be in existence at the expiration of that period.
    
- Maximum Period: The vesting of property cannot be delayed beyond:
    
    $$\text{Life of Living Person(s)} + \text{Minority of the Unborn Beneficiary (18 Years)}$$
    
- **Interpretation:** You can give property to a living person (Life Estate), and then to their unborn child. But the unborn child must get the property **absolutely** the moment they turn 18. You cannot say "To my son for life, then to his unborn son for life..."—the second "for life" is void because it delays absolute ownership too long.
    

### 2. Transfer to Unborn Person (Section 13, TPA)

This section works in tandem with the rule against perpetuity.

- **Ancient Hindu Law (_Tagore v. Tagore_):** Originally, a gift to a person **not in existence** (unborn) at the date of the gift was strictly **Void**. The Privy Council held that a person must exist to accept the gift.
    
- **Modern Statutory Law:** The _Transfer of Property Act_ (which governs Hindus in Bangladesh) modified this. Now, a gift to an unborn person is **Valid**, provided two conditions are met:1
    
    1. **Prior Interest:** The gift must be preceded by a "Life Interest" in favor of a living person (because you cannot give directly to an em2pty cradle).
        
    2. **Absolute Interest:** The entire remaining interest of the donor must be transferred to the unborn person. You cannot give a "Life Estate" to an unborn person. If you give to an unborn person, you must give them everything (Absolute Ownership).
        

### 3. Religious Endowments Exception

- **The Exception:** The rule against perpetuity does **not** apply to religious or charitable endowments (_Debuttar_).
    
- **Logic:** A Hindu _can_ tie up property forever if it is dedicated to a Deity (Idol) or a Temple. A "Debuttar" estate is perpetual; the property belongs to the God forever and generally cannot be sold. This is the only major exception where perpetuity is legally allowed in Hindu Law.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Tagore" Case:** You **MUST** cite **_Tagore v. Tagore (1872)_**. It laid down the fundamental rule that _"A Hindu cannot create an estate unknown to Hindu Law."_ Even though statutes have modified it, this case is the starting point for any answer on Hindu perpetuities.

> **Problem Solving Formula:**
> 
> - _Scenario:_ "Gift to A (living) for life $\rightarrow$ then to B (unborn) for life $\rightarrow$ then to C."
>     
> - _Analysis:_
>     
>     - Gift to A is **Valid**.
>         
>     - Gift to B is **Void**. (Why? Because Section 13 says you cannot give a "Life Estate" to an unborn person; it must be absolute).
>         
>     - Gift to C is **Void** (Dependent on the void gift to B).
>         

> **Minority Age:** In the calculation of perpetuity, "Minority" means **18 years** (under the _Majority Act, 1875_). It is not the ancient Hindu age of 15/16.
