## 9.5 Possession ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This subtopic resolves the confusion between "Ancient Law" (which demanded delivery of possession) and "Modern Statutes" (which demand registration). It is crucial for determining _when_ a gift becomes complete.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While less dominant than "Registration," this topic appears in problem questions involving "Movable Property" (e.g., gifting jewelry) or situations where a deed is registered but the donor refuses to vacate the house.
    

---

### Introduction

In the ancient Shastric Hindu Law, the physical delivery of possession (_Pradan_) was absolutely essential to complete a gift. A gift was considered incomplete without the transfer of control. The ancient text stated: _"Gift consists in the relinquishment of one's own right and the creation of the right of another; and the creation of another's right is completed on that other's acceptance of the gift, not otherwise."_

However, in modern Bangladesh, the _Transfer of Property Act, 1882_ (TPA) has significantly altered this requirement, particularly for immovable property.

### 1. Possession in Immovable Property (Land/Buildings)

For land and houses, the rules have shifted from "Possession" to "Registration."

- **The Statutory Rule:** Under Section 123 of the Transfer of Property Act, a gift of immovable property is effected by a **Registered Instrument**.
    
- **Is Possession Necessary?**
    
    - **Strict Legal View:** If a registered deed is executed and attested, the gift is **valid and complete**, even if actual physical possession has not yet been handed over to the donee. The title passes on registration.
        
    - **Practical View:** While registration transfers the _title_, delivery of possession is often looked at by courts as the strongest evidence of the _intention_ to give. If a donor registers a deed but stays in the house for 20 years paying taxes himself, a court might suspect the deed was a "Sham" (benami transaction).
        
    - **Conclusion:** Registration is the mandatory condition; Possession is a secondary (evidentiary) factor.
        

### 2. Possession in Movable Property (Chattels)

For movable assets (cash, jewelry, cars), possession remains a primary mode of transfer.

- **Two Modes:** As per Section 123 of TPA, a gift of movable property may be effected either by:
    
    1. **Registered Deed**, OR
        
    2. **Delivery of Possession**.
        
- **Sufficiency of Possession:** If a father hands over a diamond ring to his son with the intent to gift, the gift is valid and complete the moment the son takes it. No writing or registration is required.
    

### 3. Modes of Delivery (How to Deliver?)

Since "possession" is essential for movables, how must it be delivered?

- **Actual Delivery:** Physically handing over the object (e.g., giving a watch).
    
- **Constructive Delivery:** Doing an act that has the _effect_ of putting the property in the possession of the donee, without physical handling.
    
    - _Example:_ Handing over the **keys** of a godown (warehouse) or a car. This symbolizes the transfer of control.
        
    - _Example:_ If the donee is already in possession (e.g., a tenant), the donor simply declares "Keep it as your own."

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Hiba" Comparison (Crucial):**
> 
> - **Muslim Law:** Delivery of possession is the _Sine Qua Non_ (absolute essential) for a valid Hiba. Registration (historically) could not cure the lack of possession.
>     
> - **Hindu Law:** For land, Registration is the essential. If the deed is registered, the gift is generally valid even without immediate possession.
>     

> **Gift to Minor:** If a father gifts movable property to his minor child, who takes possession?
> 
> - **The Rule:** The legal guardian (usually the father himself) holds possession _on behalf_ of the minor. The law presumes the father's possession is now the child's possession.
>     

> **Conflict of Rules:** If an exam question asks: _"Is delivery of possession necessary for the validity of a gift of immovable property under Hindu Law?"_
> 
> - **Answer:** **No.** Under Section 123 of the Transfer of Property Act, registration is sufficient to pass title. The ancient rule requiring possession has been abrogated (overridden) by the statute.
