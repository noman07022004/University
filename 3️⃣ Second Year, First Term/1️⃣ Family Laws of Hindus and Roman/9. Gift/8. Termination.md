## 9.8 Termination (Revocation) of Gift ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** It is a fundamental principle of property law that a gift, once accepted, is final. Understanding the _rare_ exceptions (like fraud or conditional gifts) is crucial to prevent misconceptions that a donor can simply "take back" a gift if they get angry.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Problem questions often feature a scenario where a father gifts land to a son, the son mistreats him, and the father wants to cancel the gift. You must be able to cite Section 126 of the Transfer of Property Act to show why he usually _cannot_ do so.
    

---

### Introduction

The general rule in Hindu Law, reinforced by the **Transfer of Property Act, 1882**, is that a gift is **Irrevocable**. Once a valid gift is made and accepted, the donor loses all title to the property, and it becomes the absolute property of the donee. The donor cannot revoke it merely because he has changed his mind, needs the property back, or has quarreled with the donee.

However, the law provides two specific modes under **Section 126** of the Transfer of Property Act by which a gift can be revoked or suspended. Additionally, a special type of gift known as _Donatio Mortis Causa_ is inherently revocable.

---

### 1. Revocation by Mutual Agreement (Condition Subsequent)

A gift can be revoked if the donor and donee agreed to a specific condition _at the time of the gift_.

- **The Rule (Section 126, Para 1):** The donor and donee may agree that on the happening of any **specified event** which does _not_ depend on the will of the donor, the gift shall be suspended or revoked.
    
- **Essentials:**
    
    1. The condition must be agreed upon **at the time of the gift** (usually written in the Gift Deed).
        
    2. The event must be **uncertain** (it may or may not happen).
        
    3. The event must be **beyond the control** of the donor.
        
- **Example:** "I give this field to you, but if you and your descendants die before me, the field shall return to me."
    
    - _Analysis:_ This is **Valid**. If the donee dies without heirs, the donor can revoke the gift and take the land back.
        

### 2. Revocation by Rescission (Like a Contract)

Since a gift is a transfer, it requires free consent. If the consent was not free, the gift can be cancelled.

- **The Rule (Section 126, Para 2):** A gift may be revoked in any of the cases (except want of consideration) in which, if it were a contract, it might be rescinded.
    
- **Grounds:** A donor can sue to cancel a gift deed if his signature was obtained by:
    
    1. **Coercion** (Force/Threats).
        
    2. **Undue Influence** (e.g., a spiritual guru forcing a devotee to gift land).
        
    3. **Fraud** (Deception regarding the nature of the deed).
        
    4. **Misrepresentation**.
        
- **Limitation:** The donor must file the suit for cancellation within **3 years** from the date he discovers the fraud or coercion.
    

### 3. Revocation at Will (Void Condition)

- **The Prohibition:** A gift which the parties agree shall be revocable wholly or in part at the **mere will of the donor** is **Void**.
    
- **Logic:** You cannot give with one hand and take back with the other. A "gift" that can be taken back anytime is not a gift at all; it is merely a license.
    
    - _Example:_ "I give you this 1 Lakh Taka, but I reserve the right to take it back whenever I want." $\rightarrow$ This is **Void**. The transfer is invalid.
        

### 4. _Donatio Mortis Causa_ (Gift in Contemplation of Death)

This is a unique exception found in Section 191 of the **Succession Act, 1925**.

- **Definition:** A gift made by a person who is ill and expects to die shortly of his illness, delivering possession of **movable** property to another.
    
- **Revocability:** Unlike normal gifts, this is **inherently revocable**.
    
    1. **Recovery:** If the donor recovers from the illness, the gift is automatically revoked.
        
    2. **Resumption:** The donor can take it back anytime before he dies.
        
    3. **Death of Donee:** If the donee dies before the donor, the gift fails.
        

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Ungrateful Son" Scenario:** This is the most frequent exam problem.
> 
> - _Facts:_ "Father gifts house to Son. Son stops feeding Father and abuses him. Can Father revoke the gift?"
>     
> - _Answer:_ **No.**
>     
> - _Reasoning:_ "Failure of Consideration" (not paying maintenance) or "Ingratitude" are **NOT** grounds for revocation under Section 126. Unless the deed specifically contained a clause saying "If you don't feed me, I will take it back" (Revocation by Agreement), the father is helpless.
>     

> **Distinction:**
> 
> - **Will:** Always revocable until death.
>     
> - **Gift:** Generally irrevocable once accepted.
>     

> **Exception:** A gift can be revoked for **"Failure of Condition"** only if that condition was a _Condition Precedent_ (e.g., "I give you this car if you marry my daughter"). If he doesn't marry, the gift fails.
