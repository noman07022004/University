## 7.1 Rationale and Objectives of Adoption ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This section is the philosophical key to the entire concept of adoption. You cannot understand why traditional Hindu law excludes daughters from adoption or restricts a widow's power without grasping the religious necessity of a "Son" for spiritual salvation.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While rarely a full essay question on its own, it is the standard opening paragraph for any answer on adoption. It provides the "jurisprudential basis" for your legal arguments.
    

---

### Introduction

In modern secular legal systems, adoption is primarily a method to provide a home for a child and a child for a family, focusing on the welfare of the minor. However, in Hindu Law, the institution of adoption (_Dattaka_) is fundamentally different. It is not merely a civil contract or a humanitarian act; it is a **religious sacrament** rooted in the deep spiritual needs of a Hindu.

The rationale for adoption in Hindu Law is a unique blend of **Spiritual Salvation** and **Secular Continuation**.

### 1. Religious Objective (Spiritual Benefit)

The primary and most dominant objective of adoption is spiritual. In the orthodox Hindu belief system, a man's life is considered incomplete without a son.

- **The Concept of "Putra":** The Sanskrit word for son, _Putra_, is derived from two roots: _Put_ (a specific hell) and _Tra_ (to deliver or save).
    
    - **Meaning:** Ancient texts state: _"Because he saves his father from the hell called Put, he is therefore called Putra."_
        
    - **Significance:** It is believed that if a man dies without a son, his soul is condemned to this hell. A son is necessary to grant him salvation (_Moksha_).
        
- **Performance of Rites (Pinda-Dana):** The central duty of a son is to offer **Pinda** (funeral cakes/oblations) and water (_Tarpan_) to his deceased father and ancestors during the _Sraddha_ ceremony.
    
    - **The Void:** A daughter, wife, or brother cannot perform these specific rites with the same spiritual efficacy as a son.
        
    - **The Solution:** If a man has no natural-born son (_Aurasa_), he adopts a son (_Dattaka_) to perform these rites. The adopted son becomes a spiritual substitute, ensuring the father's journey in the afterlife is smooth.
        

### 2. Secular Objective (Temporal Benefit)

While the religious motive is paramount in theory, the secular or worldly reasons are equally practical and legally recognized.

- **Perpetuation of Lineage (_Vangsha_):** Every Hindu desires that his family name and lineage continue. Adoption ensures that the family line does not become extinct (_Nirvangsha_) upon his death.
    
- **Heir to Property:** If a man dies without a son, his property might pass to distant agnates (reversioners) or, historically, escheat to the Crown. Adoption provides an immediate heir who preserves the family estate.
    
- **Old Age Support:** A secular objective is to secure a person who will support the parents in their old age and protect the family property.
    

### 3. The Balance: Validity of Motive

Does the motive matter for the legality of the adoption?

- **Privy Council View:** The Courts have held that while the spiritual motive is the theoretical foundation, the **validity** of an adoption does not depend on the adopter's inner piety.
    
    - _Example:_ Even if a widow adopts a son purely out of spite to defeat the inheritance of her husband's brothers (a secular/negative motive), the adoption is still **valid** provided the ceremonies are performed correctly. The law looks at the _act_, not the _intent_.
        

### 4. Why Daughters were Excluded?

Understanding the rationale explains the gender bias in traditional law.

- Since the primary object was **delivering the father from hell** via _Pinda_ offerings, and since women were historically barred from performing these specific funeral rites, there was **no religious need** to adopt a daughter.
    
- Consequently, under the uncodified law applicable in Bangladesh, the adoption of a daughter is generally **invalid** (unless a specific custom allows it), because she cannot fulfill the primary spiritual objective of the institution.

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Key Term:** Memorize the definition of **Putra** (_Put_ + _Tra_). Quoting this in an exam answer regarding adoption immediately establishes your academic depth.

> **Legal Maxim:** The Privy Council in _Amarendra Mansingh's Case_ emphasized that the "validity of adoption is to be determined by the spiritual benefit to the deceased husband." This is why a widow in Bangladesh needs her husband's permission—she acts as his agent for _his_ spiritual benefit, not her own.
