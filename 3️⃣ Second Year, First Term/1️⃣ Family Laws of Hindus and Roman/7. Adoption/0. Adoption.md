## 7. Adoption ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** Adoption is one of the most distinctive features of Hindu Law, directly linking the secular law of property with the religious law of salvation. Understanding the concept of "spiritual benefit" is essential to grasp why only sons are adopted and why widows have restricted powers.
>- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** A frequent topic for both essays and problem questions. You are often asked to determine the validity of an adoption (e.g., "Can a widow adopt without her husband's permission?") or the rights of an adopted son against a natural-born son.

---

### Introduction

Adoption (_Dattaka_) in Hindu Law is not merely a secular legal arrangement for taking care of a child; it is primarily a religious institution. The ancient definition describes it as the "transplantation of a son from the family in which he is born to another family where he is given by the natural parents."

The unique aspect of Hindu adoption in Bangladesh (under the uncodified law) is that it is almost exclusively focused on the **son**. Since a daughter cannot perform funeral rites (_Sraddha_) or offer oblations (_Pinda_), the traditional law generally does not recognize the adoption of a daughter.

---

### 7.1 Rationale and Objectives

The objective of adoption is twofold: religious and secular.

#### A. Religious Object (Primary)

- **Deliverance from Hell:** The Sanskrit word for son is _Putra_, which means "one who delivers the ancestors from the hell named _Put_."
    
- **Offering Pinda:** The primary reason a Hindu adopts a son is to ensure there is someone to offer funeral cakes (_Pinda_) and water (_Tarpan_) to his ancestors, ensuring their spiritual salvation. Without a son, a Hindu believes his spiritual journey after death is obstructed.
    

#### B. Secular Object (Secondary)

- **Heir to Property:** To secure an heir to perpetuate the family name (_Vangsha_) and inherit the property so that it does not pass to distant relatives or the government.
    
- **Old Age Support:** To have someone to look after the parents in their old age.
    

---

### 7.2 Categories of Adoption

Historically, ancient texts listed 12 types of sons (some legitimate, some adopted, some illegitimate). In modern times (and in Bangladesh), only two forms of adoption are legally recognized:

1. **Dattaka (The Given Son):**
    
    - This is the standard form of adoption prevalent all over Bangladesh.
        
    - It involves the complete severance of the boy from his natural family and his full absorption into the adoptive family.
        
2. **Kritrima (The Made Son):**
    
    - This is a rare form, now practically obsolete except in the Mithila region (and occasionally among specific communities).
        
    - Here, the consent of the adoptee (the boy) is the main factor. He does not lose his rights in his natural family but gains rights in the adoptive family.
        
    - _Note:_ Unless specified otherwise, "Adoption" in exams always refers to the **Dattaka** form.
        

---

### 7.3 Requirements for a Valid Adoption

For an adoption to be valid under the uncodified law of Bangladesh, strict requirements regarding the capacity of the parties and the eligibility of the child must be met.

#### A. Capacity to Adopt (Who can take?)

- **Male Hindu:** A male Hindu of sound mind who has attained the age of discretion (usually 15+, though majority is safer) can adopt. He does not need his wife's consent.
    
    - _Restriction:_ He must not have a son, grandson, or great-grandson (legitimate or adopted) living at the time.
        
- **Female Hindu (The Dayabhaga Restriction):**
    
    - **Unmarried Woman/Spinster:** Cannot adopt.
        
    - **Wife:** Cannot adopt while her husband is alive.
        
    - **Widow:** A widow can adopt **only if** she has the express authority/permission (_Anumati-patra_) from her husband given before his death. In Bangladesh, a widow cannot adopt on her own volition without this authority.
        

#### B. Capacity to Give (Who can give?)

- Only the **father** or the **mother** can give a boy in adoption.
    
- The father has the primary right. The mother can give only with the father's consent (unless he is dead or has renounced the world).
    
- No other person (like a brother, uncle, or orphanage) can give a child in adoption.
    

#### C. Who can be Adopted? (The Subject)

- **Gender:** Must be a **male**. (Adoption of a daughter is not recognized in traditional Dayabhaga law).
    
- **Caste:** Must belong to the same caste as the adoptive father.
    
- **Age:** Traditionally, the boy must be adopted before his _Upanayana_ (sacred thread ceremony). However, amongst Sudras, even a married man can theoretically be adopted if custom allows.
    
- **Relationship:** He must not be a boy whose mother the adoptive father could not have legally married while she was a maiden (e.g., sister's son or daughter's son). _Note:_ This rule is often relaxed by custom for Sudras.
    

---

### 7.4 Ceremony

Adoption is a legal transaction that requires visible acts to prove the transfer of the child.

1. **Physical Giving and Taking:** This is the most essential requirement. The natural parent must physically hand over the child to the adoptive parent with the declaration of giving, and the adoptive parent must receive the child with the intention of taking him as a son.
    
    - _Legal Rule:_ Without this actual delivery, even a registered deed of adoption is invalid.
        
2. **Datta Homam:** This is a religious sacrifice (burning of clarified butter/ghee).
    
    - **For Twice-Born Castes:** It is generally considered necessary for Brahmins, Kshatriyas, and Vaishyas to validate the adoption.
        
    - **For Sudras:** This ceremony is **not required**. Mere giving and taking suffices.
        

---

### 7.5 Consequences of Adoption

Once a valid adoption takes place, it creates a permanent change in status.

#### A. Severance from Natural Family

- The adopted son is completely severed from his natural family.
    
- He loses all rights of inheritance in his natural family (he cannot inherit from his natural father).
    
- _Exception:_ He retains the prohibition against marrying within his natural family's prohibited degrees (Sapinda relationship). He cannot marry his natural sister.
    

#### B. Admission to Adoptive Family

- He becomes a member of the adoptive family for all intents and purposes.
    
- **Inheritance:** He inherits from his adoptive father as if he were a natural-born son.
    
- **Coparcenary:** In a Mitakshara family, he acquires a right by birth in the joint family property from the date of adoption.
    
- **Guardianship:** The adoptive father becomes his legal guardian.
    

#### C. Rights of Adopted Son vs. After-born Son

- If a natural son (_Aurasa_) is born _after_ the adoption, the adopted son’s share is reduced.
    
- **Dayabhaga Rule:** The adopted son gets **1/3rd** of the share of the natural son.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Widow's Authority" Trap:** This is the most critical point for Bangladesh law.
> 
> - In India (after 1956), a widow can adopt freely.
>     
> - In **Bangladesh**, a widow **CANNOT** adopt without her deceased husband's distinct permission (_Anumati_).
>     
> - _Exam Scenario:_ "W, a widow in Dhaka, adopts a son to save her husband's soul. The husband left no will or permission." $\rightarrow$ **The adoption is VOID.**
>     

> **Daughter Adoption:** Remember, under the uncodified law of Bangladesh, adopting a **girl** is generally invalid. If a problem mentions "Adoption of a daughter," flag it as legally void unless a very specific local custom is proved.

> **Doctrine of Relation Back:** A complex but high-scoring concept. If a widow adopts a son 5 years after her husband's death, the adoption "relates back" to the date of the husband's death. The child is deemed to be the son of the husband from the moment he died.
