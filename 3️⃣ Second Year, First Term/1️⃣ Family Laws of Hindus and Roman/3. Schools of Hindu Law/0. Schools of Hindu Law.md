## 3. Schools of Hindu Law ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the most critical conceptual framework for Hindu property and inheritance law. You cannot understand how a Hindu family owns property or how a widow inherits without knowing which "School" applies. The difference between "Joint Family" in India and Bangladesh rests entirely on this topic.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** The question "Distinguish between Mitakshara and Dayabhaga Schools" is one of the most frequently asked questions in law examinations. It is fundamental.


---

A "School of Law" in the Hindu context refers to a specific body of legal opinion and interpretation accepted by a community. Interestingly, the ancient texts (Smritis) like _Manusmriti_ or _Yajnavalkya Smriti_ are the same for all Hindus. However, the **interpretation** of these texts differs.

These differences arose because different Commentators (scholars) in different regions of India interpreted the same texts differently to suit local customs. Over time, these interpretations solidified into distinct "Schools."

The two major schools are:

1. **The Mitakshara School** (Applies to most of India).
    
2. **The Dayabhaga School** (Applies strictly to Bengal/Bangladesh).
    

---

### 3.1 Origins and Rationality

- **Origin:** The schools originated from the _Commentaries_ (Nibandhas) written during the Post-Smriti period (approx. 11th–12th century AD).
    
- **Rationality:** The divergence was not accidental; it was sociological.
    
    - The **Mitakshara** (written by Vijnaneshwara) favored the ancient, orthodox patriarchal system where the family group was more important than the individual.
        
    - The **Dayabhaga** (written by Jimutavahana) was more "modern" and reformist. It emerged later and favored individual ownership and the rights of widows, reflecting the liberal customs of Bengal.
        

---

### 3.2 Mitakshara and Dayabhaga Schools (Detailed Comparison)

This is the core of the topic. The differences are primarily in the law of **Inheritance** and **Joint Family Property**.

#### A. The Mitakshara School

- **Author:** Vijnaneshwara.
    
- **Base Text:** It is a running commentary on the _Yajnavalkya Smriti_.
    
- **Region:** It applies to the whole of India _except_ Bengal (and formerly Assam).
    
- **Key Doctrine: Right by Birth (_Janmasvatvavada_)**.
    
    - In this school, a son acquires a right in the ancestral property the moment he is born.
        
    - The father cannot sell the ancestral property without the consent of the son (because the son is a co-owner by birth).
        
- **Mode of Devolution:** Survivorship.
    
    - When a male member of a Joint Family dies, his share does _not_ pass to his heirs (like his widow or daughter). Instead, it "survives" to the remaining male members of the family.
        

#### B. The Dayabhaga School

- **Author:** Jimutavahana.
    
- **Base Text:** It is a digest of all codes (Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada, etc.).
    
- **Region:** It applies to **Bangladesh** (Bengal) and Assam.
    
- **Key Doctrine: Right by Death (_Uparamasvatvavada_)**.
    
    - A son has **no right** to the ancestral property as long as the father is alive. The father is the absolute owner.
        
    - The son's right arises _only_ upon the death of the father.
        
- **Mode of Devolution:** Succession/Inheritance.
    
    - Property passes by inheritance to heirs (including widows and daughters in certain cases), not by survivorship.
        

#### Comparative Table: Mitakshara vs. Dayabhaga

|**Feature**|**Mitakshara School (India)**|**Dayabhaga School (Bangladesh)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Right to Property**|**By Birth:** Son becomes co-owner immediately upon birth.|**By Death:** Son gets right only after father's death.|
|**Father's Power**|Restricted. He cannot alienate (sell) ancestral property without son's consent.|Absolute. He can sell or gift property as he pleases while alive.|
|**Joint Family**|Coparcenary exists between Father and Son.|No Coparcenary between Father and Son (starts after Father's death).|
|**Widow's Right**|Historically denied share in joint property (until 1937 Act).|Historically recognized right to inherit husband's share.|
|**Partition**|Son can demand partition (division) from the father.|Son **cannot** demand partition while father is alive.|
|**Calculating Share**|Share is fluctuating (decreases if a baby is born).|Share is fixed (fractional share) upon death of owner.|

### 3.3 Migration and Application of Personal Laws

Hindu Law is _Lex Personalis_ (Law of the Person), not _Lex Loci_ (Law of the Place). This leads to a unique legal situation called "Migration."

- **The Rule:** If a Hindu family migrates from one province to another (e.g., from a Mitakshara region in India to Dayabhaga region in Bangladesh), they carry their personal law with them.
    
    - _Example:_ If a family from Bihar (Mitakshara) moved to Dhaka 100 years ago, generally, they are still governed by Mitakshara law in matters of succession, unless they have adopted the local law.
        
- **Presumption:** The presumption is that the migrating family retains their original law. The burden of proof lies on the person who claims that the family has abandoned their original law and adopted the local law.
    

### 3.4 Changing of School

Is it possible for a Hindu to change their School of Law? Yes, but it is difficult.

- **By Custom:** A family can change its school by adopting the customs and usages of the new region over a long period.
    
- **Intention:** There must be a clear intention to abandon the old law and adopt the new one. Mere language or dress change is not enough; one must adopt the religious rites and legal customs of the new school.
    
- **Inter-School Marriage:** If a Mitakshara man marries a Dayabhaga woman, the wife generally adopts the husband's school.
    

### 3.5 Applicability in Bangladesh

This is the practical conclusion for your course.

- **General Rule:** The **Dayabhaga School** is the prevailing(প্রচলিত) law in Bangladesh.
    
- **Implication:** When solving problem questions in exams regarding Bangladeshi Hindus:
    
    - Assume the father has absolute power over property.
        
    - Assume sons cannot demand partition while the father is alive.
        
    - Assume the "Right by Birth" doctrine does not apply.
        
- **Exceptions:** The Mitakshara school applies in Bangladesh _only_ to families who migrated from Mitakshara regions (like Bihar or UP) and have retained their original laws (often found in tea garden communities or specific business communities).
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Coparcener" Trap:** Be very careful with the word "Coparcener" (joint owner).
> 
> - In **Mitakshara**, a son is a coparcener with his father.
>     
> - In **Dayabhaga**, a son is **NEVER** a coparcener with his father. He only becomes a coparcener with his _brothers_ after the father dies.
>     

> **Exam Shortcut:** If a question asks _"Can X's son stop X from selling the ancestral land?"_
> 
> - If **Mitakshara**: Yes, because the son is a co-owner by birth.
>     
> - If **Dayabhaga (Bangladesh)**: No, because the father is the absolute owner until death.
>     

> **Jimutavahana:** Remember this name. He is the author of Dayabhaga. Citing him adds authority to your answer regarding Bangladeshi law.
