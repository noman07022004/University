## 3.4 Changing of School ⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This concept clarifies the flexibility of Hindu Law. It corrects the misconception that a person is bound to their school by birth forever. It explains the mechanism of assimilation.
>- **Exam (⭐ 2/5):** While important for understanding, this specific subtopic is rarely a full essay question. It usually appears as a minor component of a problem question regarding migration.


---

Can a Hindu change the School of Law that governs them? The answer is **yes**, but it is not a simple administrative process. Since Hindu Law is "Personal Law" and not "Territorial Law," simply moving to a new city does not change the law applicable to a person. A Bengali moving to Delhi remains governed by Dayabhaga law unless they take specific steps to change it.

The "Changing of School" refers to the legal process by which a Hindu family, having migrated to a new province, abandons their original law (e.g., Mitakshara) and adopts the law of the new province (e.g., Dayabhaga).

---

### 1. The Requirement: Migration + Adoption

A change of school cannot happen in a vacuum. It requires two concurrent conditions:

1. **Migration:** The family must have migrated from a region governed by one school to a region governed by another. A person cannot simply "choose" a different school while staying in their ancestral home.
    
2. **Adoption of Local Custom:** The family must demonstrate a clear intention to abandon their old law and adopt the usages, customs, and law of the new place.
    

### 2. The Test of Intention

The courts look for **evidence of assimilation**. The mere passage of time is not enough. Even if a family has lived in the new province for centuries, they might still be governed by their old law.

- **Conduct is Key:** The change is proved by the family's conduct in religious and social matters.
    
- **Indicators of Change:**
    
    - Performing marriage ceremonies according to local rites.
        
    - Following local funeral customs (Sraddha).
        
    - Observing local festivals instead of ancestral ones.
        
    - Intermarrying with local families (this is strong evidence).
        
- **Insufficient Proof:** Merely learning the local language, wearing local clothes, or owning property in the new place is **not** sufficient proof of a change of school. These are seen as external adaptations, not internal legal changes.
    

### 3. Burden of Proof

In a legal dispute, the presumption is always in favor of **continuity**.

- **Presumption:** The court presumes that a migrating family has retained their original law.
    
- **Onus:** The burden of proof lies heavily on the person who claims that the family has _changed_ their school. They must bring concrete evidence of the adoption of local customs.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Sanyasi" Exception:** A person can also effectively "change" their status by renouncing the world and entering a religious order (becoming a Sanyasi), which cuts off their ties to the material family and its laws entirely.

> **Problem Solving Strategy:** In an exam problem, if the facts say: _"Mr. X, originally from Bihar, has lived in Dhaka for 50 years, speaks Bengali perfectly, and owns a shop there,"_ do **not** conclude he is governed by Dayabhaga law.
> 
> - **Reasoning:** Language and residence are not enough. Without facts about "adopting religious rites," the presumption of Mitakshara law remains.
