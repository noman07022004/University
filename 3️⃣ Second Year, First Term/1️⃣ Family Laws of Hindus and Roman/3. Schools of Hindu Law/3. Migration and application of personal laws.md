## 3.3 Migration and Application of Personal Laws ⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This concept is vital to understand the nature of Hindu Law as a "Personal Law." It explains why different Hindu families living next to each other in the same city might be governed by completely different rules of inheritance.
>- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While less frequent as a full essay, this is a standard problem question component. You are often given a scenario where a family moves from one province to another, and you must determine which law applies.

---

One of the most distinctive features of Hindu Law is that it is **Personal Law** (_Lex Personalis_), not **Territorial Law** (_Lex Loci_). In modern legal systems (like the Penal Code), the law applies to everyone within a specific territory. However, in Hindu Law, the rules attach to the person based on their religious and caste identity, regardless of where they physically reside.

This principle leads to the legal doctrine of **Migration**: When a Hindu family migrates from one part of the country (governed by one School) to another part (governed by a different School), they carry their original personal law with them.

---

### 1. The General Rule: Law Follows the Person

The fundamental presumption in Hindu Law is that a migrating family continues to be governed by the law of their original domicile.

- **Presumption of Continuity:** If a Hindu family migrates from a region governed by the **Mitakshara School** (e.g., Bihar or Uttar Pradesh) to a region governed by the **Dayabhaga School** (e.g., Bangladesh/Bengal), the presumption is that they have brought their Mitakshara law with them.
    
- **Result:** Even if they live in Bangladesh for generations, their inheritance and joint family matters will still be decided under **Mitakshara principles** (e.g., Survivorship, Right by Birth), not the local Dayabhaga laws, unless they have expressly changed it.
    

### 2. Changing the School of Law

Is it possible for a migrating family to switch to the local law? Yes, but it requires more than just living there.

- **Renunciation and Adoption:** To change their school, the family must demonstrate a clear intention to:
    
    1. **Renounce** their original law (former customs).
        
    2. **Adopt** the law and usages of the new territory.
        
- **Evidence of Change:** This is usually proved by their conduct.
    
    - _Example:_ Adopting the local ceremonies for marriage, performing funeral rites according to the local custom, or following the local rules of succession for a long period.
        
    - Mere language change (speaking Bengali instead of Hindi) or wearing local dress is **not** sufficient proof of changing the personal law.
        

### 3. Burden of Proof

In a legal dispute, who has to prove which law applies?

- **Initial Burden:** The person asserting that a family is governed by a foreign law (e.g., "We live in Dhaka, but we follow Mitakshara law because our ancestors came from Bihar") must prove the migration.
    
- **Shifting Burden:** Once migration is proved, the burden shifts to the person claiming that the family has _adopted_ the local law. The court presumes the old law applies until proven otherwise.
    
    - _Maxim:_ "A person cannot be despoiled of his personal law by the mere fact of his movement from one place to another."
        

### 4. Applicability in Bangladesh

This doctrine is practically relevant in Bangladesh today.

- **Non-Bengali Hindus:** There are communities in Bangladesh (often referred to as "Upcountry Hindus" or specific trading communities like Marwaris) whose ancestors migrated from Northern India.
    
- **Legal Status:** Unless they have fully assimilated and adopted Dayabhaga customs, their property disputes are still settled under **Mitakshara Law** by Bangladeshi courts. This creates a dual system of Hindu Law operating within the same country.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Problem Question Tip:** If an exam scenario states: _"A Hindu family migrated from Mithila to Dhaka in 1905..."_
> 
> - **Immediate Reflex:** Do not apply Dayabhaga law blindly.
>     
> - **Analysis:** State that they are presumed to be governed by **Mitakshara (Mithila School)** unless the facts mention they adopted Dayabhaga customs.
>     

> **Key Case Law Principle:** The Privy Council (in _Soorendranath v. Heeramonee_) laid down the rule: _"Migration leads to a change of domicile, but not necessarily a change of personal law."_

> **Exception - Marriage:** Note that if a Mitakshara man marries a Dayabhaga woman, the wife generally adopts the husband's domicile and school.
