## 3.1 Origins and Rationality ⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This subtopic is the intellectual foundation of Hindu Law. Understanding the _reasoning_ (rationality) behind the split is essential to grasp why the Dayabhaga school (Bangladesh) focuses on "Spiritual Benefit" while the Mitakshara school (India) focuses on "Blood Relationship."
>- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While rarely asked as a standalone long question, it is frequently needed for the introductory part of essays comparing the two schools or explaining the nature of Hindu Law.


---

The emergence of different Schools of Hindu Law is a unique phenomenon in legal history. It is important to understand that these schools did not arise from different statutes or different holy texts. All Hindus, regardless of their location, accept the same ancient Smritis (like _Manusmriti_ and _Yajnavalkya Smriti_) as authoritative.

The **Schools** originated purely from the **different interpretations** of these same texts by learned scholars (Commentators) in different parts of the subcontinent. When British courts administered the law, they recognized that the "Law of the Zone" (the interpretation accepted by the local people) was more authoritative than the literal text of the Smriti.

### 1. Origins: The Era of Commentaries (Nibandhas)

The origin of the schools lies in the **Post-Smriti Period** (roughly 11th to 18th Century AD).

- **The Problem of Obsolescence:** As centuries passed, the ancient Smritis (written in Sanskrit verse) became difficult to understand. Furthermore, social customs had evolved, and the rigid rules of the Smritis no longer fit the changing society.
    
- **The Solution:** Learned scholars wrote **Commentaries (Nibandhas)** and Digests.
    
    - They claimed to be merely explaining the meaning of the Smritis.
        
    - In reality, they expanded, modified, and altered the laws to harmonize them with local usages and customs.
        
- **The Split:**
    
    - **Vijnaneshwara (11th Century):** Wrote the _Mitakshara_ commentary on the _Yajnavalkya Smriti_. His interpretation was accepted all over India.
        
    - **Jimutavahana (12th Century):** Wrote the _Dayabhaga_ digest. He challenged Vijnaneshwara’s interpretations. His views were accepted specifically in **Bengal (now Bangladesh)**.
        
- **Legal Recognition:** The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council definitively established that the "Commentaries" overrode the "Smritis" because they represented the living law of the people. This judicial recognition formally cemented the existence of the two schools.
    

### 2. Rationality: The Conflict of Principles

The fundamental difference between the two schools lies in the **Rationality (Logic)** they used to determine inheritance and property rights. They asked two different questions: _"Who should inherit?"_

#### A. The Mitakshara Rationality: Propinquity (Blood Relationship)

- **The Logic:** Vijnaneshwara argued that one’s right to inherit arises from **Consanguinity** (being of the same blood).
    
- **Principle of Propinquity:** The person who is nearest in blood relationship to the deceased should inherit the property.
    
- **Nature:** This is a **Secular** principle. It focuses on biological closeness.
    
- **Result:** This led to the concept of "Joint Family" and "Coparcenary by Birth," where male members hold property jointly because they share the same bloodline.
    

#### B. The Dayabhaga Rationality: Religious Efficacy (Spiritual Benefit)

- **The Logic:** Jimutavahana argued that inheritance is not for the benefit of the living, but for the spiritual benefit of the deceased soul.
    
- **Principle of Pinda-Dana:** The person who has the right to offer the _Pinda_ (funeral cake/oblations) to the deceased ancestors for their spiritual salvation is the one who should inherit the property.
    
- **Nature:** This is a **Religious** principle.
    
- **Result:** This seemingly orthodox reasoning actually led to more modern/liberal results:
    
    - It destroyed the "Joint Family" ownership (Coparcenary) between father and son, giving the father absolute ownership (because the father offers Pindas to his ancestors, the son does nothing while the father is alive).
        
    - It allowed widows to inherit (because a chaste widow performs religious acts for her husband's soul), whereas Mitakshara originally excluded them.
        

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Key Concept:** **"Propinquity vs. Religious Efficacy"**. Memorize this distinction.
> 
> - **Mitakshara** = Propinquity (Blood).
>     
> - **Dayabhaga** = Religious Efficacy (Pinda/Spiritual).
>     

> **Irony of History:** Paradoxically, the **Dayabhaga school** (based on religious rituals) produced a more **modern law** of individual ownership (similar to Western capitalism), while the **Mitakshara school** (based on secular blood ties) preserved the ancient, rigid **communal family system**.

> **Exam Terminology:** When writing about origins, use the term **"Nibandhas"** (Digests). It shows you have read the academic texts.
