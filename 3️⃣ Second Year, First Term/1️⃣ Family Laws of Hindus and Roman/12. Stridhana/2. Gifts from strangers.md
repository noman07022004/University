## 12.2 Gifts from Strangers (_Non-Saudayika_) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This category highlights the patriarchal limits of traditional Hindu Law. Unlike gifts from her own family, gifts from "strangers" (outsiders) or her own earnings are **not** fully hers during marriage. Understanding this "Husband's Veto" is essential for advising working Hindu women in Bangladesh.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** A frequent problem scenario: _"A Hindu wife earns a salary as a teacher. Can she gift her savings to her sister without her husband's consent?"_ (Answer: No, under uncodified Dayabhaga law, earnings are Non-Saudayika Stridhana and subject to his control).
    

---

### Introduction

While _Saudayika_ (gifts from relations) is the absolute property of a woman, the law treats property acquired from other sources differently. Gifts received from non-relatives (strangers) or property acquired by her own skill and labor are technically classified as **Stridhana**, but with a major "catch" regarding her power to dispose of them.

This category is often referred to as **Non-Saudayika Stridhana**.

### 1. What Constitutes "Gifts from Strangers"?

This category includes two main types of property:

1. **Gifts from Non-Relations:** Any gift received from persons other than her parents, husband, or affectionate kin.
    
    - _Example:_ A wedding gift from a neighbor or a friend.
        
2. **Property Acquired by Mechanical Arts/Labor:** Wealth earned by the woman through her own exertion.
    
    - _Examples:_ Salary from a job, income from sewing/tailoring, or earnings from singing/dancing.
        
    - _Note:_ In modern times, a woman's **salary** falls under this category.
        

### 2. Legal Status: The "During Marriage" Restriction

The extent of a woman's power over this property depends entirely on her marital status at the time.

#### **A. During Maidenhood (Unmarried)**

- If a girl receives a gift from a stranger or earns money _before_ marriage, it is her absolute property. She can spend or give it away as she pleases.
    

#### **B. During Widowhood**

- If she acquires such property _after_ her husband's death, or if she still holds it after he dies, she acquires **absolute power** of disposal. She becomes the full owner.
    

#### **C. During Coverture (Married Life) – The Dayabhaga Rule**

This is the critical rule for Bangladesh.

- **Husband's Control:** Under the Dayabhaga school, although this property is technically "Stridhana" (it goes to her heirs, not his), the woman **does not have independent power of alienation** during her husband's lifetime.
    
- **The Veto:** She cannot gift, sell, or will away this property without her **husband's consent**.
    
- **Husband's Rights:** The husband has the right to use this property if he wishes. However, it does _not_ become his property; it remains hers, but "under his shadow."
    
    - _Why?_ Ancient texts believed that a wife acts under the protection of her husband, and wealth from "outsiders" or independent earnings might lead to independence that threatened the joint family structure.
        

### 3. Comparison: Saudayika vs. Non-Saudayika

|**Feature**|**Saudayika (Gifts from Kin)**|**Non-Saudayika (Strangers/Earnings)**|
|---|---|---|
|**Source**|Parents, Husband, In-laws.|Friends, Strangers, Own Salary.|
|**Ownership**|Absolute.|Qualified during marriage.|
|**Alienation**|Wife can sell/gift **without** consent.|Wife needs **Husband's Consent** to sell.|
|**Husband's Power**|Only in "Extreme Distress".|General control during his life.|
|**Succession**|Goes to her own heirs.|Goes to her own heirs.|

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Working Woman" Problem:** This is a very common modern exam scenario.
> 
> - _Facts:_ "W, a Hindu wife in Dhaka, buys a plot of land with her own salary. She wants to sell it to pay for her brother's treatment. H objects."
>     
> - _Verdict:_ Under strict uncodified Hindu Law, **she cannot sell it** without H's consent. Her earnings are _Non-Saudayika_.
>     
> - _Critique:_ You can add a note that this ancient rule conflicts with modern constitutional principles, but until codified (like in India), the Dayabhaga rule stands in family courts.
>     

> **Succession remains the same:** Even though her power to _sell_ is restricted, the _line of inheritance_ is the same. If she dies, her salary savings go to her children/heirs, not to her husband's brothers. It is still Stridhana, just restricted Stridhana.
