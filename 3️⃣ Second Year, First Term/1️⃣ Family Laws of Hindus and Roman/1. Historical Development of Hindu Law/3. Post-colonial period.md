## 1.3 Post-Colonial Period (1947 – 1971) ⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** This period explains the "Great Divergence." It answers the fundamental question: _"Why is Hindu Law in Bangladesh different from Hindu Law in India?"_
>- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While direct questions on this specific era are rare, this knowledge is mandatory for any comparative question or when explaining the nature of current law in Bangladesh.

---

The Partition of India in 1947 marked a decisive fork in the road for Hindu Law. While the British era consolidated the law, the post-colonial era saw it split into two completely different systems based on the political geography of the new nations: **India** and **Pakistan** (which included East Pakistan, now Bangladesh).

---
### 1. Developments in India (The Path of Codification)

Immediately after independence, the Indian government, led by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Law Minister Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, pushed for drastic social reforms. They viewed the traditional Smriti-based law as archaic and discriminatory, especially against women.

- **The Hindu Code Bill:** A massive legislative project was launched to codify and modernize the entire system of Hindu Law.
    
- **The "Big Four" Acts:** Between 1955 and 1956, the Indian Parliament passed four major statutes that revolutionized the law there:
    
    1. _The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955_ (Introduced divorce, monogamy).
        
    2. _The Hindu Succession Act, 1956_ (Gave daughters equal rights, abolished limited estates).
        
    3. _The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956_.
        
    4. _The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956_.
        

### 2. Developments in Pakistan / East Pakistan (The Path of Stagnation)

In contrast, the legal trajectory in Pakistan (and specifically East Pakistan) was one of **non-interference** and **stagnation**.

- **State Policy:** As an Islamic Republic, the state of Pakistan adopted a policy of not interfering with the personal laws of religious minorities unless the community itself demanded change.
    
- **Conservative Leadership:** The leadership of the Hindu community in East Pakistan was largely conservative. They feared that any attempt by the state to "reform" their religion would undermine their distinct cultural identity.
    
- **Result - The "Time Capsule":** Consequently, the reforms that swept through India (divorce, equal inheritance) were **never adopted** in East Pakistan. The law effectively "froze" in 1947.
    
    - The law applicable in East Pakistan continued to be the **traditional Shastric law** as interpreted by the British courts (Anglo-Hindu Law) and the few British-era statutes (like the 1937 Act).
        

### 3. Legal Consequences for East Pakistan

This period established the legal status quo that Bangladesh inherited in 1971.

- **No Divorce:** While India introduced divorce in 1955, it remained unrecognized in East Pakistan.
    
- **Polygamy:** While India criminalized polygamy, it remained legally valid (though socially rare) in East Pakistan.
    
- **Limited Property Rights:** Women in East Pakistan continued to hold only a "Limited Estate" (Widow's Estate) in inherited property, whereas Indian women gained absolute ownership.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Great Divergence":** For exams, you must clearly articulate that in **1947**, the single stream of Hindu Law split.
> 
> - **India** $\rightarrow$ Moved to **Statutory Law** (Codified).
>     
> - **East Pakistan (Bangladesh)** $\rightarrow$ Remained with **Case Law/Customary Law** (Uncodified).
>     

> **Common Mistake:** Students often accidentally apply Indian acts (like the Hindu Succession Act, 1956) to Bangladeshi problem questions because they read Indian textbooks. **Never do this.** These acts have no application in our jurisdiction.

> **Key Concept:** The "Non-interference Policy" of the Pakistan government is the primary reason why Hindu law here is still based on ancient texts rather than modern statutes.
