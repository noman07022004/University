## 1.4 Bangladesh Period (1971 – Present) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This is the practical reality of the law you are studying. You must understand strictly what applies _now_ in Bangladeshi courts versus what applies in India or historically.
>- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** Highly probable for exam questions. You will often be asked to "Discuss the present status of Hindu Law in Bangladesh" or "Critically analyze recent legislative reforms."

---

After the independence of Bangladesh in 1971, the legal system underwent a constitutional transition, but the personal laws of Hindus remained largely static. Unlike the radical reforms in India or the gradual changes in other legal sectors, Hindu Law in Bangladesh is characterized by **continuity** rather than change. It remains primarily "Judge-made law" based on the Dayabhaga school, supplemented by a few specific statutes.

---
### 1. Constitutional Continuity

The legal basis for the application of Hindu Law in Bangladesh lies in the Constitution.

- **Article 149:** Existing laws that were in force in the territory of Bangladesh before independence continue to be in force unless repealed or amended.
    
- **Effect:** This ensured that the body of "Anglo-Hindu Law" (British enactments and Privy Council judgments) and the status quo of the Pakistan period remained the law of the land.
    

### 2. Nature of the Law

Currently, the Hindu Law administered in Bangladesh is a combination of:

1. **The Dayabhaga School:** The primary authority for inheritance and joint family matters.
    
2. **Pre-1947 Legislation:** British-era acts like the _Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856_ and the _Hindu Women’s Rights to Property Act, 1937_.
    
3. **Case Law (Precedents):** Decisions of the Privy Council, the Federal Court of Pakistan, and the Supreme Court of Bangladesh.
    
4. **Custom:** Valid customs that have been recognized by courts.
    

### 3. Legislative Developments (Reforms)

While there has been no "Hindu Code" like in India, the Bangladesh government has enacted specific laws to address administrative and social needs:

- **The Hindu Marriage Registration Act, 2012:**
    
    - Historically, Hindu marriage required only religious rites (Saptapadi), and no document was maintained.
        
    - This Act introduced a system for registering marriages.
        
    - **Crucial Limitation:** Initially, it was "optional." While it encourages registration to help women prove marriage in court, it does not make an unregistered marriage void. The validity still depends on religious ceremonies.
        
- **Hindu Marriage Registration Rules, 2013:** Provided the procedural framework and forms for the 2012 Act.
    
- **Transfer of Property (Amendment) Act, 2004:** Though a general law, it impacted Hindus by restricting the power of specific religious donations without government permission (related to Vested Property debates).
    

### 4. Judicial Activism and Social Change

In the absence of parliamentary legislation, the Judiciary and civil society have been the drivers of minor changes:

- **Judicial Interpretation:** The High Court Division has occasionally emphasized that while they cannot "make" law, they can interpret it liberally to ensure justice for women and children within the existing Dayabhaga framework.
    
- **Law Commission Reports:** The Law Commission of Bangladesh has submitted reports recommending reforms (e.g., introducing divorce, standardized marriage registration), but these have largely not been enacted into statutes due to opposition from conservative sections of the community who view personal law as divine and untouchable.
    

### 5. Current Rights Status (Snapshot)

To summarize the current legal position in Bangladesh:

- **Marriage:** Polygamy is legally permitted (though socially declining). There is no statutory provision for "Divorce" (only limited separation or customary divorce in lower castes).
    
- **Inheritance:** The _Dayabhaga_ system applies. A widow gets a life interest. Daughters inherit only if they have no brothers and (historically) if they were likely to have sons, though case law has evolved on "barren daughters."
    
- **Adoption:** Parents can adopt, but usually only sons (Datta Homam ceremony).
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Optional" Trap:** In exams, if asked about marriage registration, remember: Under the 2012 Act, registration is **optional** (though highly recommended). An unregistered marriage is **valid** if rites were performed. Do not confuse this with Muslim law where registration is mandatory.

> **Law Commission:** Mentioning the "Law Commission of Bangladesh recommendations" in your conclusion of an essay adds significant academic weight. It shows you are aware of the _future_ direction and critiques of the law.

> **Key Difference Summary:**
> 
> - **India:** Codified, Divorce allowed, Equal Inheritance.
>     
> - **Bangladesh:** Uncodified, No statutory Divorce, Widow's Life Estate.
