## 1.1 Ancient Period of Hindu Law ⭐⭐⭐

>**Rating Justification:**
>- **Understanding (⭐ 4/5):** Critical for understanding _why_ Hindu law is religious in nature and how it evolved from "divine will" to "legal codes." It explains the root of the two major schools (Dayabhaga and Mitakshara).
>- **Exam (⭐ 3/5):** While less frequent as a standalone problem question, it is often part of short notes or the introductory part of broad essay questions on the "History of Hindu Law."

---

The Ancient Period of Hindu Law is the longest and most formative era, spanning over 4,000 years. It represents the transformation of Hindu Law from **abstract religious duties (Dharma)** into **systematic legal codes**. During this time, law was not made by a parliament or king; it was believed to be divine, revealed by God to sages, and then interpreted by scholars to fit growing societies.

This period is historically divided into three distinct stages:

1. **The Sruti Period (The Era of Divine Revelation)**
    
2. **The Smriti Period (The Era of The Institutes)**
    
3. **The Post-Smriti Period (The Era of Commentaries)**
    

---

### 1. The Sruti Period (Vedic Era)

- **Meaning:** "Sruti" means "what is heard." It refers to the divine words of God directly heard by the ancient Rishis (Sages).
    
- **Key Texts:** The four **Vedas** (Rig, Yajur, Sama, and Atharva).
    
- **Legal Significance:** Interestingly, the Vedas contain very little "positive law" (rules for daily life, property, or crime). They primarily focus on religious rituals, prayers, and sacrifices.
    
- **Role:** While they supplied the _concept_ of Dharma (duty), they did not provide the specific _rules_ of law. They are the ultimate root of authority, but not the practical handbook for lawyers.
    

### 2. The Smriti Period (The Golden Age)

- **Meaning:** "Smriti" means "what is remembered." As society became more complex, Sages "remembered" the divine revelations and compiled them into written codes to guide human conduct.
    
- **Evolution:** This era bridged the gap between religion and law. The texts transitioned from **Dharmasutras** (written in prose, focusing on duties of castes) to **Dharmashastras** (written in verse, focusing on legal rules).
    
- **The Three Major Smritikars (Law Givers):**
    
    - **Manu (Manusmriti):** The first and most authoritative lawgiver. His code was rigid and orthodox, heavily blending religion, morality, and law. It established the strict caste system and the superior position of Brahmins.
        
    - **Yajnavalkya:** He came after Manu. His code was more liberal, logical, and synthesized. _Crucial Note:_ The **Mitakshara School** is based entirely on the commentary of Yajnavalkya’s code.
        
    - **Narada:** He was the first to distinctly separate **Law (Vyavahara)** from **Religion (Dharma)**. His texts focused purely on procedural law, pleadings, and evidence, marking the maturation of the legal system.
        

### 3. The Post-Smriti Period (Era of Commentaries & Digests)

- **The Problem:** Over centuries, the ancient Smritis became difficult to understand, or their rules became outdated for changing society. However, because Smritis were "divine," they could not be changed or deleted.
    
- **The Solution:** Learned scholars wrote **Commentaries (Nibandhas)** on the Smritis. Under the guise of "interpreting" the texts, they actually modified the law to suit local customs and new social needs.
    
- **Birth of Schools:**
    
    - **Vijnaneshwara** wrote the _Mitakshara_ (a commentary on Yajnavalkya), which became authoritative all over India (except Bengal).
        
    - **Jimutavahana** wrote the _Dayabhaga_ (a digest of all codes), which became authoritative specifically in Bengal (Bangladesh).
        

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **Distinction for Exam:** If asked to differentiate between Sruti and Smriti:
> 
> - **Sruti** = Direct word of God (Vedas) $\rightarrow$ Theory/Source of Authority.
>     
> - **Smriti** = Recollection of Sages (Dharmashastras) $\rightarrow$ Practical Law/Written Codes.
>     

> **Narada's Contribution:** Remember Narada for the objective question: _"Who separated law from religion?"_ He focused on civil law and court procedures, unlike Manu who focused on religious duty.

> **Key Takeaway:** The Ancient period did not have a "Legislature." Law was not "made"; it was "discovered" by Sages.
