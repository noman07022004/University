### Introduction

The history of Roman Law spans over a thousand years, from the legendary founding of Rome (753 BC) to the reign of Emperor Justinian (565 AD). The "sources" of Roman Law refer to the authoritative methods by which rules of conduct became law.

Jurists (like Justinian) traditionally divided these sources into two categories:

1. **_Jus Scriptum_** **(Written Law):** Laws enacted by legislatures, edicts of magistrates, and imperial constitutions.
    
2. **_Jus Non-Scriptum_** **(Unwritten Law):** Customary law (_Mos Maiorum_) which grew from long usage and tradition.
    

While the syllabus specifically highlights **The Twelve Tables**, an academic understanding requires acknowledging that this was the _first_ major written source, emerging from a background of oral tradition.

### I. The Twelve Tables (_Lex Duodecim Tabularum_)

**Syllabus Covered:** _18.1 The Twelve Tables_

The Twelve Tables (c. 450 BC) is the earliest attempt by the Romans to create a code of law. It is regarded as the fountainhead of all public and private law in Rome.

**1. Historical Background (The Struggle of the Orders)**

- **Conflict:** In early Rome, the law was unwritten and interpreted exclusively by the **Patricians** (aristocrats/priests). The **Plebeians** (commoners) felt oppressed because they did not know the law and were often judged arbitrarily.
    
- **Demand:** The Plebeians demanded that the laws be written down and published so everyone could know their rights.
    
- **The Commission:** In 451 BC, the normal machinery of government was suspended. A commission of ten men, known as the **Decemviri**, was appointed to draft the laws. They produced ten tables initially. In 450 BC, a second commission added two more tables, bringing the total to twelve.
    

**2. Nature of the Twelve Tables**

- **Not a Modern Code:** It was not a comprehensive constitution or a systematic civil code. It did not cover everything.
    
- **Codification of Custom:** It mostly wrote down existing customary laws to make them fixed and public.
    
- **Strict Formalism:** The laws were extremely rigid and formal. If you made a slight mistake in the procedure (e.g., reciting the wrong word during a trial), you lost your case.
    

**3. Key Contents of the Tables** The original bronze tablets were destroyed during the Gallic invasion of Rome (390 BC), but their content has been reconstructed from references in later literature (like Cicero).

- **Tables I–III (Procedure):** Focused heavily on court procedure.
    
    - _Summons:_ If a plaintiff calls a defendant to court, he _must_ go. If he refuses, the plaintiff can drag him there by force.
        
    - _Execution of Judgment:_ Harsh rules for debtors. If a debt wasn't paid, the debtor could eventually be sold into slavery or even killed (cut into pieces) by creditors.
        
- **Table IV (Paternal Power):** Addressed the absolute power of the father (_Patria Potestas_). It included a rule that if a father sold his son into slavery three times, the son became free from the father's authority.
    
- **Table V (Guardianship & Inheritance):** Established that if a man died intestate (without a will) and had no direct heirs (_suus heres_), the nearest agnate (male relative) would inherit.
    
- **Table VI (Ownership & Possession):** Dealt with property transfer (_Mancipatio_).
    
- **Table VIII (Torts/Delicts):** famously contained "An eye for an eye" (Talio) for grievous injury, but allowed monetary compensation for lesser injuries.
    
- **Table XI–XII (Supplement):** These were the later additions. Table XI famously (and controversially) forbade intermarriage between Patricians and Plebeians (later repealed).
    

**4. Significance**

- **Legal Equality:** It established the principle that the law applies to all citizens equally.
    
- **Secularization:** It separated the law from religious ritual to some extent.
    
- **Basis of Education:** For centuries, Roman schoolboys memorized the Twelve Tables as the core of their education.

### II. Other Sources of Roman Law (Gap Filling)

_Although the syllabus focuses on the Twelve Tables, a university-level answer on "Sources" must briefly mention what came after, to show evolution._

1. **Legislation (_Leges_ & _Plebiscite_):** Laws passed by the popular assemblies (Comitia).
    
2. **Edicts of the Magistrates (_Jus Honorarium_):** The Praetors (judges) issued "Edicts" at the start of their term, stating how they would handle cases. This allowed Roman law to evolve and become more flexible (equity) without formally changing the rigid Twelve Tables.
    
3. **Senatus Consultum:** Advice given by the Senate, which later acquired the force of law.
    
4. **Responsa Prudentium (Jurist Opinions):** The answers given by learned lawyers (Jurists) to legal questions. In the classical period, these opinions were cited in court and heavily influenced the law.
    
5. **Imperial Constitutions:** Decrees and orders issued directly by the Emperor.
    
6. **Corpus Juris Civilis (Justinian’s Code):** In the 6th Century AD, Emperor Justinian compiled all previous Roman laws into one massive work. This is how Roman Law survived to influence the modern world.

### III. Important Notes & Exam Tips

- **"Fountain of all Public and Private Law":** Memorize this quote by **Livy** (Roman historian). Use it in your introduction or conclusion about the Twelve Tables.
    
- **Lex Aquilia:** A famous statute (passed later, c. 286 BC) that modernized the law of damage to property (Torts). It replaced the archaic rules of the Twelve Tables.
    
- **Comparison Point:** You can compare the Twelve Tables to the _Code of Hammurabi_. Both were ancient attempts to publish the law to prevent oppression, though the Twelve Tables were more procedural.
    
- **Exam Strategy:** If asked about "Sources of Roman Law," spend 60% of your time on the Twelve Tables (as per syllabus focus) and 40% listing the later sources to demonstrate breadth of knowledge.
