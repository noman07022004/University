## 10.1 Characteristics of a Will (_Ichha-patra_) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This subtopic defines the very essence of a Will. You must understand strictly why a "draft" or a "wishlist" is not a Will. The legal characteristics (like revocability and post-mortem effect) distinguish a Will from a Gift or a Settlement.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** Questions frequently ask to "Define Will and discuss its essential characteristics." It is the starting point for any answer regarding testamentary succession.
    

---

### Introduction

A **Will** (Testament) is an instrument by which a person makes a disposition of his property to take effect after his death. The concept of a Will was unknown to ancient Hindu Law because the family system was joint and property belonged to the coparcenary, not the individual. However, due to British influence and judicial recognition (notably in the _Tagore v. Tagore_ case), the right to make a Will is now fully established for Hindus in Bangladesh.

Today, Wills for Hindus are governed by the **Succession Act, 1925**.

### 1. Definition

The statutory definition is provided in **Section 2(h)** of the **Succession Act, 1925**:

> "Will means the **legal declaration** of the intention of a testator with respect to his **property** which he desires to be carried into effect **after his death**."

### 2. Essential Characteristics

Based on the definition and judicial precedents, a Will must possess certain specific characteristics to be valid. If any of these are missing, the document is not a Will.

#### **A. Legal Declaration**

- The document must be a "legal" declaration, meaning it must be in conformity with the law.
    
- For a declaration to be legal, it must be signed and attested as required by **Section 63** of the Succession Act. A mere letter to a friend saying "I want my son to have my house" is not a Will if it lacks attestation.
    

#### **B. Disposition of Property**

- The declaration must relate to the **property** of the testator.
    
- If a document only appoints a guardian or gives advice but does not dispose of any property, it is generally not a Will.
    
- _Note:_ The property must be transferable. A Hindu father under Dayabhaga law can dispose of all his property (ancestral and self-acquired).
    

#### **C. Post-Mortem Effect (Ambulatory Nature)**

- This is the most distinguishing feature. A Will speaks from the grave.
    
- **Ambulatory:** It is effective only **after the death** of the testator.
    
- During the lifetime of the testator, the Will is a mere piece of paper. It creates no rights in favor of the legatee (beneficiary). The testator remains the full owner and can sell the property mentioned in the Will while he is alive.
    
- _Contrast:_ A **Gift** takes effect immediately; a **Will** takes effect only after death.
    

#### **D. Revocability**

- A Will is inherently **revocable**.
    
- The testator can change, alter, or cancel his Will at any time during his lifetime. Even if the Will says "This Will is irrevocable," that clause is void, and he can still revoke it.
    
- The "Last Will" always prevails over earlier ones.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Ambulatory" Concept:** Use this legal term in your exam. It means the document "walks" or remains active/changeable until the testator dies. It implies that the beneficiaries have only a _Spes Successionis_ (hope of succession) during the testator's life, not a vested right.

> **Crucial Distinction:**
> 
> - **Settlement:** Transfers interest _now_ (Inter Vivos).
>     
> - **Will:** Transfers interest _later_ (Testamentary).
>     
> - _Tip:_ If a document says "I give this to you immediately, but I retain life interest," it is a **Settlement/Gift**, not a Will.
>     

> **Hindu Law Specifics:** Remember that while the _Succession Act_ provides the definition, the _capacity_ to make a Will (e.g., a father disinheriting a son) is governed by Hindu Personal Law (Dayabhaga).
