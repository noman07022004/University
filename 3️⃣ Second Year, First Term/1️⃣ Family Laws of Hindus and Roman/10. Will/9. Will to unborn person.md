## 10.9 Will to Unborn Person ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** This topic represents the intersection of ancient Hindu philosophy (which required a donee to be present to accept) and modern property law (which allows future planning). Understanding the strict conditions under which you can leave property to a future grandchild is essential for estate planning.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** This is a classic "problem question" area. Examiners love scenarios like: _"T bequeaths property to his son for life, and then to his unborn grandson for life."_ You must be able to identify that the second bequest is void because it is not absolute.
    

---

### Introduction

Can a Hindu leave property by Will to a person who is not yet born? In the ancient Shastric law, the answer was a strict "No." A gift or bequest was considered a bilateral transaction requiring a giver and a receiver. If the receiver did not exist, the transaction failed.

However, modern society requires long-term family planning. Therefore, the **Succession Act, 1925** (which governs Hindu Wills in Bangladesh) has modified the ancient rule. Today, a bequest to an unborn person is **Valid**, but it is subject to severe restrictions to prevent property from being tied up indefinitely.

---

### 1. The Ancient Rule: _Tagore v. Tagore_ (1872)

This is the most celebrated case in Hindu Law regarding Wills.

- **The Facts:** The testator, Prasanna Kumar Tagore, tried to create a complex line of succession, leaving property to his son for life, then to his son's son (born or unborn) for life, and so on.
    
- **The Privy Council Ruling:** The Council struck down the Will regarding the unborn heirs.
    
- **The Principle Established:** _"A person capable of taking under a Will must be such a person as could take a gift inter vivos, and therefore must either in fact or in contemplation of law be **in existence** at the death of the testator."_
    
- **Result:** Under pure Hindu Law, a bequest to a person not existing at the time of the testator's death is **Void**.
    

### 2. The Modern Statutory Rule (Section 113, Succession Act)

The _Succession Act, 1925_ overrides the strict _Tagore_ rule. A bequest to an unborn person is now **Valid**, provided it satisfies the conditions of **Section 113**.

#### **The Two Mandatory Conditions:**

To validly bequeath property to an unborn person (e.g., a future grandchild), the testator must follow this structure:

1. **Creation of Prior Interest (No Vacuum):**
    
    - The property cannot be given directly to an unborn person because there is no one to hold it in the meantime.
        
    - The legal ownership must first be given to a **living person** (e.g., the testator's son) for their life (Life Estate). This acts as a carrier to hold the property until the unborn person arrives.
        
2. **Transfer of Absolute Interest (Whole Remainder):**
    
    - This is the most critical condition. When the prior interest (Life Estate) ends, the property must pass to the unborn person **Absolutely**.
        
    - You **cannot** give a "Life Estate" to an unborn person. You must give them the _whole_ of the remaining interest of the testator.
        

---

### 3. Illustrations of Validity

#### **Scenario A (Valid Bequest)**

- **The Will:** _"I bequeath my house to my son S (living) for his life, and after his death, to his eldest son (unborn) **absolutely**."_
    
- **Analysis:**
    
    - Prior interest created in S? **Yes.**
        
    - Unborn person gets absolute interest? **Yes.**
        
    - **Result:** **Valid.** When S dies, the house goes to the grandson as full owner.
        

#### **Scenario B (Void Bequest)**

- **The Will:** _"I bequeath my house to my son S (living) for his life, and after his death, to his eldest son (unborn) **for his life**, and then to his brother."_
    
- **Analysis:**
    
    - Prior interest created in S? **Yes.**
        
    - Unborn person gets absolute interest? **No** (He only gets it "for life").
        
    - **Result:** The bequest to the unborn grandson is **Void**. (The property will revert to the testator's heirs upon S's death).
        

### 4. Child in the Womb (_En ventre sa mere_)

There is a legal exception to the definition of "Unborn."

- **The Rule:** A child who is **in the womb** at the time of the testator's death, and who is subsequently born alive, is treated by law as a **person in existence**.
    
- **Effect:** A bequest can be made directly to a child in the womb without creating a prior life interest, because legally, the child already exists.
    

### 5. Rule Against Perpetuity (Section 114)

This rule puts a time limit on the vesting of the property.

- **The Limit:** The vesting of the property in the unborn person cannot be delayed beyond the **lifetime of living persons** plus the **minority** (18 years) of the unborn person.
    
- If the Will says the unborn person gets the property only when he turns 25, the bequest is **Void** because it exceeds the perpetuity period (Life + 18).
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Life on Life" Ban:** A simple way to remember the rule for exams: **"You cannot pile a Life Estate on top of an Unborn Person."**
> 
> - You can give a Life Estate to a _Living_ Person.
>     
> - You must give an Absolute Estate to an _Unborn_ Person.
>     

> **Tagore v. Tagore:** Even though the statute modified it, you must cite this case. It explains _why_ the restrictions exist (to prevent property from being tied up forever).

> **Problem Solving:** If a problem says: _"Father wills property to Son for life, then to Son's unborn son for life."_
> 
> - **Verdict:** The gift to the unborn son is **Void**. The Son takes the life estate, but after his death, the property reverts to the Father's heirs as if no will existed for that part.
>
