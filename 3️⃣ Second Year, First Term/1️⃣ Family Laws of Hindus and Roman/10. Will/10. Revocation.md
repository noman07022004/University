## 10.10 Revocation of Will ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** A Will is effective only upon death. Until then, it is merely a declaration of intent. Understanding that a testator can change their mind at any moment—and knowing exactly _how_ to legally cancel a previous Will—is fundamental to testamentary law.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 5/5):** This is a "Grade A" exam topic. The specific rule that **marriage does not revoke a Hindu Will** is one of the most frequently asked objective and short-question topics, as it contradicts the general law of succession for other communities.
    

---

### Introduction

One of the essential characteristics of a Will is its **Revocability**. Because a Will is "ambulatory" (it "walks" with the testator during their life and only stops at death), the testator has the absolute right to revoke, alter, or replace it at any time while they are competent.

Even if a Will contains a clause saying, _"I hereby declare this Will to be irrevocable,"_ that clause is **Void**. The testator can still revoke it. For Hindus in Bangladesh, the rules of revocation are governed by **Section 70** of the **Succession Act, 1925**.

---

### 1. Modes of Revocation

According to Section 70 of the Succession Act, an unprivileged Will can be revoked in the following specific ways:

#### **A. By Execution of a New Will or Codicil**

The most common way to revoke a Will is to make a new one.

- **Express Revocation:** The new Will usually contains a standard clause: _"I hereby revoke all former Wills and Codicils made by me."_
    
- **Implied Revocation:** Even if there is no revocation clause, if the **contents** of the new Will are fundamentally inconsistent with the old one, the old one is revoked impliedly.
    
    - _Example:_ Will 1 (2010) says "House to Ram." Will 2 (2020) says "House to Rahim." Will 1 is automatically revoked regarding the house.
        

#### **B. By Destruction (_Animus Revocandi_)**

A Will can be revoked by burning, tearing, or otherwise destroying it. However, the physical act alone is not enough; it must be accompanied by the **intention** to revoke.

- **Two Elements Required:**
    
    1. **Act of Destruction:** The document must be burnt, torn, or destroyed. Merely crossing out the signature with a pen or writing "Cancelled" across the page is generally **insufficient** unless signed and attested again.
        
    2. **Intention (_Animus Revocandi_):** The act must be done with the clear intention of revoking the Will.
        
        - _Accidental Destruction:_ If a testator accidentally throws the Will into the fireplace thinking it was waste paper, the Will is **NOT revoked**. If the contents can be proved (e.g., via a copy), it remains valid.
            
- **By Agent:** The destruction can be done by another person (e.g., a lawyer), but it **MUST** be done:
    
    - In the **presence** of the testator, AND
        
    - By his **direction**.
        

#### **C. The Marriage Exception (Crucial for Hindus)**

- **General Law (Christians/Parsis):** Under Section 69 of the Succession Act, the marriage of a testator **automatically revokes** any Will made by him previously. The law presumes the new spouse creates new obligations.
    
- **Hindu Law Rule:** **Section 69 does NOT apply to Hindus.**
    
    - **The Rule:** **Marriage does NOT revoke a Will made by a Hindu.**
        
    - _Scenario:_ A Hindu man executes a Will leaving all his property to his brother. Two years later, he marries. He dies without writing a new Will.
        
    - _Consequence:_ The **Brother inherits everything**. The widow gets nothing (except her right to maintenance). The Will stands valid despite the marriage.
        

---

### 2. Doctrine of Dependent Relative Revocation

This is a nuanced legal principle used by courts to save a Will from accidental revocation.

- **The Concept:** If a testator destroys an old Will _only because_ he believes he has successfully created a new valid Will, but the new Will turns out to be invalid (e.g., not attested properly), the court applies this doctrine.
    
- **The Result:** The court presumes his intention to revoke the old Will was **conditional** (dependent) on the new one being valid. Since the condition failed, the revocation is cancelled, and the **Old Will is revived**.
    

---

### 3. Loss of Will (Presumption)

What happens if a Will is known to be in the testator's possession but cannot be found after his death?

- **Presumption:** The Court generally presumes that the testator **destroyed it with the intention to revoke**.
    
- **Rebuttal:** The burden of proof lies on the person propounding the Will to prove that it was lost, stolen, or destroyed without the testator's consent (e.g., by a fire or a disinherited relative).
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Marriage" Trick:** This is the most critical exam point for Section 10.
> 
> - _Question:_ "Mr. X (a Hindu) makes a Will. He marries Mrs. Y. Is the Will revoked?"
>     
> - _Answer:_ **NO.**
>     
> - _Reasoning:_ Section 57 of the Succession Act exempts Hindus from the operation of Section 69 (Revocation by Marriage).
>     

> **Destruction Presence:** If a problem says: _"Testator calls his lawyer on the phone and tells him to burn the Will in his office."_
> 
> - _Verdict:_ **Not Revoked.**
>     
> - _Reason:_ The destruction must happen **in the presence** of the testator.
>     

> **Effect of Conversion:** If a Hindu converts to Islam, his Hindu Will remains valid, but the _law of succession_ changes. However, generally, the mere act of conversion does not revoke the Will itself (unlike marriage for Christians).
