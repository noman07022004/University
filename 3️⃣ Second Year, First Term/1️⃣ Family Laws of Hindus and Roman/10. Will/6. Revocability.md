## 10.6 Revocability of Will ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Rating Justification:**

- **Understanding (⭐ 5/5):** The "Ambulatory" nature of a Will means it walks with the testator until death. Understanding that a Will is never final until the heart stops beating is crucial. It distinguishes a Will from a Deed.
    
- **Exam (⭐ 4/5):** A favorite topic for objective and short questions. The specific rule that "Marriage does NOT revoke a Hindu Will" is a classic trick question that catches many students off guard.
    

---

### Introduction

One of the essential characteristics of a Will is its **Revocability**. A Will is tentative; it expresses the testator's wish at a specific moment in time. Since human relationships and assets change, the law gives the testator the absolute right to alter or cancel (revoke) his Will at any time during his lifetime.

Even if a Will explicitly states, _"This Will is irrevocable and I shall never change it,"_ that clause is **Void**. The testator can tear it up the next day and write a new one. The last valid Will executed before death is the only one that takes effect.

For Hindus in Bangladesh, the rules of revocation are governed by **Section 70** of the **Succession Act, 1925** (as applied by Section 57), with a major exception regarding marriage.

---

### 1. Modes of Revocation

A Will can be revoked in two primary ways: by the **act of the party** (voluntary) or by **operation of law** (involuntary).

#### **A. Revocation by Execution of a New Will**

- **Express Revocation:** The testator writes a new Will which contains a clause: _"I hereby revoke all former Wills and codicils made by me."_
    
- **Implied Revocation:** The testator executes a new Will that creates a disposition of property **inconsistent** with the previous Will.
    
    - _Example:_ Will 1 says "House to Son." Will 2 says "House to Daughter." Will 2 automatically revokes Will 1 regarding the house, even if it doesn't say "I revoke Will 1."
        

#### **B. Revocation by Destruction (_Animus Revocandi_)**

- **The Rule (Section 70):** A Will can be revoked by **burning, tearing, or otherwise destroying** the same by the testator (or by some person in his presence and by his direction) with the **intention** of revoking it.
    
- **Two Elements Required:**
    
    1. **Act of Destruction:** There must be actual physical destruction (burning, tearing into pieces). Crossing out words with a pen or writing "Cancelled" on the back is usually **not** enough under strict law unless signed and attested again.
        
    2. **Intention (_Animus Revocandi_):** The act must be done with the specific intent to revoke.
        
        - _Accident:_ If a testator accidentally throws the Will in the fire thinking it was a newspaper, the Will is **NOT revoked**. If a copy survives (or contents can be proved), it remains valid.
            

#### **C. Revocation by Marriage (The Hindu Exception)**

- **General Rule (Christians/Parsis):** Under Section 69 of the Succession Act, the marriage of a testator **automatically revokes** any Will made by him prior to marriage.
    
- **Hindu Law Exception:** This section **does NOT apply to Hindus**.
    
    - **The Rule:** **Marriage does NOT revoke a Will made by a Hindu.**
        
    - _Scenario:_ A Hindu man makes a Will giving all his property to his brother. He later marries and has children. He dies without writing a new Will.
        
    - _Result:_ The **Brother gets the property**. The widow and children get nothing (except their right to maintenance). The old Will stands.
        

---

### 2. Doctrine of "Dependent Relative Revocation"

This is a nuanced legal doctrine relevant to revocation.

- **Concept:** If a testator destroys his old Will _only because_ he thinks he has successfully made a new valid Will, but the new Will turns out to be invalid (e.g., not attested properly), the court may apply this doctrine.
    
- **Result:** The court presumes he only wanted to revoke the old one _conditional_ on the new one working. Since the new one failed, the **Old Will is revived** (if its contents can be reconstructed).
    

---

### 3. Loss of Will (Presumption)

- **Scenario:** If a Will was known to be in the testator's possession but cannot be found after his death.
    
- **Presumption:** The court generally presumes that the testator **destroyed it with the intention to revoke**. The burden of proof lies on the beneficiary to prove it was lost or stolen, not destroyed.
    

---

### Important Notes & Exam Tips

> **The "Marriage" Trap:** I cannot emphasize this enough.
> 
> - **Question:** _"X, a Hindu, makes a will. He marries Y. Does the will stand?"_
>     
> - **Answer:** **YES.** Marriage does not revoke it.
>     
> - **Question:** _"X, a Christian, makes a will. He marries Y. Does the will stand?"_
>     
> - **Answer:** **NO.** Marriage revokes it automatically.
>     

> **Destruction by Agent:** A testator can ask someone else to burn his Will, but it must be done **in his presence**.
> 
> - _Scenario:_ Testator calls his lawyer and says "Burn my will in your office." Lawyer burns it.
>     
> - _Result:_ **Will is NOT revoked.** The destruction was not in the testator's presence.
>     

> **"Unfinished" Will:** If a testator starts tearing the Will but is stopped halfway by a friend and says "Okay, I'll keep it," the Will is **not revoked**. The act of destruction was incomplete.
